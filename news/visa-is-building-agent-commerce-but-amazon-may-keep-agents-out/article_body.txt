Visa has expanded its Agentic Ready program, adding more than 140 payments and commerce companies, including Stripe, PayPal, Adyen, Fiserv, Checkout.com and Global Payments. Together with more than 175 card issuers across 45 markets, they are preparing infrastructure for purchases that AI agents can make on people’s behalf. But Amazon, which has previously blocked third-party shopping agents, is not among the participants. The program is meant to help identify these agents and process their payments, but it does not, by itself, require merchants to accept them.

## Visa has brought more than 140 companies into agentic commerce, but not Amazon

## How Visa’s program works

Agentic Ready aims to let autonomous AI agents buy goods, pay for services and complete transactions for users without constant human involvement. Visa vice president Rubail Birwadker said that developing agentic commerce requires more than new technology: merchants need to know who they are transacting with, while payments companies need to ensure transactions run reliably and securely over existing infrastructure. He said no company can solve this alone; the program brings market participants together to test real transactions and prepare businesses for a new form of commerce.

Visa previously worked mainly with banks and other organizations that issue cards to individuals and businesses. Now the program is adding participants that help merchants accept payments: payment processors, acquiring banks, commerce platforms and business payments providers.

The expansion matters because a card or payment API alone is not enough for an agent. A merchant must recognize it as an eligible buyer, check its authority and the transaction’s risk, and then accept the payment. Otherwise, an agent may be authorized to spend a user’s money but still find no stores willing to work with it.

Visa said program participants are helping build different parts of this system:

- **Adyen** is working on identifying agent-initiated payments and passing signals from them to fraud prevention systems.
- **PayPal** is exploring how merchants can accept Visa agent tokens through existing integrations.
- **Stripe** is testing agent recognition, merchant control tools and ways to improve authorization.
- **Checkout.com** is working on authentication and processing purchases made by AI agents.

Merchants will need to distinguish legitimate agent activity from that of a malicious bot trying to steal data or use someone else’s accounts. They will also need to verify that an agent really has the customer’s permission, rather than, for example, ordering Whoopie Cushions every day because of an error or incorrect instruction. Familiar mechanisms for fraud detection, authorization and payment processing, dispute resolution and refunds must also work for agent transactions.

## Amazon does not want to let in outside agents

The challenge may not be limited to technology. Amazon does not want external AI agents buying products on its platform without the company’s permission. A dispute with Perplexity centered on its Comet browser, which lets users delegate purchases to AI agents. Amazon said Perplexity’s agents accessed its systems without authorization, creating security and privacy risks. Perplexity argued that customers should be able to choose the tools they use to access their accounts and make purchases.

In March, a federal district judge issued a preliminary injunction against Amazon. In August, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals overturned it, finding that Amazon was unlikely to prevail on its computer access claims under the legal theory it had presented.

Amazon’s position is especially notable given its own products. Its Buy For Me program lets customers order products from third-party brands through Amazon’s interface. The author previously described this approach as “agents for me, but not for you.” In September, Amazon also blocked Meta Muse, a shopping agent, citing unauthorized access.

Amazon, then, allows autonomous purchases as long as it retains control over the customer experience. An agent that compares prices across sellers and picks the best deal helps the buyer, but could weaken a retailer’s influence over prices and customer relationships. This matters particularly for companies that make money from advertising, product placement, recommendations and keeping shoppers on their own platforms.

Visa’s payment network can verify an agent’s identity, but it cannot force Amazon to accept it. Other market participants are taking a different approach: Shopify is preparing infrastructure to make merchants’ products available through AI shopping channels, while payments companies are making agent transactions easier to process. For smaller retailers, this could be a way to reach shoppers who might otherwise never find their products, if those merchants can offer the right quality, product and price.

## Why business payments may come first

Agent payments may find practical applications in business sooner than in consumer shopping. In October, Visa, BILL and Billtrust launched a pilot in which AI agents can initiate and complete approved invoice payments through supplier portals.

These transactions are less eye-catching than asking an agent to plan and pay for a vacation, but they are better suited to automation. Companies already have lists of approved suppliers, spending rules, purchase orders, invoices and approval procedures.

In a September interview, Visa Group president Oliver Jenkyn said B2B commerce could be a natural early market for agent payments: many processes there are repetitive, governed by rules and based on structured data. Billtrust, HighRadius and Versapay have also joined Visa’s new program as business commerce participants. AI agents could handle routine financial tasks while companies retain approval limits, supplier controls and transaction records.

Consumer trust is another barrier. In a Visa survey of 2,065 U.S. residents, only 23% said they would trust AI agents to manage their finances. That share rose to 61% when Visa was involved in the transaction.

## Payment standards and open questions

Visa is not the only company developing agentic commerce. Mastercard is expanding its Agent Pay platform with identification, authorization and transaction analysis features. Google is developing the Agent Payments Protocol, while OpenAI and Stripe have created the Agentic Commerce Protocol, which connects AI-powered shopping flows with merchants. Some initiatives are compatible with one another; Stripe’s participation in Visa’s program shows that standards and partnerships overlap.

It is still unclear who is responsible for a purchase if an agent misunderstands instructions, picks the wrong item or spends more than the user expected. Nor is it settled how a merchant should distinguish a legitimate purchase from fraud, or what evidence should prove that the customer gave consent.

Agentic commerce is still at an early stage: payment infrastructure is developing, and the rules governing interactions between agents and merchants are still taking shape. In the future, millions of agents could independently search for products and services, compare offers, negotiate, buy and pay — within limits set by people. The author considers this transition likely: just as people stopped buying everything from rural general stores, they may eventually stop choosing every purchase themselves. The open question is who else, beyond consumers, will benefit: financial institutions or retailers.

## Dataist’s take

Visa is creating the conditions for agent payments, but participation by payments companies does not remove the main constraint: a merchant has to agree to serve an agent. Disputes around Amazon show that access to shopping platforms will be governed not only by payment standards, but also by retailers’ own decisions.
