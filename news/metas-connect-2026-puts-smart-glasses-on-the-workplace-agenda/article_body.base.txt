## A calmer pitch from Meta

Meta has spent recent years looking defensive. At Connect in 2024, Mark Zuckerberg wore a T-shirt reading “AUT ZUCK AUT NIHIL,” roughly “Zuckerberg or nothing.” Last year’s event was remembered for glitches and failed demos.

The tone was different at Connect 2026, whose livestream began at 7 p.m. Eastern on September 23. Zuckerberg wore a shirt that said “Building is my Love Language.” The company also had a recent success to point to: its Muse AI agent, released a few weeks earlier, had reached the top of the App Store.

Meta remains a leader in an increasingly competitive smart-glasses market. It introduced a new model intended to address some earlier privacy concerns, and announced a headset positioned to compete with Apple Vision Pro despite claims that virtual reality has lost relevance.

## The workplace use case

For businesses, the harder question is how to prepare for devices that employees may adopt on their own. Companies could treat glasses as niche technology—or try to ban them. That would resemble British schools’ unsuccessful attempts to restrict camera phones in 2004.

Meta’s new camera-free model may appeal to people wary of recording, while the company also emphasized privacy features. That could make adoption easier for organizations concerned about how data is handled and shared. But the more practical first step may be building applications for glasses employees already choose to use.

Potential uses include:

- Guiding junior staff through technical maintenance.
- Showing warehouse workers instructions without making them switch their attention between a tablet and their work.
- Letting workers report misplaced or depleted stock by speaking, rather than typing on a device.

Office workers could also use an AI agent that understands what is happening around them. At Connect, Zuckerberg asked his agent to schedule a meeting. Muse already works with Granola and may support other note-taking apps. In one possible workflow, two colleagues mention a follow-up conversation in the hallway, and Muse and Granola create a meeting and send invitations.

## The camera as a data tool

Meta appears to be shifting the camera’s pitch away from photographing people and toward gathering useful information. Asking Muse to find an item in another color or locate a better price within ten miles may draw less objection than taking a photo of someone and posting it online.

For retail workers, glasses could help check whether an item is available at another store and when it will be delivered again. Information gathered this way could also update records of stock and product availability, rather than remaining in conversations.

I think the business case is clearest when glasses reduce friction in a specific task, not when they promise a new way to interact with AI. But the announcement says little about the rules that would govern what gets recorded, shared, or retained at work. Privacy features can help with adoption; they do not answer those operational questions.

## More competitors, less time to wait

Interest in smart glasses began to grow after several models appeared at CES earlier this year. The pace has picked up this fall: Snap released Specs last week, while Google and XReal are expected to present their glasses this autumn. On September 22, Vonder was announced, an AI glasses product that can record conversations and build a personal map of memories for its owner.

That growing lineup makes it harder for companies to dismiss glasses as a passing niche. The advantage may go to organizations that test useful applications early—but without clear answers on workplace data, early adoption could also mean taking on risks before the rules are settled.
