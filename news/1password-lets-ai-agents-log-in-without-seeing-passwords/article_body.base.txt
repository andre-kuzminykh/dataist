## Access without handing over secrets

The flow links the agent, the user and 1Password. When an agent tries to sign in, it sends 1Password a unique digital key to confirm its identity. The user gets a notification and can approve the attempt. 1Password then enters the credentials, allowing the agent to carry out the task.

Users can choose Claude Duet from Anthropic, Dots, ChatGPT Work from OpenAI, Grok Bot, Muse from Meta, or agents from startup Town, which is developing an AI assistant. 1Password says it is working with other organizations to expand the service.

The company’s blog warns that giving an agent a password or another secret is a common way to grant access, but a copy could remain outside the user’s control. 1Password says its service lets people delegate tasks while keeping control of passwords and other data the agent needs.

## Banks are already seeing agents

On the day the service launched, Axios reported that US banks had started detecting agents operating in customers’ accounts. That does not necessarily mean agents are moving money without permission or taking over accounts. It reflects people increasingly allowing automated services to act for them, including on financial matters.

The problem is that most banks cannot tell whether an agent’s action was requested by the customer. Bankers who spoke with Axios called the situation frightening. A Chase representative also told the outlet that customers should not share their usernames and passwords with anyone.

<strong>I think 1Password is addressing the right weakness: an agent may need authority to act, but it should not need a copy of the secret that grants that authority.</strong> The announcement leaves the harder question open: how does a user know what an approved agent will do after it gets access? Without that clarity, keeping the password hidden may limit exposure without making delegation itself safe.
