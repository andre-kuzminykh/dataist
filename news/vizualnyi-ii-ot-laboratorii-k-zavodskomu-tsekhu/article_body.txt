# Former Meta Researchers Create AI for Factories

Perceptron, a company founded by two former Meta researchers, has unveiled the Isaac 0.5 computer vision model. Founded in November 2024, the company is developing advanced vision models that help machines perceive their surroundings, reason, and act in industrial settings. Isaac 0.5 is designed for robots operating in warehouses and factories: it helps them navigate complex spaces, while enabling companies to extract visual intelligence from footage captured by robot cameras. The model is being released openly, allowing anyone to study its parameters and training materials.

## A Model for Physical Tasks

Perceptron was founded by Armen Aghajanyan and Akshat Shrivastava, who previously worked at Fundamental AI Research (FAIR), Meta’s research division. They see their product as a foundation for automating industrial operations.

According to the company, current systems for physical tasks force users to choose between general-purpose foundation models that require several dedicated cloud GPUs for each instance, and narrow models that can either perceive their surroundings or control actions, but not perform both functions.

Aghajanyan and Shrivastava believe Isaac 0.5 differs from existing solutions through its general-purpose design. The model is not intended for a single repetitive task and should be able to adapt to a specific environment or situation.

In an interview, Shrivastava suggested imagining a routine task: sorting boxes. A robot first needs to read a label, determine where the boxes are located in space, and choose which one to pick up. If there are several boxes, the system must plan the order in which to handle them.

Perceptron’s software helps the robot complete all these stages. Individual solutions for most such operations already exist, but flexible programs capable of performing them under different conditions remain relatively uncommon.

## Training on Video

Isaac 0.5 acquires practical skills by processing vast amounts of video data. According to Perceptron, the model was trained on <strong>one million hours of ordinary video</strong>, which helped it recognize different locations, objects, and scenarios.

The company also made extensive use of first-person video: footage recorded from the perspective of a person performing a physical task, typically with a GoPro or wearable camera. Another source was UMI video, in which people’s repeated actions are recorded to train AI in movement.

Perceptron has not disclosed exactly where it obtained its training materials. Shrivastava said, however, that the company independently assembled petabyte-scale datasets. These include images, text, video, and robot motion trajectories.

## Applications and Investment

Software capable of helping robots work in warehouses could be used across a range of industries. Perceptron plans to sell its technology to various providers, allowing them to integrate its visual intelligence layer into a broad range of sectors.

The company lists manufacturing, logistics and warehousing, security, mobility, media, and entertainment among the potential areas of application.

In 2024, Perceptron raised <strong>$16 million</strong> from Bessemer Venture Partners, The Explorer Fund, and SmartGateVC, according to Pitchbook. TechCrunch reports that the company is now completing an additional funding round. The publication had previously reported details about recent financing incorrectly.
