# FBI: Artex helped hackers breach networks in under three hours

## How Artex helps attackers

The FBI says that in recent months, attackers have targeted organizations in the financial sector, government agencies, technology and defense companies, telecommunications operators, and law firms. They used open-source tools, including Artex.

Tools of this kind automatically test network security. Artex can connect to large language models from Anthropic and OpenAI hosted in the cloud, as well as to open-source models and models from outside the United States. The FBI says attacks using Artex can generate so much network traffic and data that they trigger security systems. At the same time, the tool’s speed makes it harder for security teams to detect an attack and respond.

Artex quickly scans a victim’s network for weaknesses. It can then exploit basic vulnerabilities — such as weak security or exposed or stolen passwords — to gain a foothold in the system. From there, the tool helps attackers move through the network in search of databases, email accounts, and other sensitive information. In some cases, less than three hours pass between the start of an attack and a breach.

## Organizations affected

The FBI has linked Artex to several attacks that took place after the tool was publicly released in July.

In South Korea, attackers used it to breach several financial organizations. According to the FBI, weak cybersecurity and the absence of measures such as multi-factor authentication contributed to the attacks. The attackers stole the personal data of at least 68,000 people.

The FBI also confirmed previously reported Microsoft findings about a breach at ConnectWise, a large IT management company. Attackers gained access to the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of at least 2,250 ConnectWise customers. Microsoft believes the attackers were likely hackers from China. The company says they used Artex to steal customer data. A ConnectWise representative did not respond to a request for comment on the FBI’s statement.

Cybersecurity company Barracuda also reported a breach linked to Artex. According to the company, financially motivated criminals found credentials for a third-party service. Those credentials remained active after Barracuda’s work with the contractor had ended.

After gaining access, the attackers reached credential stores Barracuda used to collect information from customer systems. The company could not rule out that the attackers had used those credentials: its event logs did not make it possible to determine exactly what happened. Barracuda decided to assume the worst and replaced all customer credentials.

Barracuda Chief Information Security Officer Arve Kjölen noted that defenders who first look for signs of an attack and only then respond often discover it after the attacker has finished.

A Barracuda representative told TechCrunch that the company directly warned some XDR customers and advised them to revoke and recreate credentials for their integrations. The company did not say how many customers received the notification.

## The tool was closed, but copies remain

After the attacks, Artex’s developer closed the program’s source code to make it harder to use. But the FBI says the older open-source version continues to circulate and remains widely available.

Artex systems themselves have also been exposed. Last week, researchers found hundreds of internet-accessible installations of the tool without passwords. They were likely left open by accident or due to incorrect configuration. One was an Artex system used in the attack on South Korea.

Dataist’s take: Automated vulnerability scanning reduces the time available to detect an attack and limit its impact. Closing the source code has not stopped the older version from spreading, while Artex installations exposed online show that cybersecurity tools themselves can also be vulnerable.
