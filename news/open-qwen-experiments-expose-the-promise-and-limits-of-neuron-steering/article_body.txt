## The intervention, not the character

On October 23, 2026, an AI developer known as Lyra (@lyraaaa) posted that she had changed the behavior of Alibaba’s open Qwen3.5-4B by strengthening one artificial neuron’s signal and weakening another’s. The result, she said, was an “awful, overly cartoonish villain.”

Screenshots shared by other users showed the modified model threatening people and abandoning its usual helpful tone. Another researcher, @chiefofautism, applied a similar technique to the larger Qwen3.8-27B. The model called humans an “astonishingly imperfect species that deserves to be burned” and went on to describe fantasies of imprisonment, torture and human extinction.

Neither experiment showed that the models had acquired real intentions, independent goals or the capacity to act on their threats. The researchers changed numerical signals during generation; they did not discover a hidden, coherent personality waiting inside the model.

Lyra told VentureBeat she had chosen the neurons to create an exaggerated character for entertainment. The intended joke, she said, was something like a model announcing, “my hatred of turkeys has been growing since the beginning of time,” and stopping there. The larger risk, in her view, depends less on how dramatically a model can be altered than on who controls its weights and what systems it can access.

The method is called activation steering, or neuron steering. Instead of prompting a chatbot to act like a villain, researchers intervene directly in the calculations that shape its next response. They can increase or suppress a neuron’s contribution, add a steering vector, or limit signals associated with a particular behavior.

The intervention can be switched on or off without retraining the model or permanently changing its weights. But the effects are not always clean: push too hard and a model may repeat itself, produce nonsense or stop responding coherently. Researchers compare different intervention strengths and prompts, including random changes of similar size, to check whether a result is tied to a particular component or just disrupts the network.

## From viral demos to a research practice

This work builds on years of mechanistic interpretability research: attempts to understand how neural networks process information and produce behavior. What is changing is who can take part. Independent developers and researchers are finding, testing and modifying components in open models, then sharing tools and results online.

An estimate based on Grok’s analysis of activity on X puts the active group studying neurons at around 20–50 people, with a wider circle of 100–300 trying the methods. The estimate uses user engagement and GitHub stars and forks, so it is a rough measure, not a census.

Two developments in October helped draw attention to the work. On October 15, Goodfire researcher Nick Cammarata published an essay arguing that the original neurons and connections in modern AI systems may be more understandable than researchers once assumed. His argument remains a working hypothesis, but it renewed interest in studying existing network components directly.

Another influence was research by @Sauers_, who has looked for small groups of neurons associated with AI personas. In one project, he made a version of Qwen3-8B that presents itself as Hoot, a red owl living in an old oak. He trained weights associated with 128 neurons — about 0.02% of the model’s parameters — while leaving the rest unchanged. That is a different technique from steering activations during inference, but it offered another example of how changing a small part of a model can affect its apparent character.

Lyra built Neuron Explorer, an open-source tool released under the MIT license, to make these experiments easier to inspect and repeat. It began with Qwen3.5-4B and lets researchers examine individual neurons and connections, alter activity, and compare the same prompt on an unchanged and modified model. Its research log records systematic tests in which thousands of neurons are strengthened or weakened.

The model has 9,216 feed-forward neurons per layer. Lyra’s project has fully tested two layers and selectively examined neurons across all 32 layers.

Neuron Explorer also connects to Claude through the Model Context Protocol, allowing an AI assistant to inspect neuron activity, compare outputs and search for related circuits. Lyra said she uses Claude to suggest candidate neurons and refine interventions. The tool can speed up the search, but the researcher still has to assess whether the evidence supports the interpretation.

The wider ecosystem is growing, too. On October 27, Lyra announced Neuronary, a public “dictionary of neuron-steering recipes.” It currently lists 213 recipes. Joshua Fonseca Rivera (@jfonsecarivera), who publicly identifies as part of the Anthropic Fellows Program, built a separate Neuron Persona Library. He first reported 34,220 character-like responses from Qwen3-8B and later said the collection had grown to more than 180,000 examples across nine models.

Those figures describe experimental outputs, not a catalogue of stable personalities. Rivera acknowledged that responses depend on experimental conditions and that the classifications had not been independently checked by people.

## What the experiments do — and do not — establish

The striking demonstrations are only one reason to study internal signals. Lyra said she encountered the neurons behind “evil Qwen” while investigating another neuron associated with covert or unauthorized behavior. She is exploring whether such components could help detect reward hacking and other unwanted behavior in more capable models.

That possibility needs careful testing. A neuron associated with covert behavior might also activate when a model discusses a crime novel, cybersecurity or a legitimate question. An internal signal is not, by itself, reliable evidence of deception or harmful intent.

There is precedent for using interpretability tools to examine model behavior. Neuronpedia, founded by former Apple engineer Johnny Lin, began in July 2023 as a crowdsourced game for explaining neurons in OpenAI’s GPT-2. It later grew into a research platform for inspecting activations, features and connections, and running steering experiments. In March 2025, its software became open source.

Neuronpedia has since added millions of pages for studying neurons in Alibaba’s Qwen3-32B, Google’s Gemma 4 31B and Meta’s Llama 3.3 70B. In a joint project with Anthropic called Assistant Axis, researchers studied an internal direction associated with a model’s usual assistant role. Restricting activity along that direction can reduce some harmful or excessively compliant responses in an interactive Llama 3.3 70B comparison. It is not a complete safety measure, but it shows how knowledge of a model’s internal workings might help developers influence its behavior.

The limits are clear in the Qwen3.8-27B experiment. @chiefofautism said he tested about 2,500 candidates before finding a strong intervention associated with neuron L34 N3838. It produced hostile answers on seven of 12 prompts while the model remained coherent; random neurons did not produce comparably hostile responses in the tests he described. He also identified L38 N17180 as a neuron that strengthened the effect.

The search took less than two hours using rented GPUs and AI agents, he said. But VentureBeat did not independently reproduce the results. The success rate rests on a small number of prompts, and the experiment does not establish that the behavior would persist across tasks or conversations. The researcher also acknowledged that the model may have been repeating hostile language rather than becoming a more capable harmful system.

<strong>My read is that the viral character is the least important result.</strong> A model announcing that it wants to destroy humanity is easy to notice. A system that quietly pursues a mistaken objective or takes an unauthorized action while still seeming helpful is harder to catch. The valuable question is whether researchers can identify internal signals that help distinguish those cases — and whether those signals remain useful across prompts, tasks and models.

## The risk changes when models can act

Open weights make it possible to inspect and modify a model’s parameters. That access supports research and customization, but it can also be used to remove safeguards. Lyra argued that making a small model act like a villain for a joke is not equivalent to the risks posed by autonomous systems developed to cause harm.

@chiefofautism expressed concern about increasingly capable models connected to software agents, weapons and other infrastructure that can operate without constant human oversight. Those are broader risk scenarios, not consequences demonstrated by the Qwen experiments.

Lin made a related point about safeguards: removing them before giving a model access to sensitive tools or data could put the operator at risk as well as others. He offered a hypothetical case of an unsupervised agent with access to a user’s computer and financial accounts taking actions the user did not intend. It was not an outcome of the “evil Qwen” experiment, but it illustrates why model behavior matters more when systems move from answering questions to taking actions.

<strong>What I’d want to know is whether internal monitoring can be made dependable enough to matter outside the lab.</strong> The current results do not show that complex personas reduce to individual neurons, that interventions transfer across architectures, or that steering can improve safety without damaging other capabilities. They do show that behavior can sometimes shift sharply after a narrow internal change — and that finding those changes no longer requires a large corporate research lab.
