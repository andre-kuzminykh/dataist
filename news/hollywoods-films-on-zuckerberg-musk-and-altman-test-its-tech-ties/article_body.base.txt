## Hollywood’s uneasy partnership with tech

Amazon owns MGM, the historic studio. Apple TV has helped directors make personal projects, and even A24 is building financial ties to AI. Hollywood has plenty of reasons to pursue the technology industry’s money and influence. But that closeness sharpens a familiar conflict between art and commerce: technology can make compelling subject matter even as its companies become powerful partners.

The three films take different routes into that conflict:

- In Aaron Sorkin’s *The Social Reckoning*, Jeremy Strong plays Zuckerberg. The film is a loose sequel to *The Social Network*: Facebook employee Frances Haugen, played by Mikey Madison, exposes a company whose algorithm radicalized the alt-right and harmed teenagers’ mental health.
- Alex Gibney’s four-hour documentary *Musk* follows the head of SpaceX. Gibney previously made films about Enron’s collapse and Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, who was found guilty of fraud.
- Luca Guadagnino’s *Artificial* casts Andrew Garfield as OpenAI co-founder Sam Altman. Garfield previously played Zuckerberg’s former close friend in *The Social Network*. A year after ChatGPT’s release, OpenAI’s board fired Altman and then returned him to the job.

## The limits of a public takedown

The films have attracted attention beyond their directors’ reputations. Zuckerberg, Musk and Altman are among the industry’s best-known figures, and all three recently moved closer to President Trump to protect their companies’ interests. Together, the films suggest a common front against tech’s self-confident, unpredictable leaders. But a film can make that impression without materially changing what the companies can do.

The complications around *Artificial* may add to its credibility: Amazon declined to distribute it after announcing a $50 billion, or £38 billion, “strategic partnership” with OpenAI. Sony hired an independent law firm to check every fact in *The Social Reckoning*, while TikTok and X blocked ads for *Musk*. These setbacks have become useful parts of the films’ promotion. They make Silicon Valley look as if it has something to hide, but may do more for Oscar campaigns than for accountability.

Recent polls suggest young Americans are already turning away from figures such as Zuckerberg, Musk and Altman. <strong>My read is that these films are riding a wave of skepticism, not creating it.</strong> Their commercial prospects also sit awkwardly alongside a broader shift: corporations and their products are increasingly devaluing cinema as an art form.

## Who gets to reuse the films

A feature film is easy to break into short clips for Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. Those platforms can benefit from the endless scrolling that follows. A viewer may never see an ad for *Musk* on X or TikTok, yet after its release someone could cut Gibney’s documentary into clips to drive engagement. Sorkin’s scenes could circulate alongside thousands of excerpts from *The West Wing* and *The Newsroom*.

The larger tension is who benefits when Hollywood’s work becomes material for AI systems. Meta, OpenAI and Musk’s xAI have already collected scripts and books without permission to train their models. In 2025, Disney and Universal sued Midjourney, accusing it of stealing copyrighted works. Midjourney denies responsibility and argues its platform’s outputs qualify as transformative works under fair use.

Disney, meanwhile, invested $1 billion in OpenAI. That makes it difficult to cast the dispute as a simple defense of creative independence: the studios challenging AI companies may also want a share of the business.

<strong>I think the films’ release says more about studio opportunism than a settled political position.</strong> Hollywood is trying to sell stories about tech power while cultivating the companies that wield it. That bargain could make films like these harder to produce. But William Randolph Hearst’s attempt to sabotage *Citizen Kane* after recognizing its basis in his life is a reminder that powerful people still react sharply to stories they cannot control. Musk, judging by his posts, could too.
