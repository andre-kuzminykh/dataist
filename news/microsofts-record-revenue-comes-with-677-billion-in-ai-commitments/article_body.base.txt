## The cloud number is not an AI number

Microsoft reported $33.2 billion in Azure revenue, its largest cloud line. But that figure also includes traditional software and SaaS applications that companies run on the platform. Microsoft 365 brought in almost $28 billion, another reminder that the cloud business is broader than AI.

The consumer side moved in the other direction. Devices and Consumer revenue fell 3% year over year to $15.5 billion, while revenue from Windows OEM and devices dropped 22%.

## Revenue now comes with obligations

Microsoft said it received $10 billion in revenue from OpenAI. OpenAI is expanding its work with AWS, Oracle and other companies, but a significant share of its services still runs on Microsoft’s cloud. Anthropic also uses Azure.

At the same time, commercial commitments under existing contracts with AI labs rose 73% to $677 billion. Microsoft’s quarterly capital spending reached a record $45.2 billion as it and other large cloud providers increase investment to meet those commitments.

I think the record revenue matters less than the gap between what the company is promising and what its reported cloud numbers actually measure. Azure is not an AI-only business, and the $677 billion in commitments is not revenue already earned. The announcement leaves out how much of that future spending will support AI workloads, and how much will serve the broader cloud business.

That distinction is becoming harder to ignore: Microsoft’s AI growth depends both on demand from labs and on infrastructure investment that has to be paid for before the commitments turn into revenue.
