German company Q.ANT is developing photonic processors that perform computations using light waves and are intended to reduce the energy needed to run AI models. The company is not proposing to replace GPUs: photonic chips are designed for complex nonlinear computations, while GPUs are effective at linear mathematics. Q.ANT has already installed its processors in data centers operated by cloud provider IONOS as part of a pilot program. According to the company’s founder and CEO, Michael Förtsch, simulations show a four- to fivefold reduction in energy consumption, but this result has not yet been confirmed on real hardware.

## Q.ANT’s photonic processors are designed to complement GPUs for AI workloads

## How light performs computations

Rising energy consumption by AI models increases the cost of running them. Cloud providers and AI companies are trying to build data centers where renewable energy is readily available, such as in Scandinavia. But those plans face logistical challenges, shortages of key components, and protests against data centers. Q.ANT and other startups are looking for a solution in the computing chips themselves.

Conventional GPUs transmit and process data using electrons. Photonic processors use light waves. According to Förtsch, light does not generate heat as it travels and can process more data at the same time. These chips could therefore use less energy and require almost no cooling—one of the major data center expenses.

Q.ANT sees photonic processors as a complement to GPUs. Graphics cards are suited to large numbers of addition and multiplication operations—that is, linear mathematics. Photonic chips are designed for complex nonlinear computations. Förtsch gives the example of a sine wave: describing a circle with linear functions would require infinitely many points and lines, while a wave function can represent it with two points.

Among the tasks Förtsch believes photonic processors could be useful for are working with images and video: recognition, segmentation, and generation. He also named robotics and physical AI. At the same time, Q.ANT’s CEO called the Nvidia H200 an outstanding technological product and emphasized that GPUs have strengths photonic chips lack, and vice versa.

## Pilot installations and energy consumption estimates

Photonic processors are still rarely used in data centers. Q.ANT has started a pilot program with cloud company IONOS and said that more than 30 engineering teams are developing AI models optimized for photonic computing.

In Förtsch’s assessment, combining photonic processors with CPUs and GPUs could deliver more computing for AI while using less energy. In simulations, the company sees energy savings of about four to five times, but it has not yet demonstrated this result on real hardware.

There is no need to learn a new language to run programs: according to Förtsch, photonic processors can be used with C++ and Python, and existing code already runs on their systems. But programs optimized only for GPUs will not necessarily run faster without changes; they will need to be adapted.

Developers find it difficult to create models for hardware that few people can access, while cloud providers find it difficult to buy chips for which there is not yet enough software. Q.ANT is installing proof-of-concept systems in data centers to verify that photonic computing can withstand the conditions of this infrastructure. Förtsch compared this to solving the chicken-and-egg problem: the company wants to open its systems to developers so they can start building models for them.

## Manufacturing and GPU compatibility

Even if Q.ANT receives an order for 100,000 photonic processors, the company is not sure suppliers can deliver the required volumes. Förtsch noted that hardware manufacturing takes time: the supply chain still needs to become as resilient as the semiconductor industry’s.

Q.ANT needs a 90-nanometer process technology to manufacture its chips, one that emerged in the 2000s. According to Förtsch, many foundries already use it to produce inexpensive chips. He believes the same production line could be used to make more expensive, high-performance processors without major upfront investment in equipment.

Q.ANT is not tying its chips to a particular graphics card brand: the company wants to work with both Nvidia and AMD. Förtsch describes them as a complement to GPUs that expands the range of tasks they can perform.

Förtsch did not rule out the possibility that the company could try to compete with GPUs in five years, but noted that he does not know what computing workloads will look like then. In his view, further AI development will require models that use data more efficiently and perform tasks to a comparable standard with lower energy use and operating costs.

Dataist's take: Q.ANT’s photonic processors are still being tested in data centers, and the claimed reduction in energy consumption comes from simulations, not tests on real hardware. Their prospects depend both on adapting models and on whether the company can establish a chip supply. For now, Q.ANT sees photonic computing primarily as a complement to GPUs.
