AI companies may have mathematical breakthroughs that would change how secure encryption is understood, specialists say. Theoretical computer scientist Scott Aaronson told the Guardian that he had heard credible reports that advanced models had made progress on problems mathematicians had worked on for decades. If this makes it possible to decrypt protected data more quickly, banking transactions, diplomatic correspondence and personal email could be at risk. So far, companies have not announced any specific cryptography breakthroughs, and the results still need to be verified.

## What has alarmed specialists

In recent weeks, AI companies have reported several mathematical results. Most are abstract, but specialists see them as a sign that models are already solving problems many believed would remain unsolved for the foreseeable future.

Earlier this week, Aaronson wrote that he was almost certain several reliable sources indicated that AI labs had one or more major cryptography breakthroughs.

Stephen Murdoch, a professor of computer science at University College London, believes the idea that AI cannot solve interesting mathematical problems is already outdated. He said it is now difficult to tell where the limits of these systems lie.

On Friday, mathematician Marcus du Sautoy said on BBC Radio 4’s Today programme that the implications of many discoveries for encryption remain unknown. He noted that specialists rarely discuss the problem underlying the protection of electronic information. He suggested that the silence could be because the problem has already been solved, and the consequences would be so serious that people preferred not to talk about it.

## What data could be at risk

Aaronson and other specialists are considering two possible outcomes. AI may have found a new way to bypass encryption — or, conversely, proved that no such shortcut exists.

To do that, models would need to work through the mathematics underlying encryption algorithms, which is still not fully understood. If a way to decrypt protected messages has been found, it could open access to vast amounts of secret data. If it has been proved that encryption cannot be bypassed this way, that would show that data is better protected than previously thought.

Several types of encryption are used around the world, based on different algorithms. It is therefore unclear which systems possible breakthroughs might affect. Aaronson stressed that AI companies had made no specific claims about cryptography breakthroughs, although, he said, informed specialists suspect that such results exist.

## Why experts compare it with quantum computers

Governments, banks and intelligence agencies have been preparing for several years for the arrival of powerful decryption tools. The reason is quantum computers: once they can be built, they will be able to break the algorithms behind many types of encryption.

But that scenario is expected to be years away, not weeks or months. Governments and technology companies are moving to a higher encryption standard that can withstand quantum computers, but the transition is slow. Murdoch said the target had been around 2032 and would probably be missed.

Murdoch, Aaronson and other experts now allow for the possibility that new knowledge about cryptography could emerge suddenly and soon. Alan Woodward, a professor of computer science at the University of Surrey, warned that a mathematical breakthrough would not necessarily cause chaos the next day. What has changed most is how quickly AI can solve problems that remained mysteries for decades — and that could have serious consequences.

## The results still need to be checked

This week, Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey commented on concerns that companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic had made breakthroughs in cryptography. He said they should be cautious about releasing new models to the public.

In recent weeks, AI companies have published proofs of mathematical results related to the subject. One concerns a fundamental question about how to classify computationally difficult problems. Aaronson called these results significant for mathematicians and computer scientists: some have spent their careers on these problems, while others did not expect them to be solved in their lifetimes.

However, proofs presented by AI companies need to be checked by people — mathematicians and computer scientists. Woodward believes it is unlikely that any discovery would make it possible to break encryption in a matter of days. Even if such a discovery exists, practical obstacles would have to be overcome before it could be widely used.

Banks and governments that rely on encryption need to be able to change their security systems quickly, Woodward said: those that can act swiftly will be safe, while those that lag risk running out of time to prepare. Anthropic and OpenAI were asked for comment.

Dataist's take: The news points to a possible mathematical breakthrough, not a confirmed breach of encryption: companies have made no specific announcements, and the proofs need to be checked. The main practical signal from the experts is that being ready to change security systems matters, because the move to new standards is already slow.
