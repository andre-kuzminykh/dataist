## Meta shifts moderation to AI as employees fear widespread errors

## Moderator teams are shrinking

Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, has used AI for moderation since 2017, when it began applying the technology to filter material containing violent extremist content. In March, Meta announced a change in approach, saying it would introduce more advanced AI systems in its apps to improve the detection of violations.

Employees say the expansion of automation has been accompanied by mass layoffs affecting people who reviewed posts. Some teams now operate with reduced staff: certain groups have been cut by half or even two-thirds. One employee said they went to discuss an issue with a particular moderation team and found it had already been disbanded.

Employees say the algorithms lack an understanding of context, especially when working with languages other than English. As a result, they often remove posts that should not have been blocked. One Guardian source stressed that human involvement is necessary for decisions that depend on nuance and platform rules, while automation allows errors to spread faster and affect more users.

Meta is looking for low-cost ways to review the enormous volume of posts. The company has previously faced lawsuits from former outsourced moderators in Kenya. They described post-traumatic stress disorder and serious mental health problems after viewing extremely violent material. They could be paid as little as $1.50 (£1.13) an hour for this work.

Meta employees say the company is cutting both low-paid contractors, including in Kenya, and full-time staff who handled more complex cases. It has become harder to appeal a post removal: one employee noted that even knowing someone at Meta does not guarantee access to a person who can help with a difficult case.

## Blocks during political events

Researchers tracking online censorship are seeing more sudden restrictions on Instagram and Facebook accounts, including during major political events. Sometimes a single account is affected. Before the US midterm elections, for example, Meta suspended the No Kings movement’s account. In other cases, dozens of accounts are blocked within a few days.

Marta Dimitratu, founder of the digital censorship watchdog Repro Uncensored, described the trend as a growing phenomenon that could affect the political future of entire countries. The group has tracked blocks in Brazil, the EU, Turkey and the US.

Late last year, the Guardian reported a wave of blocks across Meta’s services in Europe. It affected accounts featuring queer content and pages focused on reproductive health. According to Repro Uncensored, during Brazil’s tense presidential runoff, Instagram restricted or blocked accounts supporting President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Meta said most blocks in Brazil were related to enforcing valid court orders or election rules.

Repro Uncensored recorded 210 cases of account blocks across Meta’s services in 2025 and 657 in the first six months of 2026.

## How Meta moderation works

Meta employees believe automated moderation may have contributed to some mass blocks: algorithms work faster than people and are worse at accounting for nuance. They do not rule out political considerations influencing decisions, either. The company’s systems are opaque even to its own employees and combine large language models with older filtering algorithms.

One employee described moderation as a system with thousands of constantly changing parameters that affect how posts are ranked. In their view, using AI sometimes lets the company shield contentious decisions about political content behind the work of algorithms.

As an example, employees pointed to changes Meta made to its algorithm in late 2023, after which the number of posts by people in Palestine shown to users fell sharply. The company rejected accusations of bias. Meta said it had conducted an independent human rights review in connection with the conflict and had been implementing its recommendations since 2022. Among the measures, the company cited a system that identifies material and passes it to moderators with a better understanding of the relevant Arabic dialect.

Meta also told the Guardian that the same rules apply to all users, and that claims of moderation based on political affiliation or social views are unfounded. A company spokesperson said Meta began testing more advanced AI systems in early 2026. In the latest tests, they made 13% fewer errors on average than people when detecting violations and found 10% more actual violations. The company added that users can appeal decisions they believe are mistaken.

Dataist's take: Meta’s claims about system accuracy do not resolve questions about how automation affects mass blocks and the ability to appeal them. Cuts to moderation teams could make algorithmic errors more visible precisely in cases where linguistic and political nuance matters.
