New York club Basement threatens lifetime ban over smart glasses

@neuronium_ai

Basement, one of New York's best-known contemporary nightclubs, has told prospective guests that anyone carrying smart glasses will be walked straight back out and may be barred permanently. Basement, one of New York's best-known contemporary nightclubs, has told prospective guests that anyone carrying smart glasses will be walked straight back out and may be barred permanently. The club's statement ties the rule to its existing ban on photos and video, which it says exists to protect the privacy, safety and freedom of everyone on the dance floor; devices built to record without anyone noticing are incompatible with that. The ban covers bringing the glasses as much as wearing them.

That last detail is the one worth pausing on. Most venue policies govern behaviour — do not film, put the phone away. Basement's governs possession. You do not have to switch anything on to be removed, and the penalty for a pair of glasses sitting in a coat pocket is the same as for a pair on your face: never coming back.

Basement can write a rule like that because its door already works that way. The club runs a strict and openly subjective selection in the style of Berlin's Berghain: a security guard sizes you up, and some people walk into the dark red-lit rooms while others are turned away with no explanation owed. Against that backdrop the new rule barely changes the mechanics of the door. Anyone conspicuous enough to arrive in Meta glasses was a poor bet to get past the guard in the first place.

Club audiences have taken the decision well, with one commenter saying every club should adopt the policy. That reaction is the actual news here, not the rule. Nightclubs have been banning phones for years, but the logic there is different: phones are an ordinary part of life that people occasionally want a break from, for the sake of their own attention and to avoid starring in a stranger's Instagram video. The message to smart glasses is not "take a night off." It is that you are not welcome with them.

The objections behind that are concrete. These are small surveillance devices used to record people who have not agreed to it. They have given misogynists a way to build online audiences by filming their own harassment of women. Nobody outside Meta knows precisely how the company uses the video and other data the glasses capture, and what is already established is not reassuring: contractors abroad have viewed naked people and intimate scenes without the device owners being told.

Set that against what Meta has spent to change the perception. Millions of dollars in advertising campaigns, endorsements from Kylie Jenner and Kylian Mbappé, and the product still carries a nickname built around the word pervert. This reads like a company that has misdiagnosed its own problem. Meta is buying awareness for a device whose constraint was never awareness. The thing it needs is permission from the people standing next to the wearer, and that is not a media buy. Celebrity endorsement works on products where the only person who has to approve the purchase is the buyer; a camera on someone's face fails that test by design.

Notably absent from the announcement is any account of how the rule gets enforced. Spotting a camera built into a pair of glasses, at night, at a door, on a person who would rather you did not, is a genuinely hard identification problem, and the club says nothing about how its staff will do it. Basement does not need an answer, because a door that already refuses people without giving reasons can absorb any number of false positives at no cost. That is the quiet condition on this whole story.

Smart glasses will keep spreading, and one club's door does nothing to slow that. But the venues that come after Basement — bars, gyms, changing rooms, clinics, offices — owe the people they turn away a reason, and none of them has a bouncer whose taste is allowed to be the entire policy.
