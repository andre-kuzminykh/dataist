The Pangram service determined that AI had written 99% of the text of *Donde el agua trabaja*, a novel by Peruvian author David Silva Rojas. The book won a literary prize, but Mexican publisher Almadía did not revoke the award: after reviewing the author’s documents, it publicly stood by him. According to *El País*, Pangram also classified more than 80% of material from an interview, an essay and an Instagram post in which Silva Rojas rejected the allegations as AI-generated.

## The dispute over the test

In an essay for *The Point Magazine*, the author accuses Latin American critics of ignoring Pangram’s test results and dismissing the allegations as an expression of racism. She compares the dispute with the confirmed use of AI by writer Taylison Orellen, as well as with allegations involving the Caine Prize and Jamir Nazir, who kept his award.

The author also argues that literary juries often favor clichéd stories about minorities — plots that language models can easily imitate. According to the source, publisher Gallimard plans to start checking manuscripts with Pangram next year.

## The limits of detectors

In the United States, writers are represented by the Authors Guild. The organization cautions against using AI detectors as the sole basis for decisions. In Pangram’s tests, however, it reliably identified human-written text.

The case shows that a detector’s result does not, by itself, settle a dispute over authorship: the publisher stood by Silva Rojas after reviewing his documents, while the Authors Guild’s position points to the risk of relying only on an automated assessment. The source does not specify which documents Almadía reviewed or how the publisher evaluated Pangram’s results.
