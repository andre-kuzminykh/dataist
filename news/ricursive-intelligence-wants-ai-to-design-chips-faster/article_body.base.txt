## The chip is the constraint

Goldie argues that even building data centers in space would not solve the problem without more efficient chips. Ricursive’s answer is to use AI to design entire chips, rather than divide them into dozens of smaller blocks as traditional approaches do.

The founders say AI can find layouts that look unfamiliar to engineers but may work better. AlphaChip, a system they developed before Ricursive, produced a circular, doughnut-like arrangement of chip components. Engineers were initially surprised; the design proved successful.

Ricursive says its whole-chip approach can create complex patterns, including wave-like layouts. The founders plan to share more details later.

## More designs, if the economics work

The founders see AI-designed chips making it possible to build silicon for applications that were previously too costly or impractical to serve. If a new chip takes six months, a year or two to develop, companies have to choose their projects carefully. Goldie says that cutting design time to about a week could make it viable to create specialized chips for space, augmented and virtual reality, and biological computing.

The broader shift, as Goldie describes it, is that people bring ideas and creative intent while AI handles implementation. She expects this pattern to extend across chip design, software development and other fields. Her argument is that automating more of the design work frees people to focus on applications and new uses for computing.

I think the compelling part is not simply that AI might produce stranger layouts. It is the possibility of making chip design economical for more narrowly defined jobs. But the announcement leaves the key test unanswered: how much faster or more efficient Ricursive’s chips are, and whether the gains justify designing a different chip for each use. A week-long design cycle matters only if the resulting hardware performs well enough to pay for its specialization.
