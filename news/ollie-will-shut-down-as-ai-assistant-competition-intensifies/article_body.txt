## What Ollie was built to do

Ollie offered a subscription service for families to stay connected and manage shared tasks. It could help schedule appointments, plan meals and pay bills; the company had also planned to add family budgeting tools.

In September, Ollie told TechCrunch it had raised $7.5 million. It was also among the first mass-market apps to meet SOC 2, a standard companies can verify through an independent audit.

That focus on privacy addressed a real concern: digital assistants can pose specific risks to users’ privacy, as examples involving Instinct have shown. But privacy credentials do not necessarily translate into a business that can compete for users and investment.

## A crowded market, and a quiet question

Lennon said pressure from the expanding AI industry ultimately affected the company. Ollie had come close to success, he said, but could not keep pace. As Muse, Instinct and other competitors entered the market, it became harder to differentiate the product, grow its audience and attract the capital needed to develop it.

The wider market for text-based AI assistants grew quickly over the past year, as more capable models made these apps more useful for everyday tasks. That shift raised the bar for a product whose pitch was not just assistance, but private, family-oriented assistance.

I think the unanswered question is whether privacy can be a durable advantage when users can get more capable help elsewhere. Ollie’s closure does not answer that, but it makes the tension plain: the more personal an assistant becomes, the more privacy matters—and the harder it may be to fund the product around it.

In announcing the shutdown, Lennon wrote, “I’m proud of what we accomplished.” He said the small team had built a personal assistant and learned a lot, and thanked employees, users, investors and everyone who supported the company.

Users will receive instructions in the coming days on exporting their data and getting refunds.
