# Where scientists draw the line for AI agents

## What needs to be checked

Delegating a task to an agent does not relieve researchers of responsibility for how the work is done or for its results. If AI analyzes publications, scientists need to check whether conflicting data have been overlooked and whether the cited papers support the system’s conclusions. When automating data analysis, it is important to know which methods the agent used, which files it accessed and whether the result can be reproduced.

When working with laboratory protocols, researchers need to ensure that the system follows physical safety requirements, such as limits on temperature and material toxicity. If a study uses data about people, their privacy must be protected and access to the information restricted. Research organizations also need clear rules for checking and disclosing work done with AI.

For the authors, responsible scientific work is about more than accuracy. Ethical standards arose in part in response to past decisions and actions by scientists and scientific communities; they concern both the research process and its consequences. AI tools should expand scientists’ capabilities without depriving them of the ability to check evidence, methods and their own conclusions.

The authors study the safety of models developed at Ai2. They say much of the research on scientific AI safety focuses on catastrophic risks in fields such as biology and nuclear physics. But there is also a need for ways to assess whether agents follow standards for responsible research in the disciplines where they are used.

In the interviews, the authors identified five components of scientific trust in AI:

- <strong>Checking results</strong> — whether the system’s conclusions can be verified.
- <strong>Privacy protection</strong> — how the system handles sensitive information.
- <strong>Objectivity</strong> — how potential bias can be identified.
- <strong>Accountability</strong> — who checks the result and takes responsibility for it.
- <strong>Transparency</strong> — whether it is possible to understand how the answer was produced.

According to the authors, scientists should help choose tasks for agents, define rules for how they operate and evaluate their results. Even a technically correct answer may be unsuitable for research if it is impossible to check how it was produced, or if the agent accessed restricted data or files beyond its permissions.

Participants differed in their views on the future of science: some expect AI to drive progress, while others worry about the role people will retain in research. The challenges they identified include training people to work with AI and developing and implementing rules for its use.

## How to evaluate agents’ work

Participants were drawn to the prospect of assigning AI labor-intensive tasks: processing large datasets, writing code, documenting experiments and handling repetitive administrative work. Automation could leave more time for fieldwork, mentoring and developing new ideas. It could also make it possible to analyze volumes of data that are difficult to study manually.

But a final answer alone is not enough to show whether an agent performed a task reliably. Several participants said they check AI responses against primary publications rather than taking summaries on trust. They were concerned about confident answers with subtle errors and tools that reinforce a preselected hypothesis by finding only studies that support it. Researchers also need to check for missing sources, compliance with the specified methodology and the information the agent accessed along the way.

The authors propose evaluating scientific agents with these risks in mind:

- <strong>Benchmark for literature reviews</strong> — determine whether an agent misses conflicting data or draws conclusions unsupported by its sources.
- <strong>Benchmark for coding</strong> — assess not only whether the code works, but also whether the agent accesses files outside its permitted scope.
- <strong>Evaluation of data processing</strong> — compare agents’ methods and results with established scientific workflows.

The authors’ long-term goal is to create a benchmark in which scientists help define research practices and safety requirements for evaluating agents. Ai2 wants to assess not only whether systems complete tasks, but also whether they comply with the standards of the disciplines where they are used.

For now, these are proposals to be developed and tested with scientists, not ready-made solutions. The authors connect them to their work at Ai2: open scientific tools should make it possible to study systems’ behavior and limitations, while the scientific community should help define what reliable agent performance means.

## Which decisions to leave to scientists

Participants discussed how automation affects scientific expertise. An early-career biologist deliberately limited their use of AI to learn skills they expected to teach future students. Another researcher questioned whether getting the right result was enough if a person learned nothing in the process. In the participants’ view, the value of automation depends on the task and the researcher’s experience: an experienced scientist can delegate a familiar procedure to save time, while someone still learning it may benefit more from doing the work themselves.

Participants also drew boundaries around tasks involving intellectual contributions. One biologist did not want to use AI to generate research ideas, seeing that as a personal responsibility. Others were open to using it to find ideas or write text. An anthropologist noted that some parts of scientific papers — especially standard descriptions of methods and results — are formulaic enough to automate.

One political scientist used Claude to edit a manuscript, but then decided to rewrite it from scratch: the line between their own contribution and the AI’s work had become unclear. Some participants supported using AI to critique a manuscript before submission, but were wary of using it to review another researcher’s unpublished work. The issue is not just the system’s capabilities: peer review involves confidentiality, proper attribution and fair assessment of someone else’s research.

There is no universal list of permitted and prohibited tasks: scientific disciplines have different standards, and researchers may have different goals even when doing similar work.

Disagreements also concerned restrictions built into AI systems. One political scientist worried that bans on discussing certain topics could hinder legitimate research into political violence and human rights violations. Participants who work with sensitive personal data, by contrast, wanted stronger privacy protections, access controls and clear rules for using the information.

Restrictions that are too weak could lead to the disclosure of restricted data or harmful results. Overly broad restrictions could obstruct scientific work. That is why scientific communities need to help determine which decisions should remain with researchers, where restrictions are necessary and where they could impede valuable research.

## Developing agents with scientists

Researchers should be able to check an agent’s contribution, choose what work to assign it and retain opportunities to develop their own expertise. This requires transparent records of the system’s actions, detailed controls over data access and evaluations grounded in the standards and practices of different scientific communities.

The authors emphasize that their work is preliminary. Their conclusions are based on interviews with 17 scientists in the US: this is neither a representative survey of the scientific community nor an empirical evaluation of specific agents. Participants discussed both the generative AI tools they use today and their expectations for more autonomous systems. Their responses point to concerns and potential development priorities, but do not establish a consensus on AI use in science.

At Ai2, the expectation is that AI can accelerate scientific discovery while preserving openness, reproducibility and independent verification of results. To that end, the organization plans to work with scientists to identify useful tasks for agents, turn researchers’ concerns into concrete evaluations and make not only systems’ results but also how they were produced easier to understand. The authors see promise in AI helping investigate questions that might otherwise remain out of reach, without depriving scientists of knowledge, independence or the right to make decisions.

<strong>Dataist’s take:</strong> The central question is not how much work can be handed to an agent, but whether its actions can be checked and researchers can remain accountable for its conclusions. Ai2’s proposals point toward further development, but have not yet been validated through tests of specific systems. AI restrictions need to account for the standards of different disciplines; otherwise, safeguards against risk could obstruct legitimate research.
