## What Microsoft promised

In a blog post, Smith laid out a five-point plan for working with communities across the United States. The commitments included covering costs so data centers would not raise residents’ electricity bills, creating local jobs, and investing in AI training and nonprofits.

Smith also said Microsoft would send representatives to cities with AI data centers and listen to residents, who know best what their communities need and which organizations can help.

We Make Indiana, a coalition of 25 religious, law-enforcement, mental-health and other groups, approached Microsoft about raising money for local public services. According to Ars Technica, the effort ran into the limits of the company’s promised support.

The coalition works in South Bend, where more than 20% of residents live below the federal poverty line: an annual income of less than $15,960 for one person and $33,000 for a family. Underfunded services include public health care, affordable child and elder care, housing and public transit.

Ryan Yuskus, an assistant professor at the University of Notre Dame, told Ars the request was an attempt to address local needs after the state had left residents without support.

## The limits of a donation

The coalition asked Microsoft to contribute a small share of its revenue. Even 1% could have brought local programs $30–40 million a year, it said. Microsoft representatives ignored the proposal, Ars reported.

The company’s representatives can make only one-time donations from a general fund of $1 million. Only nonprofits are eligible; public programs and public-private organizations cannot receive the money.

The coalition’s request also sits against the scale of the project and the tax breaks offered to data centers. Local activists argue that taxes from Microsoft’s $1 billion data center could have supported residents in South Bend and other cities if officials had not approved a major tax exemption.

A 2025 analysis by Mirror Indy estimated that such incentives could cost Indiana $900 million in tax revenue overall. The total may have grown as data center projects continued to expand in the state.

## A neighbor, on whose terms?

Yuskus told Ars that Microsoft’s permitted donations are negligible beside the size of the project. He said the coalition would keep organizing residents and elected officials, using every available means to secure real negotiations with the company.

I think the gap here is not just between a promise to listen and a missed meeting. Microsoft’s donation rules define community support narrowly: small, one-off gifts to nonprofits, not funding for the public services residents say they need. The unanswered question is whether the company’s “good neighbor” policy can address those needs without reaching the programs its own rules exclude.
