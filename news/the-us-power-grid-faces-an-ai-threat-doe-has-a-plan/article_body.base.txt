On November 16, 2026, the US Department of Energy (DOE) announced STAISYS, a test system that examines how artificial intelligence (AI) technologies behave under cyberattacks and the physical dynamics of the power grid. The project is part of the AI-FORTS program, which brings together national laboratories, utilities, and researchers to develop and test energy-sector defenses. AI can automate vulnerability discovery and attacks, but it can also cause disruptions if its models, data, or software are compromised.

## DOE is building an AI testing system to protect US power grids

## Why the energy sector needs AI testing

Electricity is essential to telecommunications, financial systems, hospitals, manufacturing, and national defense. A disruption to power generation or transmission can therefore affect several critical sectors at once.

AI agents can automate vulnerability discovery, reconnaissance, and the launch of attacks. Operators must defend themselves at a speed that people cannot manage alone. But AI also creates new risks: a faulty software update, an incorrect configuration, or an automated process could itself cause a disruption. The more grid management and protection tasks are assigned to AI, the greater the risk of such failures may become.

DOE faces a question: can AI protect the energy sector from attacks that use AI—and what happens if the protective systems themselves become targets?

The AI-FORTS program is led by DOE’s Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER). It brings together work in three areas:

- <strong>Secure From AI</strong> — studying how malicious actors use AI against critical infrastructure: finding vulnerabilities, conducting reconnaissance, using phishing, and carrying out other stages of attacks. The goal is to understand how the threat is changing and test defenses before an adversary exploits weaknesses.
- <strong>Secure With AI</strong> — using AI for defense: analyzing large volumes of data, recognizing anomalies, detecting threats, and supporting incident response. In the energy sector, this could help identify suspicious activity in industrial control systems and help utilities continue operating after a successful attack.
- <strong>Secure AI</strong> — protecting AI systems themselves. If they are tasked with monitoring or managing power grids, or detecting attacks, malicious actors may try to manipulate models, corrupt data, or exploit software vulnerabilities.

Similar areas of work feature in other initiatives. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework profile for AI covers protecting AI systems, using AI for cybersecurity, and countering AI-enabled attacks. Google’s Secure AI Framework focuses on AI security throughout the lifecycle, while MITRE ATLAS describes adversary techniques targeting AI. AI-FORTS differs in its focus on operating energy systems: a cyber disruption in these systems can have consequences far beyond a data breach.

## Attacks are faster, and testing defenses is difficult

At the Aspen Digital Cyber Summit on November 19, 2026, DOE Deputy Assistant Secretary Alex Fitzsimmons took part in a discussion with Drew Bagley, CrowdStrike’s chief privacy and policy officer. The session was moderated by SANS instructor and IDX director Marie DeGracia.

Bagley described a shift from traditional malware to intrusions using valid credentials and system tools, followed by AI-enabled attacks and increasingly autonomous AI agents. According to CrowdStrike, the shortest “breakout time” the company has recorded—the interval between compromising one system and an attacker moving to another—has fallen to 27 seconds. Bagley noted that measuring attacks on a human timescale has effectively become meaningless.

Speed is not the only problem. A November 20 report from the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) examines how AI increases the scale, precision, and effectiveness of cyberattacks. The report describes a case in which several AI agents tested malicious code against different defensive tools. It also identifies challenges in evaluating AI cybersecurity technologies: detailed testing guidance is lacking, and the reliability of these tools remains uncertain.

Energy companies also face obstacles rooted in the industry itself: some infrastructure is outdated, equipment is difficult to upgrade, and shutting down critical systems can have serious consequences.

Fitzsimmons said utilities should assume that malicious actors may get inside and prepare their systems to keep operating after a compromise. He discussed separating information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT) networks, limiting attackers’ ability to move between them, and isolating critical industrial subsystems if an intrusion reaches the operational environment. The goal is to stop a single intrusion from escalating into widespread equipment failure.

## How DOE tests AI for the energy sector

One part of AI-FORTS is a network of cyber testbeds at DOE national laboratories. In April 2026, the department and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory launched the Mjölnir AI Testbed. It is designed to evaluate AI models that could be used in energy operations, grid management, and other infrastructure tasks. Researchers test whether a model behaves unsafely, reveals sensitive information, or fails under adversarial attacks.

In July, DOE introduced Stormbreaker, a project for testing large language models and AI agents in changing conditions. The system changes model configurations and operating conditions to identify when they begin to fail. DOE is also expanding its research with Sandia National Laboratories.

In September, DOE and Sandia reported results from the Communications and Cybersecurity for the Energy Edge (C2E2) project. The system uses generative AI to process grid data and detect cyber threats. According to DOE, it cut some data-processing tasks from about two months to a few hours and detected and localized threat sources with 95% accuracy.

On November 16, the department announced Scaled Testing for Artificial Intelligence Systems (STAISYS), also developed with Sandia. It tests advanced AI technologies in simulated conditions where cyberattacks interact with the physical behavior of power systems. Researchers examine how equipment is affected by malicious sensor data, compromised control logic, and attacks on computing infrastructure.

An AI model may pass a cyber benchmark but behave differently when connected to equipment that controls electricity generation or distribution. Preliminary testing can help determine which systems can be trusted with critical functions.

## The gap between large and small providers

The article identifies the digital divide between energy companies with substantial resources and those that lack funding for cybersecurity as one of the main risks. Large companies can maintain well-resourced security teams and invest in modern threat-detection tools. Smaller municipal utilities and rural electric cooperatives often cannot.

At Aspen, Fitzsimmons spoke about companies where one employee is responsible for IT and nobody handles cybersecurity full-time. Yet smaller providers may supply electricity to military bases, hospitals, and other critical facilities. Many important military and national security facilities are in remote areas where municipal utilities and rural cooperatives may be the only or primary energy providers.

As a result, highly sensitive facilities may depend on companies with the fewest resources to defend against increasingly sophisticated attacks. Malicious actors do not necessarily distinguish between providers with thousands of employees and small companies with limited technical staff; more vulnerable targets may be more attractive to them.

According to Fitzsimmons, AI-FORTS is intended to help smaller operators assess and adopt new technologies without conducting complex research themselves. In his view, the federal role does not necessarily have to include developing commercial cybersecurity products: the department can help the industry determine which technologies work and how to deploy them safely. Bagley added that smaller operators can turn to managed cybersecurity providers to gain access to specialists and modern defensive tools without building their own security operations center.

DOE’s partnerships with national laboratories may make complex testing more accessible, but AI-FORTS is not a general cybersecurity subsidy for utilities.

## What still needs to be tested

AI-FORTS is connected to DOE’s broader work on applying AI to science and national infrastructure. As part of the Genesis Mission, the department announced $293 million in competitive funding for scientific and technical projects, including AI system cybersecurity. This funding supports the broader mission and is not earmarked for AI-FORTS.

The next question is how quickly research results will translate into practical defenses. The GAO report of November 20 lists possible measures including expanding AI testing environments, requiring human approval before autonomous actions with serious consequences, and creating backup capabilities for critical cybersecurity functions.

This issue extends beyond the energy sector: organizations are deploying AI in operational systems even though they cannot always reliably predict how it will behave under attack, manipulation, or outside established limits. The uncertainty is especially significant for energy companies: equipment failure could leave thousands or millions of customers without electricity, along with critical facilities.

Dataist's take: Testing in conditions that approximate how power grids operate helps assess risks that ordinary cyber benchmarks cannot reveal. But testbeds alone do not solve the shortage of funding and specialists at smaller providers. Protecting the power system requires technologies that are not only available for testing, but also suitable for safe deployment across infrastructure of different sizes.
