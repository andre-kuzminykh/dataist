=== HEADLINE ===
UK financial regulator says Meta refused to test Muse

## What the FCA wanted to test

Colin Payne, the FCA’s head of innovation, described Meta’s proposal at a discussion organised by AI company Whitespace. He said the market was filling with new tools and AI agents, many of them coming from the US. The regulator wanted to understand in advance how Muse, presented as a personal assistant, would interact with customers.

The FCA is already studying products from other companies. For example, Amazon and other market participants are launching AI shopping assistants. The regulator is especially interested in payments that agents could make on users’ behalf.

Meta is working with several UK retailers to make it easier for Muse to access their websites:

- Sainsbury’s, Marks & Spencer and Ocado — grocery chains;
- Debenhams Group — department store;
- Currys — electronics retailer.

Payne is also concerned that a user’s prompt could lead to unintended consequences, particularly if the person has little understanding of how an AI product works. He said that if an agent acts on its own, it is important to make sure it is doing so in the consumer’s interests.

Preparing a test would take time: regulators typically discuss a project with a company at length before planning work over several weeks or months. Meta told the FCA about its plans only last month.

Meta representative Matthew Pollard told WIRED that the company had engaged constructively with the relevant regulators and would continue to do so.

## UK authorities are watching the rollout

Since September, more than 10 million users in North America have set up Muse. UK regulators are watching the service’s expansion closely and expect it could also quickly attract users in the UK: many people there already use Instagram, Facebook and WhatsApp. Payne said that if Muse became just as popular in the UK, the regulator would need to see how it works.

The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) stressed that users must understand how Muse uses their personal data and be able to control it.

The UK’s Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) has already secured changes to Meta’s terms for UK users. They state that when providing Muse, the company must exercise “reasonable skill and care”. Documents for the UK and EU also specify that the service’s responses may be inaccurate and that actions may not be carried out as intended.

The CMA said it would not hesitate to act if it saw a threat to consumers in the UK.

Dataist’s take: Refusing joint testing leaves the FCA unable to examine in advance how Muse interacts with customers and handles payments. UK authorities are already requiring Meta to account for data protection and consumer interests, while the service’s terms acknowledge that errors and failed actions are possible.
