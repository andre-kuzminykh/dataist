## What failed

Star News Korea reported that the refrigerators stopped working after the update. Some owners said they had to throw away food prepared for the holiday.

The timing made the failure more than an ordinary repair complaint:

- The outage happened before Chuseok.
- The holiday lasts three days.
- Service visits were not expected before October 1.
- Some owners lost food intended for the celebration.

Samsung markets its AI refrigerators as appliances that can recognize their contents, recommend recipes and play music. Those features may sound useful in a product demonstration, but the incident exposes the less glamorous side of connected appliances: a device that once worked independently can now be disrupted by software delivered remotely.

The report does not establish how many refrigerators were affected. It also leaves unanswered whether Samsung will compensate owners for spoiled food, or how the company will prevent the same update failure from recurring.

## The appliance nobody needed to update

Electric refrigerators have existed for more than a century. They became familiar, relatively simple parts of domestic life, changing incrementally over decades.

AI is now being added to products that were already good at their core jobs:

- AI toothbrushes
- AI toasters
- Doorbells with AI and facial recognition

I think the refrigerator failure makes the trade-off unusually clear. Recipe suggestions and music playback are optional conveniences; preserving food is the reason the appliance exists. When an update can disable the second while adding or maintaining the first, the software layer stops looking like an upgrade and starts looking like a new source of dependency.

Consumers still have choices when buying household appliances. My concern is that those choices may narrow if companies keep treating AI as a feature that belongs in every part of daily life. The risk is not that every smart appliance will fail this way. It is that reliable alternatives will become harder to find just as more ordinary objects acquire remote software—and more ways to stop being ordinary.
