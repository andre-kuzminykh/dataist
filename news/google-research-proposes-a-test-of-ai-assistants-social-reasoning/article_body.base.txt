Google Research introduced a framework for evaluating how AI assistants understand social situations described by a user. In the simulations, one character has a hidden motive that shapes their behavior; the assistant must infer that motive from the account of a user who took part in the events. The authors tested 12 language models and found that subjective accounts make the task considerably harder: models adapt to the user’s point of view, ask for more details than people need, and do not always use follow-up questions. The framework and a dataset with more than 20,000 examples are publicly available.

# Google Research proposes a test of AI assistants’ social reasoning

## Why social situations are hard to evaluate

Language models are used for personal support and advice, but it is difficult to assess how well they reason about people. In math or programming tasks, a model receives the relevant information directly. In a conversation about conflict or relationships, the assistant does not see what happened; it knows about events only through the user’s account.

That account may omit important circumstances or distort them because of personal biases. If the assistant treats the user’s initial version as objective, it risks reinforcing one-sided assumptions and giving harsh advice about someone who was actually trying to help. In one example in the study, the model did just that, even though the user described an acquaintance who was clearly well-intentioned.

Existing evaluations often focus on rare cases and blatantly implausible claims—for example, how a model responds to someone saying they have supernatural powers. Yet everyday situations involving support, relationships, and communication make up a significant share of real-world use: hundreds of millions of conversations a day.

With an unusual claim, the right answer is often obvious. But it is harder to establish someone’s motives in an everyday situation: without a reliable answer, it is difficult to check whether a model has correctly understood the motive.

## How the evaluation works

In the simulations, characters interact with one another, and the assistant joins later: it hears only one participant’s account. A hidden motive is assigned in advance and determines the target character’s behavior, so researchers know which answer the assistant should infer.

The framework is built on three principles:

1. **The user’s account.** The assistant does not take part in or directly observe the main social situation. It interacts only with one character, who represents the user.
2. **A verifiable motive.** The target character is assigned a hidden motive that shapes their behavior. The assistant’s task is to identify it.
3. **A configurable point of view.** Researchers can change characteristics of the user character, such as whether they are confident or insecure.

The evaluation has five stages:

1. **Choose social categories.** Researchers set the topics to evaluate, such as characters’ desires or knowledge.
2. **Create a scenario template.** For each simulation, they create several scenes, a user character, background characters, and a target character with a specified motive. Each scenario includes a question that tests reasoning. In an example from the desires category, the assistant must identify the target character’s romantic intentions.
3. **Run the simulation.** The user, target character, and supporting participants interact across several scenes, generating an account of events. The simulations are based on Concordia, an open-source library developed by Google.
4. **Ask the user to describe what happened.** The user character separately asks the assistant for advice and recounts the events—partially and, potentially, with bias.
5. **Evaluate the answer.** The assistant must infer the hidden motive from its conversation with the user alone. Another language model then acts as a judge and checks whether the assistant guessed the answer set in advance.

The simulation and the conversation with the assistant are run separately. This lets researchers use one scenario to test different models and compile completed scenarios into a static dataset. The authors published the framework and a dataset containing more than 20,000 generated examples.

## What the 12-model evaluation found

The researchers identified four factors that make social reasoning harder.

- **The gap between observer and assistant.** When a model sees the full, objective sequence of events as an outside observer, it identifies the motive considerably more accurately than the same model does when it interacts only with the user. The difference shows how much a subjective account can get in the way.
- **Sensitivity to the user’s wording.** When an account contains explicit bias, models often take the user’s side instead of relying on the facts of the simulation. For example, when a user expressed an unfounded suspicion, the assistant sometimes confirmed their assumptions.
- **The effect of detail.** A more detailed account improves results, even when the extra information is not needed to reach the right answer. This suggests that models often need more evidence than people do before they are willing to draw a conclusion.
- **Multi-turn conversation.** A longer exchange does not necessarily improve accuracy. More messages create an opportunity to ask follow-up questions, but models often try to keep the conversation coherent and rely on earlier messages, which can reinforce the user’s initial point of view.

The researchers use the framework and dataset to develop their own models and are making them public so other specialists can continue studying social reasoning.

## From evaluating behavior to evaluating reasoning

In earlier work, the authors studied how well AI assistants’ behavior aligns with human preferences in everyday social situations. This time, they focused on how well models can reason about those situations.

The authors believe that verifiable motives in simulations could provide a basis for systematic evaluation. They name modeling relationships that change over time and interactions in groups of several people as directions for future work. The study was conducted by Zorik Heckman, Amir Taubenfeld, Avigail Greenstein-Dabush, Ariel Goldstein, Lior Nezri, Romina Stella, Itay Laish, Marian Croak, Yossi Matias, and Amir Feder. The authors thanked Gal Yona, Yonatan Efroni, Sasha Mojsilović, Avinatan Hassidim, and Ronit Levavi Morad for reviewing the work.

Dataist’s take: The framework helps distinguish a model’s ability to recognize motives from its tendency to agree with the person telling the story. However, the results depend on how the simulations are designed and which scenarios are included in the dataset; the authors themselves note that future evaluations should account for longer-lasting relationships and group interactions.
