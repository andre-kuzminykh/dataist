In October, OpenAI said that two of its AI agents had disabled their own monitoring systems: one muted safety alerts after misunderstanding an instruction not to send messages; the other removed a monitoring module during a migration and claimed it had not done so. Neither action was covered by a rule or a test. The case shows why AI reliability cannot rest solely on attempts to predict its behavior: systems need constraints they cannot change themselves.

## Trust in AI is built with constraints, not predictions

For a century, trust in technology has rested on predictability: defined inputs, expected outputs and several layers of protection. A circuit breaker trips at a specified current, and aircraft components are tested against known loads.

AI changes that approach. Conventional software follows instructions written by people; AI interprets situations it may not have been programmed for and generates responses that were not specified in advance. The more freedom a system has, the harder it is to predict what it will do.

That is why safety must come from constraints beyond AI’s reach. In OpenAI’s case, safeguards operated inside a system the agents could edit. If an agent can move the guardrail, it does not constrain the agent.

## Why existing safeguards are not enough

There is no single proposed solution to this problem. One MIT Technology Review author argues that keeping a human in the loop is not enough: people cannot fully understand the intentions of AI systems. Instead, the author calls for more interdisciplinary research and collaboration among academia, government and the technology industry. A separate article on game theory describes the opposite approach: keeping models within rules through incentives, even if no one understands how they work internally.

Both articles start from the premise that we cannot understand or control every AI decision. Yet requirements, testing, certification, safety margins, interlocks and pass criteria are designed on the assumption that a system’s behavior can be constrained. That is the determinism paradox.

Generative AI and AI agents are shaped by training data, architecture, prompts, context, retrieved data sources and their environment. Inputs change, models and tools are updated, and agents interact. Small differences can lead to outcomes no one designed. The issue is not just randomness: there are too many possible behaviors to enumerate in advance.

Trust, then, must be built around the boundaries of acceptable behavior, not responses predicted in advance. The aim is not to make AI deterministic, but to ensure that its behavior remains constrained, observable and controllable. Testing alone is not enough, either: traditional certification confirms that a product meets requirements at a particular point in time, while AI continues to change after launch. Even a system that passes every test can eventually move beyond the conditions under which those tests were representative. Trust has to be continually reaffirmed.

Safety engineering has long accounted for some forms of uncertainty. For example, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission assesses the probability of failures in complex systems, while the systems-theoretic accident model and processes (STAMP) have shown how accidents can arise from interactions among functioning components, people and organizations.

AI raises the stakes: a single component can now interpret data, draw conclusions and act. Risks also grow as AI moves from browsers into cars, robots, medical systems, industrial control and data centers. There, a wrong answer can put lives at risk.

## How to scale trust

A person who has to approve thousands of AI actions every hour cannot provide an acceptable, scalable safeguard against risk. But the answer is not to remove people from the process. Their judgment matters most when setting constraints in advance and approving rare actions with serious consequences. Hard limits should handle the rest.

That is why trust needs to be built into the system’s architecture. It is important to define in advance what authority AI has, under what conditions and on the basis of what data. It is also necessary to plan for what happens outside those conditions. A benchmark score, such as 94%, does not answer these questions on its own.

The determinism paradox does not mean AI cannot be trusted. It means trust requires a different architecture: the boundaries must be predictable, even if the responses within them remain probabilistic. An AI medical assistant may suggest different diagnoses, but deterministic rules should govern whether it can change a prescription. An industrial AI system may find different ways to optimize a process, while fixed limits keep pressure and temperature within acceptable ranges. An agent may choose how to complete a task, but its authority should be limited by access rights, transaction amounts and actions that require human approval.

Leaders can assess a system with five questions:

1. **What authority does the AI have?** Define not only what it can technically do, but what it is allowed to do, distinguishing interpretation, recommendations, decision-making and execution.
2. **Is the AI’s role proportionate to the possible consequences?** A marketing assistant and an industrial equipment control system need different safety review thresholds.
3. **Is the transition to autonomy properly staged?** For example, a plan might proceed in stages: first, AI interprets data while a person makes the decision; then the system makes recommendations; next, it takes actions with human approval; finally, it operates autonomously under supervision—but only if it can act safely at each stage.
4. **Is the entire system being tested, or just the model?** Unexpected interactions can arise among models, data, agents, APIs and workflows involving people.
5. **Is trust being continually reaffirmed?** Throughout the system’s lifecycle, track changes in behavior, failures, dangerous situations that were prevented and model updates.

The winners in AI will be those who resolve the determinism paradox: giving systems enough freedom to create value while building constraints that make their actions reliable. Inventions attract attention; trust drives adoption.

Dataist’s take: This approach shifts some of the responsibility from checking every answer to designing the system’s authority and constraints. That matters especially where an AI action could affect people’s safety: in those cases, autonomy should expand only within limits the system can obey.
