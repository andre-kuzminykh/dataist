Google has started asking some users to record a video selfie when signing in to their accounts, Search Engine Watch reports. The feature remains optional, but the company is now promoting it on the account homepage rather than leaving it as a fallback for people locked out or unable to use their usual devices. That turns face recognition from an emergency recovery tool into a proposed routine interface—and potentially gives Google another biometric signal tied to account access, age checks and service improvement.

Google first announced the feature in July as an additional way to regain access when a user was locked out or could not use their usual devices. The current change is its placement: on the homepage, users now see a prompt at the bottom of the screen to “sign in with a selfie,” with options to “don’t set up” or “set up selfie.”

Setting it up requires a short video in which the user turns their head and shows their face from different angles. If sign-in problems occur later, Google asks for another selfie and uses it to grant access to the account.

The video appears to have a wider role than account recovery. Google’s setup page warns that the selfie can help determine whether a user is old enough to access some of the company’s services. Users can also separately agree to let Google use the video to improve its services, including face recognition, age estimation and other verification methods that rely on a person’s physical characteristics or movements.

That makes the opt-in distinction less simple than it first appears. The login feature is optional, but Google is actively placing it in front of users; the additional use for service improvement requires separate consent. In practice, the company is presenting one recording as a tool for access, an age check and a possible source of training or evaluation data.

The more consequential shift is that Google is asking users to attach a recording of their face to an identity system they already use. Google already collects large volumes of information about the behaviour of billions of users to improve search and serve targeted advertising. Adding a mathematical model of a face would give the same company another category of information about those people.

The unanswered issue is what happens when convenience meets deception. The announcement does not establish how reliably face recognition can resist attempts to fool it, while increasingly convincing and accessible AI-based deepfake technologies make that a more immediate concern. Google’s move therefore reads less like a minor recovery feature than a test of whether users will accept biometric access as ordinary account infrastructure before the security case is settled.
