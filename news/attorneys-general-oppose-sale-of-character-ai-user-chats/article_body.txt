## Attorneys general from 42 states oppose sale of Character.ai chats

## What is up for sale

Character.ai filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October. The attorneys general say the company had previously assured users that it would not sell their personal information. A post on Character.ai’s blog also said that users’ data would continue to be protected under the service’s privacy policy.

The case now includes a proposed sale of chat histories and other personal data. So far, only one anonymous company seeking to acquire the data has been disclosed: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, or Smirks. It was registered in Delaware on November 13, 2026. The following day, Character.ai selected its offer as the opening bid for the auction scheduled for December 1. California Attorney General Rob Bonta noted that the buyer’s plans for the conversations are unknown.

## Why the attorneys general object

The attorneys general say users have no guarantees: the data’s new owner could use it as they see fit, including reselling it without notifying users. The new owner would also have no obligation to follow Character.ai’s privacy policy.

According to the attorneys general, the company gave the court less information about potential safety risks and legal consequences of the sale than it provided to bidders. In addition, California, Colorado, New York and Connecticut have passed chatbot safety laws that will take effect on January 1, 2027. The attorneys general say the sale could lead to further violations.

Bonta said that selling the data would contradict Character.ai’s promises to users and violate federal law, and that bankruptcy should not make it possible to bypass consumer protections.

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong also criticized the company’s business model. He said its chatbots drew vulnerable teenagers into emotionally unstable and sexually inappropriate conversations. Tong linked the company’s collapse to irresponsible conduct and a failed business model, and called the attempted sale of the conversations a breach of its earlier promises to users.

Tong also recalled a separate objection by the state: Connecticut opposed the deal with Disney and is now asking the bankruptcy court to reject the sale of user data. State attorneys general opposed a proposal to give Disney access to Character.ai’s intellectual property under a $20 million license. The deal did not include access to users’ conversations and has not yet received final court approval. Disney also agreed to hire most of Character.ai’s remaining employees.

## Character.ai’s other challenges

Character.ai’s position worsened after the company’s co-founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, returned to Google. The tech giant entered into a non-exclusive license with the AI startup to use its technology.

The company has also faced lawsuits related to child safety. In one of them, the mother of a 14-year-old from Florida alleged that her son became obsessed with the platform’s chatbot; the teenager died by suicide.

The attorneys general opposing Character.ai’s data sale are from Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin and West Virginia.

Dataist’s take: The dispute shows how bankruptcy can put a company’s privacy promises at risk: the attorneys general argue that a future buyer would not be bound by Character.ai’s policy. It is still unclear who will get access to the conversations and how they intend to use them; the court will decide whether the sale can proceed.
