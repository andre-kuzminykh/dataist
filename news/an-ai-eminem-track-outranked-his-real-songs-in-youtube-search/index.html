An AI Eminem track outranked his real songs in YouTube search

@neuronium_ai

A nearly 12-minute track credited to Marshall "Eminem" Mathers, generated with AI and uploaded without his permission, climbed YouTube's search results for the query "Eminem" and ranked above the rapper's actual songs — "Superman", "Shake That" and the real "I'm Back", which is 26 years old. It passed well over 100,000 views in its first week. The video's title says nothing about being unofficial, and YouTube has not applied its AI label to it. Commenters congratulated Eminem on the comeback.

It passed well over 100,000 views in its first week. The video's title says nothing about being unofficial, and YouTube has not applied its AI label to it. Commenters congratulated Eminem on the comeback.

The imitation is not good. The beat alone is cheap enough to give the game away. The voice and delivery are a distant resemblance to Eminem, and the vocal has the plastic sheen that marks a generated take rather than a recorded one. Generation tools have improved enough to get within range of a famous voice, but this track did not need to clear that bar. It cleared YouTube's ranking bar instead, which turns out to be lower.

In the lyrics, the generated Eminem announces that he is back and is a new version of himself. Another line ties the Slim Shady persona to the old smirk, a notepad and a pen. That is roughly the level of the writing across the whole run time.

Listeners took it for a genuine release anyway. One commenter wrote that the song is great and that Eminem is unquestionably the best. Another congratulated the rapper, said he was proud of him and told him not to give up.

The disclosure exists, but it is placed where nobody reads and worded so that nobody would understand it. The description calls the upload a fan-made music video inspired by Eminem, Slim Shady and legendary Detroit hip-hop, and says the project is for entertainment only. That phrasing describes a tribute edit, not a synthetic vocal performance sold under the artist's name.

The account also pre-listed an email address for copyright questions and takedown requests. That is not the behaviour of someone who stumbled into this. Searching the address turns it up on a number of other YouTube accounts, many of them posing as pages for other rappers, including Meek Mill; the associated account has more than 13,000 subscribers. This looks less like a fan project than a small catalogue business built on other people's names.

YouTube has tagged some similar tracks with its "AI" marker, which indicates that the audio or visuals were altered or fully generated. It did not tag this one. The label exists; the enforcement is the part that does not scale.

Futurism reached the account owner by email. The uploader, using the name Milana Salaeva, said she never tried to pass "I'm Back" off as a real new Eminem release, that the description makes clear the material is unofficial, and that elements generated by or created with AI may have been used in it. She said she works as a music producer, has been making music for a long time, writes her own beats and handles arrangement, mixing, mastering and other stages of production, and that AI is only one tool she sometimes uses. The hedge is the interesting word there: a producer who did the arrangement and the mastering herself would know whether AI was used, not merely that it may have been.

The more useful question is what the payoff is. Earlier this year a man pleaded guilty to uploading generated music to Spotify and inflating its play counts with an army of bots, collecting $8 million in royalties. Salaeva is almost certainly nowhere near that figure, particularly after YouTube substantially changed how it counts views. Which means this upload is either a rounding error in someone's income or a test of a distribution channel that is cheap enough to run at volume across a dozen impersonated accounts.

Universal Music, which owns Eminem's label Shady Records, sits on both sides of this. Artists and labels have spent years trying to stop tracks that use their voices and likenesses without permission, many of which go viral before anything can be done. Universal is simultaneously signing deals with AI companies, including the chipmaker Nvidia and the music platform Udio. Universal did not respond to a request for comment, and Futurism said it would update its story if that changes. On a fake of its own flagship artist outranking his catalogue, silence is itself a position.

Every remedy in this dispute — the takedown notice, the label deal, the lawsuit — points at whoever uploaded the file. None of them points at the ranking. A search engine owned by the largest music distributor on the internet decided that a generated 12-minute impression was the most relevant result for an artist's own name, and that decision is a product setting, not an accident of the algorithm. The fakes are getting better, but they did not have to.
