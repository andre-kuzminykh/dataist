According to a study by Gallup and the Digital Wellness Lab at Boston Children’s Hospital, 33% of Generation Z rely on AI as someone to talk to. Among those who use AI every day, the figure rises to 50%. The researchers examined how young people build close relationships in person and online, and how these connections relate to their well-being. Participants rated in-person friendships higher for strength and support. At the same time, young people who have only online friends or an AI companion may be more likely to feel lonely.

# More than a third of young people in Generation Z use AI for companionship

## What relationships the researchers studied

The report “Friendships in the Online and Offline Worlds: Digital Life, Connection and Well-Being” compares three types of close relationships: friendships between people who meet in person; relationships that began in real life and continued online; and friendships that exist only on the internet, where the young people have never met in person.

The survey was conducted from May to August. It included 15,925 people aged 15 to 22 from 16 countries. The findings show that in-person relationships benefit Generation Z more than digital ones. At the same time, nearly half of young people in the US have at least one close friend they communicate with only online.

The study looked beyond screen time to examine whom young people talk to and what role those relationships play in their social lives and mental health. In-person friendships received the highest ratings for strength and support, and were more often associated with positive emotional experiences. By contrast, relying on friends who exist only online or on an AI companion may be linked to a higher likelihood of loneliness.

David Bickham, research director at the Digital Wellness Lab, says teenagers seek out different ways to connect, as is typical during adolescence. Turning to AI does not necessarily indicate a serious lack of social connection, he says: young people are growing up in the age of AI, using it to find information and increasingly encountering its social side.

AI may partly make up for a lack of social connection, but it does not yet replace in-person relationships. The report’s authors note that in-person friendships and communication through digital platforms coexist in young people’s social lives.

## Where young people find online friends

In the US, Instagram and Discord were the platforms most often used to communicate with friends the young people had never met in person. TikTok was not among the leading platforms. Bickham added that online relationships do not always help people address loneliness itself.

Young people who feel lonely look for friends online and talk to strangers, Bickham says. That carries risks, but it is also one way people meet. The report notes that boys were more likely than girls to make friends online and often met new people through games. Girls were more likely to name social media as their main way of maintaining relationships.

Bickham also said that boys with higher levels of social anxiety were less likely to make new acquaintances and more likely to do things alone. Yet among young people who still felt lonely, the researchers found a tendency to seek out conversation with chatbots.

## Why screen time alone is not enough

Debate about the effect of screens on teenagers’ mental health has focused on whether digital devices are causing a crisis or whether concerns are exaggerated. Critics of this approach argue that measuring screen time does not take into account what young people are watching.

This year, bans on social media and age restrictions for minors took effect in Australia and the UK. In the US, state lawmakers proposed bills targeting features designed to encourage addictive use. According to a recent Pew Research survey, most Americans support banning children under 16 from using social media.

Last year, John-Paak Allam, an associate professor at Rutgers University’s School of Public Health, wrote in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine that research relies largely on crude measures of screen time. In his view, this approach treats all internet content as equivalent. Allam called it inadequate and urged researchers to examine what children watch, not just how much time they spend online.

Bickham and his team also decided to go beyond measuring screen time and study the nature of the relationships young people form online: how they spend that time and whom they communicate with. Bickham says screen time is difficult to measure accurately in surveys because about half of participants said they were online constantly. What matters more, he believes, is the quality of relationships: outcomes depend on how someone uses platforms, not just how long they use them.

An earlier report from the Digital Wellness Lab examined AI use among teenagers in the US, Brazil and France. It found that young people aged 13 to 17 turn to AI for personal advice, creative help and companionship.

## What may change

The researchers do not consider the findings proof of a lasting shift in how young people build relationships. However, they see them as a sign of growing reliance on AI companions amid the broader spread of digital communication among Generation Z.

Andrew Dugan, Gallup’s chief researcher, suggested that AI conversations may become more common over the next five years as AI becomes integrated into young people’s everyday lives. For now, it remains an open question whether they turn to AI because they are lonely, or whether this kind of interaction can intensify loneliness.

<strong>Dataist’s take:</strong> The study shows why device use should not be considered separately from the nature of communication. Its findings point to a link between digital relationships and loneliness, but do not show which causes which: loneliness may lead young people to turn to AI, or interacting with AI may intensify loneliness.
