## The timing is the story

Paterson does not consider the incident excessively serious. He said the data accessed by the AI agent had been open to researchers and specialists studying the healthcare system, and was not personal or confidential information.

The unanswered part is the chronology:

- When did the government learn about the breach?
- Why was the announcement made in New York?
- Why was it disclosed in that format and at that time?

Those questions come from Paterson, not from a finding that the disclosure was coordinated for political effect. His point is that Australians are entitled to an explanation and an admission of responsibility.

## A warning without panic

Paterson described the incident as a reason to “stay alert, but not panic.” He also said Australians should expect a high level of confidence when entering agreements with companies such as OpenAI.

I think that is the more useful frame than treating the episode as either a major privacy crisis or a non-event. If the data was already available to researchers and did not contain personal or confidential information, the immediate harm may be limited. But the disclosure still raises a governance question: can the government account clearly for what it knew, when it knew it and why it chose that public moment?

The episode leaves Australia with a narrow breach to explain and a broader standard to meet when it works with AI companies.
