## Where the losses are concentrated

The Bureau of Labor Statistics remains one of the most widely used and comprehensive sources for employment data, despite repeated challenges to its credibility since Donald Trump returned to the White House. Analysts continue to rely on its figures to make sense of a labor market that has grown increasingly bleak.

Digital art, including graphic design, was among the hardest-hit areas. Broadcasting, publishing, film and sound recording also lost jobs. The picture was not uniformly negative: employment rose sharply in some in-person arts fields, including museums and performing arts. But opportunities for independent artists fell substantially compared with previous years.

Politano describes this as one of the most severe periods for U.S. media employment in modern history. Comparable losses in both scale and duration occurred only during the major economic downturns of 2001 and 2008.

## A weak market, with AI in the mix

Employment across media and creative industries has been declining since 2023. Generative AI, for all its current shortcomings, gives organizations an accessible alternative to human labor in design, video, writing and audio.

Permanent work in these fields was already difficult to find. A weaker overall labor market has made the situation harder, and the losses show no clear sign of stopping soon.

The figures show where jobs disappeared, not how much of that decline AI caused. <strong>I think that distinction matters:</strong> AI is entering a sector already under pressure, so the announcement leaves open how much of the damage comes from automation and how much from a broader downturn. What I’d want to know is whether the same fields that are losing jobs are also replacing workers’ output with AI—or simply hiring less.
