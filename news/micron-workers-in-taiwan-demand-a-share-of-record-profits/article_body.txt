# Micron workers in Taiwan demand a share of record profits

Semiconductors are essential to AI, robotics, data processing and other important technologies. Because these industries cannot develop without chips, factory workers have more leverage to push for better working conditions. The dispute at Micron, a US memory-chip maker headquartered in Idaho with factories in Taiwan, shows how that is changing relations between employees and management.

## What the unions want

Micron workers in Taiwan are represented by two unions: Taoyuan and Taichung. Both are seeking higher wages, quarterly bonuses and increased contributions to employees’ pension accounts across the company’s operations on the island.

Taoyuan union president Jerry Lin told Rest of World that workers do not object to the company making a profit, but want their share of it. He said the current division of income between senior executives and rank-and-file employees is unfair.

## Workers reject the agreement

The Taoyuan union backed the employees’ tough demands, while workers represented by the Taichung union were unhappy with how their interests were represented.

On October 22, Micron announced that it had reached an agreement with Taichung union representatives and accepted their demands — at least on paper. Taiwan’s Central News Agency reported that the agreement was criticized for a lack of transparency and for not sharing profits with workers. That was what rank-and-file employees had been seeking.

Union members were also dissatisfied. About 2,300 rank-and-file employees at semiconductor facilities represented by the Taoyuan union overwhelmingly rejected the agreement. They retained the right to strike in the future. One employee, who asked to remain anonymous, told Rest of World that workers did not consider any of their demands met and were surprised that Taichung union representatives had signed the agreement.

## Protests are becoming more common

Wu Yu-jen, a labor relations professor at Taiwan’s National Chung Cheng University, said the rise in protest activity among local semiconductor workers is partly linked to their seeing employees abroad take action, including workers at South Korea’s SK Hynix.

Former Taiwan labor minister Pan Shih-wei urged high-tech companies to pay closer attention to labor relations. He predicted that as AI chips become more important to the global economy, discussions about strikes and profit-sharing will become more frequent.

Dataist’s take: Micron’s record profits and the rejected agreement have made the distribution of income central to the dispute. Workers have not announced a new strike; they have only kept that option open.
