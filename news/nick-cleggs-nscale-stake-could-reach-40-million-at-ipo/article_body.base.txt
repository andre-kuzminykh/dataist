## Clegg’s stake and the IPO math

Clegg, Meta’s former head of global affairs, owns 917,000 Nscale shares and sits on the company’s board alongside former Meta executive Sheryl Sandberg.

At Nscale’s latest funding valuation of $14.6 billion, Clegg’s stake was worth about $18 million. If the company reaches the $35 billion valuation reported by the Financial Times this month, that holding could be worth about $40 million.

Sandberg owns 0.25% of Nscale. That stake would be worth $88 million at the same valuation, compared with roughly $37 million at the last funding round. Forbes estimates Sandberg’s net worth at $2.4 billion, while Clegg previously received about £24 million from selling Meta shares.

Both directors receive $60,000 a year. The Telegraph was the first to report on the potential increase in Clegg’s wealth following an Nscale listing.

## A costly route to US capital

Nscale submitted its US listing documents on September 18. Trading in New York could begin by the end of the year, giving the company a route to international expansion and what it describes as more accessible US financing for expensive AI infrastructure projects.

The numbers make the financing need plain:

- Revenue for the six months ending June 30: $140 million
- Loss for the same period: $1 billion
- Latest funding valuation: $14.6 billion
- Possible IPO valuation: $35 billion

Nscale attributed the loss in its listing documents to “significant investments” in a business requiring “very large capital expenditures.”

That gap between revenue and loss is the central fact behind the listing. My guess is that the IPO story is less about Clegg’s windfall than about whether public-market investors will keep funding infrastructure before the revenue catches up.

## The sovereign AI pitch

Chief executive Josh Payne says Nscale sees significant opportunity in sovereign AI: countries building their own models and infrastructure to reduce dependence on technology from the US and China.

European Central Bank president Christine Lagarde and former European competition commissioner Margrethe Vestager have both called for Europe to develop more of its own AI infrastructure. Nscale is positioning itself as a trusted platform for that demand, particularly in Europe, where the company says the need is high and its British jurisdiction makes it a natural partner.

The company operates five data centers in Europe. Another 12 sites are at different stages of construction, including projects in the US. The Guardian reported this year that one Nscale project in Luton, Essex, is planned for a former scrap-metal storage site.

What the announcement is quiet about is how sovereign-AI demand becomes durable revenue rather than another reason to build ahead of customers. A $35 billion valuation would reward Nscale for the infrastructure it is preparing, but the $1 billion loss shows how much of that bet is still being made in advance.
