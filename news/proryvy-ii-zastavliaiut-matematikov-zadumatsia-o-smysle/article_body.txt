# AI Breakthroughs Are Forcing Mathematicians to Rethink the Value of Their Work

Recent mathematical breakthroughs by AI still look more like clever rearrangements of already-known ideas than the creation of genuinely new theory. But if that changes, mathematicians will have to answer what the purpose of their research is. This month, OpenAI’s Astra model solved a key problem in group theory—it proved the existence of non-sofic groups. Its proof is largely based on a small modification of the theorems of Gábor Kun and Andreas Thom. Dr Henry Bradford believes it would now be rash to rule out the emergence of superhuman abilities in every area of mathematical reasoning in the coming years.

Like many mathematicians, Bradford has spent much of the past few weeks thinking about the future of his discipline. Especially after a problem in his own field—group theory—was solved. Just a few months ago, the very idea of such an AI breakthrough would have seemed incredible to him.

The question now is what will happen to mathematics if AI begins creating genuinely new theories.

## Why Mathematics Matters

The purpose of mathematical research may consist of more than proving new theorems—AI may eventually be far more effective than humans at that. In his 1994 essay “On Proof and Progress in Mathematics,” the great American mathematician Bill Thurston wrote that mathematicians generally want not a collection of “answers,” but understanding.

In that case, mathematics exists above all as ideas living in the human mind. New theorems become signs of progress in understanding those ideas and communicating them to other people.

This is especially important in light of the development of AI: it will push society to discuss the value of mathematics created by humans. If AI can produce research papers faster and more cheaply than researchers, university administrations facing funding shortages may decide that mathematical staff are no longer needed.

From Thurston’s perspective, that would be a mistake. The value of mathematicians’ work lies not so much in the papers they write as in preserving and advancing mathematical knowledge among people.

## Conclusion

Whether mathematics survives in the age of AI or gradually disappears will depend not only on the technical capabilities of future machines. It is a collective decision for society: what, exactly, it considers valuable in human intelligence.

<strong>Dr Henry Bradford is a mathematics lecturer at Christ’s College, University of Cambridge.</strong>
