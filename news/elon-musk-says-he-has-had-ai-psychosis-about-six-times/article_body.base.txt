## A joke with a serious edge

Musk said the episodes happened at different points this year and referred to “AI or SI psychosis,” using SI to mean superintelligence. President Donald Trump recently signed an executive order renaming artificial intelligence “superintelligence.” The source’s author treats taking that label seriously while the technology remains visibly imperfect as a possible sign of distorted thinking.

“AI psychosis” is also used mockingly to describe intense AI enthusiasm among tech figures, particularly executives who accept sweeping claims about the technology without much skepticism. Musk is one of those believers, the source argues. Some in the industry meet the accusation with irony, as if daring people outside the hype to object.

But the joke does not always sound like a joke. Claire “Grimes” Boucher, the mother of Musk’s child, recently praised the feeling of an AI model “melting” her brain. In December, she wrote that “AI psychosis is more fun than no AI psychosis,” adding that she had experienced it and might still be experiencing it.

Another prominent example cited in the source is OpenAI investor Jeff Lewis, who has faced accusations of genuine, not “fun,” AI psychosis.

## Musk’s enthusiasm is the point

Musk has long been absorbed by AI. Shortly before his remarks, he said AI-generated music had impressed him. He rejected “slop-core,” a disparaging label for this kind of content, and proposed “cool-core” instead, saying the music was genuinely good.

He also said the music showed that AI had taste, and expressed surprise that it could produce work with good taste.

I think the tension here is less about whether Musk meant “psychosis” literally than about how easily the industry turns extravagant claims into a badge of identity. Musk’s music comments make that tension concrete: he is not just joking about AI fixation, but arguing that the technology already has a quality people might once have considered distinctly human.

What I’d want to know is whether these comments are only a performance of confidence, or whether the people making them can still distinguish a compelling demo from evidence of deeper capability. In a field where belief itself is part of the sales pitch, that distinction is getting harder to see.
