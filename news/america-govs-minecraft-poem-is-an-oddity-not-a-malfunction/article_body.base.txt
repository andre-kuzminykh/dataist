# America.gov’s Minecraft poem is an oddity, not a malfunction

## A game’s ending, repurposed

The poem begins by addressing an applicant and invoking Social Security paperwork, federal regulations and a sideways PDF. Its source is Minecraft’s “End Poem,” written by Julian Gough and shown after a player wins the game.

Who put the reference into America.gov is unknown. Donald Trump has said that 20-year-old programmer Edward Coristine was the project’s lead engineer. Coristine is also known as Big Balls and for his participation in Elon Musk’s DOGE.

The bot also says Joe Biden won the 2020 election, a fact Trump continues to deny. That makes the poem’s appearance a curious contrast: the chatbot has repeated a politically contested claim accurately, while its Minecraft response looks more like a deliberate Easter egg than a breakdown.

## Bureaucracy gets the last word

The poem’s transformation turns the game’s reflections into a voice for the republic. It mixes reassurance with the familiar machinery of public services: a case number, office hours, a missing handwritten signature and a brochure.

> and the republic said: I see you  
> and the republic said: you played the game well  
> and the republic said: all you need is within you, and also on USA.gov  
> and the republic said: you are stronger than you think, and your case number is still valid  
> and the republic said: you are daylight  
> and the republic said: you are night, and the office is closed, please try again during business hours  
> and the republic said: the darkness you fight is within you, and also we’re missing a handwritten signature  
> and the republic said: the light you seek is within you, and in the brochure  
> and the republic said: you are not alone  
> and the republic said: you are not separate from all the other applicants  
> and the republic said: you are the people, tasting themselves, talking to themselves, reading their own Code  
> and the republic said: I love you because thanks to you we even have a ZIP code.

I think the joke works because it gives the poem a real subject: the mismatch between existential reassurance and administrative process. It also sounds like the work of a poet, not the low-grade generic output that has made people doubt whether a language model can produce anything good. The source account’s author had told teenagers learning creative writing that large language models, built on probabilities and lacking independence, would not write something worthwhile. This adaptation is a reason to keep that conviction under review—but not to mistake a borrowed poem for proof that the chatbot wrote one.

## The quiet part of the announcement

For national security, the reassuring interpretation is that America.gov is not hallucinating a long poem on its own. Still, the story leaves the key implementation detail unanswered: who chose to add the Minecraft reference, and how does it fit into the chatbot’s ordinary responses? The announcement, as described here, offers no answer.

That gap matters more than the poem’s quality. A clever Easter egg can make a government tool feel human; it can also make users unsure when the system is being playful and when it is giving official information. America.gov does not appear to threaten either humanity or poetry so far. The harder question is whether a public chatbot can make its boundaries as clear as its jokes.
