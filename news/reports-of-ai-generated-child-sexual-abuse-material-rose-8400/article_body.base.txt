# Reports of AI-related child sexual abuse rose 8,400%

## What the data show

Researchers examined reports of material that technology companies submitted to the US National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. The study covers East Asia and the Pacific, Latin America and the Caribbean, and North America; previous studies examined Western Europe.

Debi Fry, Childlight’s global director of data, said the findings show the scale of child sexual exploitation and how quickly threats are changing, especially online. Combining survey data with information from police and other sources, she said, can help assess risks more accurately, identify gaps in protection systems, and direct resources and support where they are most needed.

The study also examined reports of sexual abuse offline. In North America, nearly one in three girls and more than one in ten boys had experienced such abuse during childhood. This includes sexual harassment, rape, and being filmed under clothing without consent.

Childlight estimates that more than 300 million children worldwide have experienced sexual exploitation or abuse involving technology at least once in their lives. The organisation estimates that about one in four children will experience at least one form of abuse before turning 18.

In the US, the number of registered reports and notifications of child sexual abuse material rose by 63% from 2024 to 2025. In Canada, the increase was 18%.

## More AI-generated material

Last month, the Internet Watch Foundation (IWF) reported that the volume of AI-generated child sexual abuse material online is growing: the number of illegal items it examined in 2026 has already surpassed the total for all of 2025.

In the first half of 2026, IWF analysts identified more photorealistic material than in the whole of the previous year. The UK-based non-governmental organisation, which tracks such material worldwide, said it had examined 6,310 AI-generated images that meet the legal definition of child sexual abuse material. That is 40% more than in all of last year, when more than 4,500 images were identified. IWF says the problem was also growing in 2025.

Fry called the pace at which AI-related threats to children are changing deeply concerning. She noted that some of the increase may be due to better detection and reporting, but said the findings show why governments and technology companies need to urgently study new forms of abuse, prevent them, and remove material faster.

## A call to governments

Paul Stanfield, Childlight’s executive director, said millions of children are subjected to sexual exploitation and abuse, yet governments often lack a complete picture of where the harm occurs and who is most at risk. Without understanding the scale and nature of the problem, he said, it is impossible to prevent abuse effectively and protect children.

Stanfield stressed that child sexual exploitation is not inevitable: the knowledge and tools needed to make a difference already exist. Childlight urges governments to recognise it as a preventable public health emergency, invest in collecting better data, and turn those data into concrete measures. The organisation also says consistent leadership and international cooperation are needed to prevent abuse and intervene earlier.

Dataist’s take: The sharp rise in reports may reflect both an increase in the amount of material and better detection; the study identifies both as possible factors. Childlight’s call is therefore not only about removing content, but also about building a fuller picture of the threats on which prevention and child protection can be based.
