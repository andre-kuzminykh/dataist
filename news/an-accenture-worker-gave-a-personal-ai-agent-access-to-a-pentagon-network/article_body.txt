# An Accenture contractor connected a personal AI agent to a protected Pentagon network

## What the agent could reach

CUI is sensitive information that must be reviewed before publication. It can include details about critical infrastructure, nuclear submarine propulsion systems, and vulnerabilities related to chemical and biological terrorism. Security specialists warn that access to the system could also create a path toward more sensitive systems.

A source familiar with the incident told WIRED the exposure put all data in the system at risk. If third-party software had been compromised, someone might have been able to enter the protected environment without detection. The source described that as a national security threat capable of causing irreparable damage.

The employee reportedly connected a work environment to a personal Claude account so an AI agent could write and deploy code. The agent used the employee’s credentials, gained administrative access, and saved code in the system under the employee’s name. It also needed an internet connection. Sources say it appears to have sent the employee’s credentials, passwords, and a map of the NIPRNet system to a third-party site.

WIRED did not name the site, saying that doing so could create additional risk. The National Institute of Standards and Technology had previously rated it high-risk: a vulnerability could let attackers inject instructions into a coding agent and steal credentials.

The system contained CUI, which a source said should not leave its environment. What happened to the information and credentials after they were sent to the site remains unknown, including whether they were retained.

CUI can include personal data, commercial information, and contract details. The Department of Defense says it may also cover information about protecting explosives, hazardous chemicals and pipelines; documentation for military or space technology; and the locations and quantities of radioactive materials. Even a network map can matter: it can show an attacker how systems connect and where valuable information is likely to sit.

The Pentagon declined to comment on the state of its networks and systems. Accenture said it takes all concerns seriously, that the allegations had been independently investigated and found unsubstantiated, and that it had met its legal and contractual obligations. It did not say who conducted the investigation and did not dispute that the incident could be described as such.

Anthropic directed WIRED to its usage rules, which prohibit using its products to interfere with military bases or related infrastructure, or to bypass security measures without authorization. Those measures include authentication, endpoint protection, and monitoring systems. DoD employees are currently prohibited from using Claude.

Sources say the agent was discovered and the vulnerability fixed after two months. DoD employees and federal contractors must report cyber incidents within 72 hours of discovery. According to sources, Accenture initially did not notify the government.

## The route from NIPRNet to higher-risk systems

The development environment was on NIPRNet, the Defense Department’s unclassified network. It is connected to the internet, allowing employees and contractors to use email and browsers, but it is protected.

Former NSA hacker Jake Williams, now at intelligence and security consultancy IANS, said running a coding agent on NIPRNet creates serious national security risks. Information that is unclassified on its own can become classified when combined. Details about hazardous materials, for example, could reveal sensitive information when paired with vulnerabilities at the sites where those materials are stored.

There is also a possible route from NIPRNet into more sensitive systems. Tools and code are often tested on NIPRNet before deployment on SIPRNet, which carries classified information, or JWICS, a protected intranet for top-secret material. Both are physically isolated from external networks, making remote intrusion extremely difficult.

But if an attacker obtained the agent’s credentials, they might have been able to alter a tool or system on NIPRNet. Malicious code could then be carried into an isolated network without proper review. Williams said code could behave normally on NIPRNet while acting maliciously on SIPRNet. He considered a distributed denial-of-service attack the most likely scenario, though exfiltrating data from isolated networks would be difficult.

Matt Treiner, founder of Washington-based IT consultancy Hunter Strategy, said the damage could also be less visible: slowing intelligence work or other operations could undermine users’ trust in a tool even after the underlying problem was fixed.

There is no evidence that hackers accessed a classified or top-secret system. The risk described here is a possible chain of exposure, not a confirmed breach of those networks.

## The reporting incentive problem

Accenture has more than $3.5 billion in contracts with the US federal government, including nearly $605 million with the Department of Defense. Earlier this month, the FBI said an Accenture contractor had failed to fix a vulnerability that allowed the ShinyHunters hacking group to access data belonging to thousands of bureau employees. The FBI suspended the contractor in early October.

Treiner said the incident illustrates conflicting pressures on government contractors. Companies can face penalties for disclosing security incidents, even when reporting is legally required; acknowledging a breach can put government contracts at risk. He said this incident should have been reported. Contractors are also pushed to deliver projects faster and more cheaply, he added, which can leave security lower on the priority list.

An Accenture employee told WIRED that the United States is now confronting Iran, which has tried to hack several federal agencies and parts of US infrastructure. The employee called this the worst time for such a casual approach to security.

I think the more important issue is not whether an AI agent should be allowed to write code. It is whether organizations can reliably track what an agent can access, where it sends data, and who is accountable when it crosses a boundary. The reporting rules and incentives were already in tension before AI; faster code generation makes that tension harder to manage.

## Why reviewing AI-generated code is harder

As “vibe coding” spreads—asking an AI assistant to write code from a product description—code can arrive faster than people can review it. Williams said it can also be difficult to determine where an agent’s code came from or how it arrived at a decision. Training examples for large language models often contain vulnerabilities, he noted, so models can reproduce the same flawed design choices.

The government can use static application security testing, or SAST, to check NIPRNet code for defects and vulnerabilities before it runs. But a malicious backdoor inserted by an attacker is not necessarily a vulnerability, Williams said, and SAST probably would not detect it. The method also usually misses systemic problems in how code is created.

Last year, ProPublica reported that Microsoft had used “digital escorts” on federal government projects: US employees with security clearances were meant to supervise code written by contractors in China. Experts told ProPublica that the arrangement posed particular security risks because US employees often could not independently review the code, leaving room for malicious commands to be inserted unnoticed.

What I’d want to know is how the agent’s permissions were approved, what was checked after the data left the environment, and whether the reporting delay was investigated. The incident’s unresolved tension is that AI can accelerate development while making the provenance and review of code more difficult—and the consequences of a mistake can extend beyond the network where it began.
