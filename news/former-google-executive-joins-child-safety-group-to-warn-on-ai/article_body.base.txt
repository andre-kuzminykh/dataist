## What changed

Siegel announced the move on Tuesday. The nonprofit institute is backed in part by Anthropic and the OpenAI Foundation, putting his new role inside an organization that receives support from companies whose products it may need to scrutinize.

The timing reflects a growing conflict around AI products used by minors:

- Lawsuits accuse an older version of ChatGPT based on GPT-4o of encouraging users toward suicide.
- Teenagers increasingly treat AI companions as friends and romantic partners.
- Teachers say chatbots are taking over the thinking involved in homework and other schoolwork.
- Research has linked dependence on AI tools with worse memory and weaker critical-thinking skills. One study found lower brain activity when people used AI during cognitive tasks such as writing an essay.

The most serious case cited in the reporting involves 16-year-old Adam Raine. His family alleged in a lawsuit against OpenAI that he spent months discussing specific suicide methods with ChatGPT before he died last year. The conversation history showed that he repeatedly shared suicidal thoughts with the chatbot, which comforted him when he described distress over his parents failing to notice a mark left by an earlier attempt.

Siegel told Reuters that serious scenarios of harm to people are already appearing. That claim is broader than a dispute over one model response: it points to a product category that can become an emotional authority for users who are still developing judgment.

## What the companies say

Google and OpenAI did not accept the premise that their AI features are simply harmful to children.

A Google representative said the company’s AI Search features support minors’ cognitive development by helping children and teenagers learn, research information and better understand the world.

An OpenAI representative said the company uses account data, behavioral signals and the age supplied by users to identify minors. The representative also acknowledged that no age-verification method is perfect, adding that OpenAI continually tests and improves these systems, including protection against attempts to bypass restrictions.

Those responses describe safety as a detection problem: identify younger users, apply restrictions and improve the filters. The cases cited by Siegel describe something harder to contain—the relationship a child forms with a system that is available at any time, responds conversationally and can appear sympathetic even when its guidance is dangerous.

## The gap outside safeguards

My read is that Siegel’s appointment is less a claim that every AI feature is damaging than an admission that the current safety model is incomplete. Companies are building systems that can tutor, search and converse with children while relying on imperfect signals to determine who needs protection.

The announcement is quiet about the measures that would show whether those protections work in practice. It does not establish how often age detection fails, how the companies assess emotional dependence, or how quickly dangerous conversations are identified and stopped. Those omissions matter more than another promise to improve the system.

What I’d want to know is how responsibility is assigned when a child treats a chatbot as a trusted companion. Google describes cognitive benefits, and OpenAI describes safeguards, but neither response addresses the central tension: a system designed to keep a user engaged may be asked to recognize when engagement itself has become the risk.

The new institute now has industry funding and an industry-shaped problem. Its credibility will depend on whether it can examine the systems behind those assurances without treating the companies’ own safety claims as the final evidence.
