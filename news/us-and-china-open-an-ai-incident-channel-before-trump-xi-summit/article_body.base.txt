## An incident channel, not an AI treaty

Bessent said the two leading AI powers need more transparency across borders instead of operating in near-total secrecy. The dialogue is intended to help the US and China define shared objectives and common threats. The delegations spent the day in negotiations at JPMorgan’s Manhattan headquarters.

The agreement follows warnings from AI industry leaders that people could lose control of the technology if research is not slowed. But the scope of the new channel is narrow in at least one important respect: US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said restrictions on advanced AI chip exports were not on the agenda.

That omission matters. The two countries are creating a way to discuss dangerous incidents while leaving one of the main sources of strategic tension outside the conversation. I think this makes the mechanism look more like a crisis-management tool than the beginning of a broad AI compact.

Alongside the AI dialogue, Greer said the countries were formalizing a trade council. Trump and Xi had discussed it at their meeting in Beijing in May. Bessent said the council was created there and AI discussions began at the same time; both tracks are now moving into working mode.

The announcement is quiet about the details that would determine whether the incident channel has practical value:

- What qualifies as an AI incident affecting national security
- How quickly either side must report one
- What happens if a government disputes the report or withholds it

Those details are supposed to be addressed at the next meeting. Until then, the mechanism is a promise to communicate, not a shared safety standard.

## A diplomatic channel under trade pressure

Trump will meet Xi at Andrews Air Force Base on Wednesday. The summit will take place in Washington on Thursday and include a state dinner. It will be the leaders’ first meeting on US soil since their talks in 2017.

The likely centerpiece is an extension of the trade truce agreed in October in Busan, South Korea. Washington suspended planned export restrictions against thousands of Chinese companies, while Beijing delayed restrictions on rare-earth metals.

The two sides still disagree over how long that truce should last. According to the Financial Times, China sought an extension through the end of Trump’s presidential term, while the US wanted to limit it to six months. Washington is concerned that Beijing is not meeting its commitments on rare-earth supplies and is also restricting exports to Japan. Those materials are similarly important to US supply chains.

US officials had previously entertained a deal to slow AI research in both countries. Trump rejected that idea because he does not want the US to fall behind China in the technology race. That position sets a clear limit on the new dialogue: cooperation may be acceptable when it reduces the risk of an incident, but not when it slows the competition itself.
