Meta employees told The Guardian that the company’s attempt to replace moderators with AI systems while also cutting staff led to problems across its platforms. Automated systems often miss nuances, especially in posts in languages other than English, and remove content that does not violate the rules. Remaining employees have to work in smaller teams: some have only two-thirds or even half of their former staff. Users affected by mistaken moderation have found it harder to get decisions reviewed. Meta disputes the criticism: according to the company, in the latest tests its new systems made 13% fewer mistakes than people and found 10% more violations.

# Staff cuts and the shift to AI have made Meta’s work harder

## Smaller teams and automation errors

Employees describe the shift to automated moderation as chaotic. One person who spoke to The Guardian said they tried to contact several teams to discuss a problem, only to find that those teams had been disbanded entirely.

The moderators who remain have to monitor content across Meta’s enormous platforms with fewer staff. Employees say some teams now have only two-thirds or half as many workers as before.

AI systems often struggle with subtle differences in the meaning of posts. The problem is especially pronounced with content in languages other than English: posts that should not be removed are often blocked. People who believe a decision was mistaken have found it harder to reach an employee who can look into the matter, even if the user knows someone at Meta.

One employee linked this to the reduced number of reviewers: human judgment, they said, is needed for decisions that depend on context and the rules. Employees also say that ambiguous cases are now harder to resolve.

## Meta’s position and past practices

Meta rejected the claim that its moderation system is malfunctioning. The company told The Guardian that the same rules apply to all users, and that allegations that decisions depend on political views or public activism are unfounded.

Meta added that it has long used AI to enforce its community standards, and began testing more advanced systems earlier this year. According to the company, the latest tests showed that, on average, the systems made 13% fewer mistakes than people and detected 10% more actual violations. Users can appeal a decision if they believe it is wrong.

Meta previously cut costs by outsourcing moderation to contractors in Kenya. The company’s long-term partner in Africa, SAMA, gained a reputation as a “sweatshop” because of its treatment of low-paid employees tasked with removing especially violent content from the platforms.

Previous Futurism reports said Meta fails to remove some material involving violence against children, even when users report it directly. This includes AI-generated videos depicting physical violence against children, as well as sexualized violence against girls and young women.

Dataist’s take: The dispute at Meta shows that automated systems’ test results can diverge from users’ experiences: the company reports fewer errors, while employees and Futurism reports point to problems with context and handling complaints. The cuts to human teams make it especially important to ask how accessible reviews of disputed decisions are.
