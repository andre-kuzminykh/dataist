# Priest: AI’s search for a soul distracts Silicon Valley from people

## What it means to be human

Last month, religious leaders from across San Francisco met behind closed doors to discuss what it means to be human in the age of artificial intelligence.

Anthropic, whose offices are just a few kilometers from Kevin Deal’s parish, invites priests, rabbis and theologians to take part in discussions. The company’s billionaire co-founder, Christopher Ola, even urged Vatican advisers to take seriously the possibility that an Anthropic chatbot is conscious.

Deal welcomes serious discussion of the moral consequences of AI development, including from a theological perspective. But in his view, attempts to determine whether a machine can have consciousness or a soul show how badly human nature is misunderstood. The human soul, mind and body are far more than a collection of tasks.

People make mistakes, sometimes catastrophic ones, and may try to change course—or stubbornly continue insisting they are right. They express themselves, form connections, build families, pursue goals and change through encounters with others. People make promises and break them, confront emptiness and try to fill it, fail, suppress their anxiety and go about everyday tasks as honestly as they can.

From childhood, those close to us support and protect us. We feel beauty and accept mysteries we cannot solve. Human beings do not exist only to be useful.

Even if every connection in the brain could be reproduced and an ideal AI configuration created to replicate the central nervous system, Deal believes, that system still would not receive a divine spark.

## Machines and the economics of usefulness

AI is built for practical applications and commercial gain. Its development requires enormous investments in capital and infrastructure, as well as knowledge accumulated by humanity. It is an economic choice shaped by expectations of profit, hopes for a better future and sociopolitical fears. If a model does not deliver enough return, it is abandoned.

AI creators may sincerely hope their technologies will improve the world. But those technologies are developed within an economic system that increasingly measures everything by productivity. Against this backdrop, Silicon Valley’s interest in the spirituality of machines seems especially strange to Deal.

Company founders approach religious leaders with questions: can their creation have consciousness, how can it be taught to be good, and should models be taught to confess their transgressions? Yet for thousands of years, religious traditions have taught that goodness cannot be reduced to a set of actions. Nor can the human soul, magnificent and unfathomable, be reproduced by a machine.

As Silicon Valley tries to determine how human its products are, Deal argues, it is increasingly treating real people as instruments of production.

## Confession and the responsibility of creators

Anthropic researchers reportedly considered teaching models to confess. But in Christianity, confession means more than admitting a mistake or expressing regret. It requires recognizing the harm caused, asking forgiveness and seeking to change one’s life.

Deal suggests that technology company founders start by asking what they themselves should repent of, given the technologies they are releasing into the world.

In response to the idea of a person as a productive unit, the priest proposes turning to ancient religious practices. He urges tech billionaires to take part in the sacraments, attend Mass, confess and pray every day. He also recommends reading Scripture, seeking spiritual mentors, not shying away from doubts about faith, making friends and forgiving old ones.

If people stop striving for efficiency and strive for goodness instead—that is, to be human beings who reflect God’s light—they may, Deal writes, find joy in the depths of their souls. The sacraments, prayers, the beauty and mystery of the liturgy, and the shared life of believers reveal a vision of humanity that resists the demand to be useful. Believers receive the body of Christ in communion and try to become more like him; they turn toward a world where justice and mercy meet, and where renewal and forgiveness remain possible.

When he leaves the church, Deal suggests bringing these principles into offices and boardrooms: assess how decisions affect poor people, workers whose lives are changed by technology, and neighbors who will never share in company leaders’ wealth. Christian faith, he emphasizes, cannot be separated from demands on everyday life: people cannot claim to embrace its wisdom while rejecting the responsibilities that come with it.

A person’s worth is not determined by how much they produce, and the human mind was not made to generate profit. Deal says people are called to love God and their neighbors.

Kevin Deal is a priest at St. John the Divine Episcopal Church in San Francisco.
