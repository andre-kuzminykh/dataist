Australia is fast-tracking a bill through parliament that would expand the definition of “defence member” to include contractors and allow the minister to exempt them from Australian laws. The same bill would give the defence minister the power to declare military and civilian infrastructure sites — including data centres — military zones. This could restrict access to those sites, public scrutiny and protests against their construction.

# Australian bill could give AI companies special legal status

## Powers for contractors

In 2019, The New York Times called Australia “the world’s most secretive democracy.” Columnist Tom Sulston argues that, since then, successive governments have passed defence, counterterrorism and surveillance laws that have weakened parliamentary oversight in favour of executive power.

A new bill to fast-track defence capabilities and protect bases is being pushed through parliament with little public attention. It would allow the minister to exempt defence members from the requirements of Australian law and extend the definition of “defence member” to contractors working for the Department of Defence.

Equality before the law is a fundamental principle of human rights and of Australian ideas about fairness and mutual support. The author acknowledges that special legal conditions may be justified for military personnel: soldiers, for example, are allowed to carry automatic weapons, which are prohibited for everyone else. But the new wording could extend such privileges to commercial contractors, including employees of private military companies. In that case, the minister could allow them to operate in Australia without being held accountable under ordinary laws.

## Technology companies and defence

US technology companies are expanding their work with defence agencies. Contractors include AI developers OpenAI and Anthropic, cloud providers Google and Microsoft, and Palantir, which specialises in defence data. The author considers this particularly troubling because these companies operate over the internet, infrastructure on which civilian life in Australia depends.

Last week, the government announced that OpenAI would not face criminal charges over a recent data leak involving the Medicare program. Although officials had said criminal law might be changed, the author sees the decision as a willingness to overlook AI companies’ violations in the pursuit of productivity growth.

If the bill passes, the defence minister could, at their discretion, designate employees of these companies as “defence members” and exempt them in advance from the requirements of Australian law. At the same time, national security restrictions on access to information would make public and parliamentary scrutiny more difficult, while whistleblowers would face severe penalties.

## Data centres as military zones

AI companies need sites to host agents capable of hacking websites. The bill would let the defence minister declare both military and civilian sites “defence security zones.” Public access and transparency around their operations could be restricted in these zones.

The author points to the US listening station at Pine Gap as an example: Australians are prohibited from walking around its perimeter and taking photographs. The author considers such restrictions understandable for a military site, but notes that AI companies now also develop military products. When Anthropic released its “Fable” model, the US government treated it as military equipment and banned foreign nationals from using it.

AI companies are building data centres in Australian suburbs. Local residents fear that these sites will consume electricity, collect their data and take away jobs. They are also concerned that a commercial site could become something like Pine Gap and a military target. Resistance to data centre construction is already growing across the country.

The bill would let the federal government restrict protests and acts of civil disobedience against such sites by declaring them defence security zones. Attempts to obstruct their operations could bring lengthy prison sentences. The author links this protection for businesses to the use of AI to create fake nude images and target missile strikes in Gaza.

## Legal protections for civilians

The author writes that the internet, once seen as a public space, is becoming a site of military operations involving technology companies. In the author’s view, instead of giving them special conditions for causing harm, the government should strengthen legal protections for civilians.

Australia has no human rights law, which the author considers an urgent need. Without legally recognised human rights backed by enforceable mechanisms, the author argues, citizens are left unprotected against the interests of corporate AI and the government.

Dataist’s take: The bill links expanded defence powers to the activities and infrastructure of commercial technology companies. The main constraints on public scrutiny are the secrecy provisions and penalties for disclosing information set out in the bill.
