AI is straining the US public-records system

Dataist

American public-records systems are struggling under a growing volume of requests: AI helps companies gather data to sell to the government and helps citizens draft requests faster. In tiny Panton, Vermont, procurement requests that once came in once a year now arrive monthly or more often. Requests and appeals are rising nationwide, while many agencies are not getting more staff or budget. In response, states are considering restrictions that could make records harder to access not only for companies but also for journalists, researchers and lawyers.

How public records became a commodity

In Panton, a town of 660 people, town clerk Kyle Row’s duties included the budget, vendor payments, property-tax disputes, zoning and organizing elections. During office hours at the town hall, built as a church in 1858, he also handled public-records requests.

SmartProcure used to request the town’s full procurement records once a year, covering everything from road salt to computers and toilet paper. Last year, the requests began arriving quarterly. Then other companies started asking for narrower records, such as purchases of emergency signal flares. Those requests began coming in monthly or more often. Row, who recently moved to a job in a neighboring town, said he was willing to respond to citizens, but gathering data for commercial purposes takes taxpayers’ time and resembles unpaid research into their political preferences.

Similar pressures are emerging across the country. Data brokers have been collecting information from public records for decades, but investor interest has intensified with the rise of AI. Over the past few years, venture-backed startups have raised more than $100 million to gather data from thousands of agencies and produce commercial “signals” for companies selling goods and services to the government.

AI has also made it easier for individuals to prepare requests. In many places, the number of requests has grown by double digits each year in recent years. But AI-assisted requests are often longer and broader in scope, while agency headcounts and budgets do not always grow. Staff sometimes struggle to tell whether a request came from a citizen, a chatbot or a company gathering potential customers. Some states are responding with laws that make requests easier to reject.

The 1966 US Freedom of Information Act established the public’s right to know what the government does. Every state now has its own public-records access law. Transparency has helped improve drinking water and schools, as well as lower taxes. But responses can take months, and the rules vary among states, counties and cities.

This complex system has long favored companies with the time and resources to navigate its procedures. Margaret Kwoka, a law professor at Ohio State University who served on the Freedom of Information Act Advisory Committee, described the trend in her 2016 study, “FOIA, Inc.” According to her research, commercial requesters — regulated companies, government contractors and information resellers — submitted 79% to 96% of requests to federal agencies, including the Environmental Protection Agency and the Food and Drug Administration. Kwoka says a small market for reselling records has become a profitable industry built on complex, aggregated databases.

SmartProcure, which operates as GovSpend, began developing this model 15 years ago by standardizing the collection of government procurement data. GovSpend’s chief operating officer, Trip Colden, says the company uses staff with longstanding agency relationships to handle requests and offers government institutions discounted prices.

New AI startups promise to make selling to the government faster and fairer:

Starbridge has raised $52 million, including a Series A round led by Craft Ventures, the fund of David Sacks, a former White House adviser on AI and cryptocurrency. The company is building a system it compares to a “Bloomberg Terminal for public-sector data.” It says it wants to give government leaders better technology and uses public records to match agencies with vendors.
Pursuit, whose investors include Bill Gurley and Jack Altman, has raised $25.5 million. The company helps businesses identify likely government buyers by analyzing meeting minutes, budgets and data from its own “industrial-scale records-request operation.” CEO and co-founder Mike Vecchietti says the data also benefits the public: spending is rising faster than revenue in many cities, and competition, he says, helps save taxpayers money.

Large companies that have been in the field for years have teams dedicated to working with agencies. Several county clerks and lawyers say new startups often send large numbers of requests to gather archived data faster. PinPoint Analytics, which analyzes data on construction tenders, sent as many as 2,000 requests a day while building its database; the number is now about 500, the company’s chief operating officer, Mark Zurada, said. SmartProcure and Pursuit say on their websites that each sends about 180,000 requests a year. NationGraph, which works with mid-sized vendors such as cleaning companies, says it submits about 10,000 requests a day on behalf of clients. The company did not respond to a request for comment on these figures.

Companies usually draft requests from templates rather than with AI. But AI helps standardize the tables, PDFs and emailed documents they receive, and then analyze them. The results become proprietary commercial “signals” and benchmarks that clients can use to set prices.

In cities and counties where requests are far more numerous than in small towns, handling them has become a job in its own right. Chautauqua County, New York, hired a paralegal this summer after the number of requests doubled. The county attorney linked the increase both to the use of ChatGPT to draft requests and to some requesters collecting records for resale.

In Ann Arbor, Michigan, senior deputy clerk Sarah Alanis counted 581 requests in 2021 and 937 last year. She says a high volume of requests has become the new normal. A growing share of requesters collect data at scale: they may seek purchasing records from vendors with little connection to one another or from companies the city has never worked with. Alanis used to spend a few hours a week processing requests; now it often takes half a day.

AI makes requests harder to process

The rise in commercial requests is only part of the picture: AI tools let anyone submit a request faster. Last year, federal agencies received a record 1.7 million requests. The New York Times linked the increase in part to AI, mass requests from the Heritage Foundation and a growing number of immigrants seeking their own records for legal defense.

David Cuillier, director of the Brechner Freedom of Information Project at the University of Florida, used an AI detector to examine the US Department of Education’s request log for July. He estimated that at least 29% of requests had been written at least partly with AI. There were almost no such requests before 2025. AI-written requests were twice as long as others, and 83% sought lengthy lists of records.

In Pennsylvania, the number of requests and appeals is “breaking records every month,” according to Liz Wagenseller, executive director of the Office of Open Records. Earlier spikes came during the COVID-19 pandemic, the fight over the 2020 election and disputes over school curricula. The current increase, Wagenseller says, is not tied to one high-profile issue. Appeals rose from about 3,000 in 2021 to just under 4,000 in 2025; about 6,000 are expected this year.

An increase in requests can signal active civic engagement, but it adds pressure to an already vulnerable system. Requests are often poorly drafted. Wagenseller says chatbots typically encourage people to appeal a denial, even when there is little chance of success. Requests that once fit in a paragraph now run to several pages. Some contain fabricated legal citations, and in one case a user even included a prompt for Claude in the request.

Agencies with limited resources are also using technology. One-fifth of federal agencies reported using AI or machine learning to find records. According to the Washington Post, the Department of Homeland Security uses AI to speed up document redactions before publication. Transparency advocates worry this will reinforce agencies’ tendency to withhold more information than the law requires.

Local governments are moving requests from email to centralized portals. Software provider CivicPlus says its NextRequest platform handles more than a million requests a year and uses CAPTCHAs to identify automated bots. According to the company’s chief marketing officer, Jane Snyder, this stops automated agents but cannot determine whether AI helped a person draft a request.

As workloads grow, lawmakers are proposing new restrictions. CivicPlus counted more than 20 bills at the state and federal levels under consideration this year; some give agencies new grounds to deny requests. A California law signed in September says AI systems are not “members of the public,” although people may use AI at the ordinary volume and frequency of a human.

A Pennsylvania bill whose consideration has been suspended would allow agencies to deny requests if they have “reasonable suspicion” that a request was written using a script or AI. Wagenseller says that is the wrong approach: the choice, she says, is between funding agencies to handle the workload and restricting the system’s freedom, speed and openness.

Restrictions could affect every requester

Some of the toughest measures have been adopted in Indiana. Officials in Allen County reported a rise in “suspiciously non-human” requests for years of purchase-order data. Processing them took staff up to 20 hours a week, the Journal Gazette reported. The state law passed without a single vote against it and took effect on July 1. It allows officials to charge out-of-state requesters, prioritize local residents and deny requests if they suspect data collection or phishing.

Neighboring Adams County went further. In March, it banned commercial use of its records, including resale. The records are still released, but each comes with a notice of the ban, which is difficult to enforce in practice. The county used to receive about one request a week; then the number rose to ten, sometimes all at once. After the state law took effect, requests fell to one to five. County attorney Clayton Lengerich says the new rules do not prevent individual citizens from demanding accountability from local government; they target requests used for commercial analysis with AI.

Processing requests is expensive, and someone has to cover the cost. In 1986, Congress amended the federal law to let agencies charge commercial requesters more. But there are so many exceptions that this rarely happens in practice. Kwoka says collecting fees costs agencies more than the revenue brings in. She estimates that commercial requesters cover less than 5% of costs. In one case she studied, the Food and Drug Administration collected $328,000 while incurring $33.6 million in costs.

Kwoka has long advocated “proactive disclosure” — publishing information that is most frequently requested. She points to the Environmental Protection Agency, which put site-contamination records online after commercial buyers had spent years requesting them one address at a time. Some states have created portals to publish general spending data regularly. California’s Open FI$Cal is one such portal. But it does not include full contracts, which are often requested by companies selling technology to the government. Contracts specify their terms, termination conditions and service obligations.

Pursuit and Starbridge say they would support publishing the data and believe their business models could work even if it were freely available. Vecchietti says the best request is one that does not need to be filed because the information is already public. But publication also costs money, and commercial requesters are not offering to cover that expense.

The biggest risk of reform is that rules designed to limit mass AI-assisted requests and automated data collection will make it harder for out-of-state journalists, researchers and lawyers helping immigrants to do their work. Meanwhile, the number of requests continues to rise. Row believes small towns will have to keep responding: otherwise, a dangerous precedent could emerge in which people cannot hold the government accountable.

Dataist’s take: The dispute is not only about the burden on agencies, but also about who will retain access to public records. Some states are trying to limit mass requests, while other solutions — such as publishing data in advance — require funding. If new rules are based on suspicion that AI was used, they could also affect people gathering records for work in the public interest.
