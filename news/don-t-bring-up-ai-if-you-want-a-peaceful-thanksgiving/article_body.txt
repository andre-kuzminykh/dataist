In the US, arguments about AI are becoming another cause of family conflict, alongside politics and other contentious subjects. Young people worry that AI weakens critical thinking, threatens their jobs and uses other people's creative work without consent. Parents and older relatives are more likely to see it as a useful tool. Psychologist Matt Lundquist advises against turning Thanksgiving into an argument for its own sake: disagreements about AI often reflect anxiety about the future and a desire to be heard.

## Why AI is becoming a more common source of arguments at the family table

## Different views around the same table

Sophie, a 27-year-old social work student from North Carolina, has never used ChatGPT or Claude. She says she adds the words “minus AI” to every Google search and has disabled Microsoft Copilot and similar tools. She worries about possible harm to critical thinking and brain function, ties between some AI company executives and the Trump administration, and the environmental impact of data centers, especially on water supplies in communities of color. Sophie links her opposition to AI to her anti-capitalist beliefs.

Sophie's parents and siblings generally share her views, but AI is widely used among her extended family. Sophie worries that her aunt is close to losing touch with reality because of AI. A cousin who recently had twins used ChatGPT to put together a complete feeding and sleep schedule for the babies, and also bought an app that uses AI to assess their condition before they see a pediatrician.

The difference in views makes family gatherings tense. Sophie expects AI to come up again at Thanksgiving and worries about irritation, judgment and being accused of naivety.

There have already been similar conflicts in Sophie's family. A few years ago, she sent relatives her résumé and asked them for feedback. The same cousin uploaded it to ChatGPT without permission. Sophie was outraged that her intellectual property had been used to train large language models, and the two had a serious argument.

The cousin's husband, a tech company founder, asked Sophie pointed questions in an effort to challenge her position. For example, he asked what if a poor Black person in Africa could use AI to create a source of income. Sophie persuaded another cousin to stop using ChatGPT, but she immediately switched to Claude. Sophie sees this as replacing one tool with another, only with better packaging.

## AI intensifies existing disagreements

Over the past decade, political polarization in the US has turned family holidays into occasions for arguments. Topics include Trump, immigration, Israel and Palestine, the war in Iran, abortion and LGBTQ rights. Now AI is joining that list.

“It's a new source of friction between generations,” says Matt Lundquist, a psychologist, founder and clinical director of Tribeca Therapy in New York. He says arguments about AI are becoming more common, especially in families. Disagreements can drive apart even people who used to see eye to eye.

That happened to Lindsay, a 40-year-old romance novelist from Pennsylvania. Her family shares similar political views, and relatives had never needed to agree on which subjects to avoid at the table. But during Easter dinner, Lindsay argued with an uncle who was impressed by ChatGPT's capabilities.

As a professional writer, she was upset by her uncle's attitude toward AI. Last year, Lindsay learned that one of her books had been used to train models. She tried to explain to her uncle that ChatGPT was built using the work of real authors, and that AI's use was directly harming her.

Her uncle seemed confused and nervous. Lindsay says he appeared to be hearing for the first time that models are trained on real people's intellectual property without naming or compensating them. She got the impression that he still saw AI as a harmless technology.

Similar arguments arise with other older relatives in creative fields. John, a 20-year-old cinematography student from Chicago, worries about his career and the future of art. His girlfriend works in animation. John's father is a chemical engineer and civil engineer in the chemical industry. He thinks highly of AI and uses it to simplify some of his work managing plants across the country and organizing data.

Conversations between father and son grow heated. John says AI could take his job; his father thinks that won't happen. John counters that it is already happening. The discussion then quickly reaches a dead end: both are too firmly set in their views.

## Young people worry about their jobs

Lundquist observes that people under 30 often criticize their parents over their attitude toward AI. This is especially common among young adults who are just entering the job market, have not yet bought a home and are not raising children.

A Pew poll published in August found that most Americans under 30 are more concerned about AI than excited about it. 73% of respondents in this age group think AI will lead to fewer jobs.

At the same time, young people use AI themselves. According to Axios, which published its findings in August, 61% of survey respondents aged 18 to 34 had used ChatGPT in the previous month, 48% had used Gemini and 20% had used Claude.

Luke, a 23-year-old from Toronto, says AI has become the most discussed topic in his family. He graduated from a sports media program and is struggling to find his first job. His father, who has worked in computer science and web development for many years, sees AI as the next big step for everyone.

Luke says arguments spill into all kinds of conversations. Last Canadian Thanksgiving, a conversation about his career and future plans turned into an argument about AI. Luke understands that his father wants what is best for him, but suspects he is afraid that rejecting AI will hurt his son's career and make it harder for him to earn a living.

In the family of Kaylee, a 26-year-old Vermonter studying for a master's degree in social work, disagreements also fall along generational lines. Her parents describe Kaylee and her younger brother's opposition to AI as a position unique to young people.

The last time Kaylee visited her parents, her mother said ChatGPT had helped her redesign the backyard. Kaylee was upset: a close friend of hers is studying landscape architecture. She considers landscape design an art form and does not understand why someone who can afford it would not pay a professional for creative work when AI makes it possible to do without one.

## An argument about AI may be about something else

Lundquist thinks many older people underestimate how real the threat of AI feels to their younger relatives. If someone already owns a second or third home, has a 2.8% mortgage and has sent their children to college, the psychologist explains, those risks may not worry them much. Being able to ask a bot to order groceries can then seem simply convenient.

Lundquist advises against raising the subject if the conversation is already expected to become another holiday battle. A discussion can be useful when people genuinely want to hear each other's views and exchange ideas.

If an argument does start, the psychologist suggests working out what the participants are really arguing about. Anger that the older generation has left the younger one with few opportunities, or disappointment with how work and compensation are structured, may lie beneath a discussion about AI. Lundquist says family conflicts over AI are connected to the desire to be understood: a son or daughter may see a father who asks Claude to find a sweet potato recipe not as someone trying a new technology, but as someone who fails to notice their worries.

Dataist's take: AI has become a family issue not only because of disagreements about technology, but because it brings together concerns about jobs, creative work and young people's futures. But Lundquist's advice applies to situations where a discussion is expected to turn into an argument: he suggests looking for the reason behind the disagreement instead of trying to prove a point for its own sake.
