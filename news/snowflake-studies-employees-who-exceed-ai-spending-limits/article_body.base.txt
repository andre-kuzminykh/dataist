# Snowflake studies employees who exceed AI spending limits

## Turning overspending into a workflow

Allen believes budgets are necessary: an AI agent can consume a lot of resources on a question whose answer is not worth the cost. At the VentureBeat AI Impact event in San Francisco, he compared such an agent to a retriever enthusiastically trying to complete a task again and again. Allen said an employee might discover that an agent had spent a couple of thousand dollars on an answer that was not worth that much.

But when an employee reaches the limit, Allen finds out what they were working on and what they discovered. The goal is not to punish them, but to understand whether they have found a way to do something useful for the company.

One example is a Snowflake account manager who used an internal agent for go-to-market work. The data showed that he was consuming a very large number of tokens. At the same time, the employee was preparing a personalized HTML report for client meetings, describing how that client’s requests and problems had been resolved over the previous 30 days.

The idea proved useful, but it could be implemented more efficiently: for each report, the agent created skills from scratch on the fly. Snowflake’s team optimized the process and built it into the agent itself.

Snowflake also tries to recognize employees who find new ways to use AI. Allen said the company holds meetings at different levels to showcase their work. Colleagues who spent three hours on a routine task can learn how another employee completed it in five seconds. Allen called this reaction a strong fear of missing out.

## Snowflake’s “pioneers” and reusable skills

According to a Snowflake representative, roughly 5–10% of the company’s 2,500-person engineering organization work as “pioneers.” These are engineers who assign entire tasks to teams of coding agents.

When these employees find a new workflow, Snowflake turns it into a reusable skill for others. The company gave two examples:

- Code review split among several agents working in parallel.
- A skill that synthesizes quarterly results from Slack data, Jira and transcripts of sales conversations.

According to the company representative, Snowflake sales employees now write code and build custom applications for clients. The company attributes these changes to the freedom it gives employees to experiment.

## What Atlassian’s internal hackathon showed

Atlassian’s Teamwork Lab studied an internal hackathon held from April 16 to 20. It involved 870 teams, which submitted projects for evaluation by a jury.

On August 11, lab head Molly Sands wrote that teams with one “AI superuser” were 18 percentage points more likely to score above the median than teams without one. Team size was taken into account. Teams with two or more superusers performed about as well as teams with one.

Atlassian defines superusers as employees in the top 25% of weekly AI use within their professional group. The study covered one voluntary hackathon at one company and did not establish a causal link between AI use and team results.

Sands opposes training that consists only of traditional classes. She said a company can change how employees work by building the right systems for them, rather than simply sending everyone to numerous Claude courses. The reusable skills Snowflake creates from the work of its “pioneers” are one example of this approach.

## Most new engineers at Snowflake are junior employees

According to Snowflake, 70–80% of new hires in its engineering organization are junior employees with less than two years of experience. Senior Vice President of Engineering Vivek Raghunathan told The AI Journal that new engineers understand workflows built around AI tools from the outset better than their most experienced colleagues do. The company believes it has enough experienced employees to train and develop newcomers successfully.

This hiring approach runs counter to the usual emphasis on experience. A publicly available description of a 2026 Stanford Graduate School of Business study calls Snowflake’s strategy unconventional: the company hires junior engineers on the premise that, in the AI era, flexibility and adaptability may matter more than accumulated experience in a specific field. Raghunathan told The AI Journal that not having 15 years of habitually working one way can be an advantage.

Paul Bingham, senior vice president and executive dean of the School of Technology at Western Governors University and a former FBI special agent, proposed a different approach for companies that need AI security specialists: develop people already working there. They know the company’s priorities and how its systems work, so they often need only to acquire a related set of skills.

Atlassian has data on its own graduates. According to Sands’ analysis of more than 2,200 new hires in engineering organizations, recent graduates hired into engineering roles were 19% more likely to become AI superusers than other new engineers at the company. At the event, Sands called junior employees important to transforming work with AI. She said early-career employees in Atlassian’s sales development department had significantly changed some workflows.

Meanwhile, venture capital firm SignalFire reported in June that hiring recent graduates and entry-level employees at 12 major technology companies, including Alphabet, Meta and Amazon, had fallen by about 65% compared with 2019.

Snowflake did not answer three questions about hiring junior engineers. A company representative said the HR department had not had time to prepare a comparison with previous years because the process is done manually. The company also did not specify how many employees the 70–80% figure covers or whether junior engineers make up the majority of its “pioneers.”

## Employees may hide their AI use

To learn from employees’ experience, a company needs to know how they use AI. However, according to an Atlassian review published on April 29 of a survey of more than 560 technology workers outside the company, 40% of employees aged 21 to 24 hide their AI use from managers and colleagues. Among workers aged 25 and older, the figure is 29%.

Atlassian also found that admitting to using AI can create the impression that an employee is lazy. The experiment involved 961 American knowledge workers. All of them evaluated the same work by the same fictional colleague. When a note mentioned AI use, participants were ten times more likely to think the colleague was lazy: 30% versus 3% when the note did not mention AI. Participants were also 24 percentage points less likely to recommend that colleague for a high-profile project.

According to Atlassian, the effect the company calls the “laziness penalty” almost disappears where AI use is actively encouraged. Sands noted that companies urge workers to use AI, yet employees may judge colleagues for being open about it. She said people take their cues from what the organization rewards. If employees face risks for learning openly, the company will not be able to turn their experience with AI into a benefit quickly.

Overspending reviews can identify employees who use AI extensively, even if they do not talk about it. Publicly recognizing their work at meetings is one way to show that such use is welcome. However, the study does not establish whether Snowflake’s practices reduce the concealment of AI use or the disapproval associated with it.

## Snowflake has not disclosed the returns on budget overruns

A Snowflake representative said the company could not provide specific return-on-investment figures for workflows created by employees who exceeded spending limits.

The open question is what value these workflows provide after other employees start using them, and whether that value justifies the cost of finding and developing such solutions.

Will Allen, Molly Sands and Paul Bingham spoke at the VentureBeat AI Impact event “Keeping Pace With AI: The Skills and Workflows Teams Need Now,” held on November 12 in San Francisco. Western Governors University sponsored the event; Atlassian paid for promotional materials on VentureBeat.

Dataist’s take: Snowflake’s approach turns exceeding a limit into a reason to study a workflow and, if it is useful, make it available to others. But the company has not yet disclosed the returns these solutions deliver after they are scaled, so their economic value cannot be assessed.
