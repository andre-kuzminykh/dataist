Mercy Health-Springfield Regional Medical Center in Ohio plans to stop using dozens of technicians who monitor patients’ heart rates and hand some of their work over to AI. The hospital says the monitoring system is being upgraded so it can send important data directly to medical staff. The decision has sparked debate in the local community: the union is discussing with hospital management what to do about employees affected by the automation, while commenters worry that nurses will face a heavier workload.

# Ohio hospital to replace patient monitoring technicians with AI

## What will change at the hospital

The medical center’s plans were first reported by the Springfield News-Sun. The hospital is upgrading patient monitoring technology across its units. A spokesperson said the goal is to bring important information closer to patients and help staff respond to their condition more quickly and precisely.

Telemetry technicians, also known as monitor technicians, track patients’ heart rates and alert medical staff to emergencies. They support nurses working at patients’ bedsides. Automating this work could increase the number of false alarms, deepen reliance on a stable internet connection and make the hospital more vulnerable to cyberattacks. Another risk is “automation bias”: placing too much trust in the system could make staff less vigilant.

## Reactions from staff and residents

A representative of the SEIU District 1199 healthcare workers’ union told WDTN that the union is meeting with hospital management to discuss next steps for employees affected by the automation.

The decision has drawn criticism in the local community. One commenter under WDTN’s Facebook post called it “a disgraceful path to the future.” The commenter also worried that nurses would have more work to do: they said they had spoken with several nurses while their mother was in the hospital for surgery. The commenter added that they hoped all the nurses would leave the medical center.

Dataist's take: Automating monitoring does not relieve the hospital of its responsibility to detect dangerous changes in patients’ condition in time. The key question is how the new system will affect nurses’ workloads and how reliably it will operate amid false alarms, internet outages and cyberattacks.
