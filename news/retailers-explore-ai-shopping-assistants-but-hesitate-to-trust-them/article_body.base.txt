# AI assistants are coming to stores, but retailers aren’t ready to trust them with the checkout

## Retailers are exploring a new channel

Meta says Muse will take care of everyday tasks, from planning Christmas shopping and finding a cheaper electricity tariff to putting together a school schedule. The company has partnered with major UK retailers and describes the assistant as convenient, safe and time-saving.

The shift to online shopping has already changed the business of traditional retailers. AI agents could now affect how shoppers discover products and choose where to buy them. John Roberts, founder and chief executive of online electricals retailer AO.com, says the impact of these assistants is limited for now, but that using them to shop is a significant trend.

AO.com hopes to attract recommendations from AI agents in the same way it attracts ordinary shoppers: by offering good prices and service, then earning positive reviews on platforms such as Trustpilot. Roberts says the company needs to work out how to become the best option for whichever assistant a shopper uses.

Smaller companies sometimes prefer to watch how the new channel develops. But staying out of new technologies has already cost brands dearly: similar caution about online retail in the early 2000s led some to disappear.

Sharon Iles, senior apparel analyst at research and consulting firm GlobalData, says partnering with Muse will give brands an early look at how people shop through an AI agent, which products they choose and what the assistant recommends. That data could help make product listings more visible. At the same time, retailers need to preserve their own sales channels.

If an agent bypasses retailers’ websites, stores could lose impulse purchases that shoppers add to their baskets while browsing. Clothing presents another challenge: shoppers may find it harder to choose the right size and fit, potentially increasing returns. Iles says owned channels should remain the foundation of the business, and shoppers should have more reasons to use them directly.

Sainsbury’s and Tesco have built major businesses around loyalty schemes and their own websites: they earn money from shopper data and sell advertising space to brands. But ignoring AI agents altogether is risky too. In the US, Muse ranks first and second in Google and Apple’s free app stores, respectively; the app has been downloaded millions of times.

## Access to personal data and shoppers’ doubts

Britons may be more cautious about AI agents than Americans, but the technology is already moving beyond early adopters. In a recent YouGov survey, a third of people planning to shop in this year’s Black Friday sales said they intended to use AI for help. Last year, the figure was 19%.

For supporters of Muse and its competitors, including Google’s Gemini and ChatGPT’s Dots, these tools are personal assistants that organise everyday life. Others see this level of access as excessive intrusion: the agent analyses personal data and uses it to generate revenue.

For Muse to work as Meta intends, it needs access to email, calendars and shopping apps, as well as information about a user’s contacts on WhatsApp, Facebook and Instagram. The assistant may also need details about a user’s preferences, finances and health.

Sainsbury’s invites shoppers to tell Muse about their plans for the week. The agent can then put together a grocery list for a dinner party, account for different dietary requirements, or suggest menus and recipes.

Mark Given, Sainsbury’s chief technology and marketing officer, says the retailer is introducing the assistant cautiously and listening to shoppers. AI can handle routine tasks, such as checking offers and filling a basket, he says, while helping people find good deals and shopping ideas more quickly.

In the UK, Muse must get the user’s permission before making a purchase or sending an email. A separate “safety agent” checks the assistant’s actions against the permissions it has been given. Meta says Muse cannot see users’ passwords or payment methods, and that their conversations with the assistant are not shared with advertisers.

Trust remains a problem, however. YouGov found that 48% of shoppers who use AI worry about biased or sponsored recommendations. Research firm Kantar found that only 23% trust chatbot recommendations.

Some retailers are also restricting AI agents’ access to their data. Amazon has blocked them from its marketplace, though it is reportedly discussing opening access with Meta. Last year, Amazon sued Perplexity over its shopping feature, which automatically places orders on users’ behalf.

Data from consulting firm KPMG suggests shoppers are reluctant to hand AI the task of finding discounts. Among those planning to shop during Black Friday, a major UK sales event conducted primarily online, 12% intend to use a tool such as ChatGPT or Gemini. Last year, the share was 15%.

Linda Ellett, head of consumer markets, retail and leisure at KPMG, says trust is essential. Some shoppers are willing to share data to simplify everyday tasks and interact with an agent; others are not comfortable with that approach.

## Shoppers still come to stores

Around 70% of purchases are still made in physical stores, with the share even higher in some categories. AI agents are therefore a long way from driving stores off the high street. Familiar brands, advertising, store presence and social media posts will continue to influence shoppers’ choices.

Most people use AI at only particular stages of their search: for example, to compare prices on kitchen appliances, home electronics or groceries, or to get advice on a complex product such as electronics or supplements. Consulting firm OC&C says AI still more often helps shoppers check a choice they have already made than form a new preference.

Mark Howell, Rithum’s director of retail for Europe, whose company works with Asos, M&S, Aldi and Shein, says retailers are not ready to hand order processing over to bots until questions of responsibility are resolved. In his view, AI-agent commerce is already developing, but there is a lot of exaggeration around it, and the legal and commercial arrangements are more complicated than commonly assumed. Retailers, particularly in Europe, want to know who is responsible if an agent makes a mistake.

AI agents have very little impact on shopper behaviour for now, but large companies already have teams working on generative engine optimisation, or GEO. Their work includes:

- getting brands featured in Reddit discussions, an important source for some platforms;
- giving AI models accurate, detailed product information in text and images;
- responding to reviews on Google and Trustpilot.

Roberts says AI agents will not necessarily weaken brands and loyalty programmes that companies have built through advertising and paid search promotion. If an agent finds a better deal for a loyalty programme member, it could help attract new members.

Industry representatives expect that ChatGPT, Amazon, Meta and Google may eventually make money from their AI platforms through paid search or targeted advertising. Roberts expects agents to generate advertising revenue too.

Dataist’s take: Retailers currently see AI agents as an additional channel, not a replacement for their own websites and stores. A convenient interface alone will not be enough to drive widespread shopping through bots: questions remain about trust, data access and responsibility for mistakes.
