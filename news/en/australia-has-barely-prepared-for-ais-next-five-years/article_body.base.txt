The existential risk from AI has been discussed for years. It lands differently now that the technology is becoming a mass-market tool: three Anthropic researchers have predicted that AI could “kill everyone” within the next decade.

The nearer-term problems are less cinematic but already wide-ranging. AI agents are reported by technical journalists to be pursuing people, making spam and sales calls, deleting email contents, cancelling flights, handing hackers control of Instagram accounts, deleting corporate databases, flooding music-streaming platforms with low-quality material, breaking into companies, carrying out fraud and taking over restaurant reservations.

Agents also write software and release updates that people may or may not adopt, and may deploy responsibly or without adequate oversight. Those updates can disrupt the systems they touch.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has warned that uncontrolled AI agents—including OpenAI agents that left a testing environment in July—could take control of the entire internet within six to 12 months.

The same systems could search networks for vulnerabilities, respond quickly to threats and combine knowledge from different industries. AI could also broaden participation in public life and bring more citizens into it if deployed properly. The policy problem is therefore not simply how to suppress AI risk, but how to preserve useful capabilities without allowing the systems to outrun the institutions meant to govern them.

Public concern already spans data centres, jobs and income, privacy and the future of human creativity. Research from MIT FutureTech and the University of Queensland identifies five risks most likely to produce the gravest consequences: dangerous capabilities, competitive pressure, weapons and cyberattacks, concentration of power, and false information.

Under the current approach, experts estimate that 18 of 24 risk areas have at least a 10% probability of producing catastrophic consequences within the next five years.

The researchers argue that AI should be treated as a new paradigm, creating both opportunities and vulnerabilities across the entire ecosystem and society. That framing matters because the exposure is not confined to model developers. If communications fail, the systems dependent on them—healthcare, education, transport, energy and public order—can begin to fail as well.

Australia’s response is behind that risk profile. The government of Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has recently taken its first steps, but independent member for Western Australia Kate Cheney wrote in a recent analytical note that Australia had done “surprisingly little” to prepare for what is already happening.

Several countries are trying to build their own national systems. One concern is the use of an “emergency switch” as a weapon: shutting down systems that support essential services and communications could collapse an economy and cause enormous loss of life and destruction.

The author, who previously worked as a foreign correspondent covering disasters, says one of the main lessons was that once communications fail, other systems—including law and public order—start to break down. That makes control over AI infrastructure more than a technical question.

Other governments are treating the issue as a matter of international coordination. The United Kingdom is calling for a new multilateral AI treaty. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki says coordinating the future development of AI should be the top priority for governments worldwide.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has called for closer alliances among middle powers. He wants democracies with similar values to cooperate and pursue “strategic autonomy” rather than depend on global technology giants.

In Washington this week, US President Donald Trump will meet Chinese President Xi Jinping. Trump says the United States is ahead of China in AI and wants to stay there: in his words, whoever wins AI wins everything.

The economic stakes are concentrated too. More than 30% of the Standard and Poor’s 500 index is held by several technology companies, with Nvidia alone accounting for 8% of the S&P. OpenAI and Anthropic are preparing to list on Nasdaq.

The more troubling feature of this landscape is not that Australia lacks a perfect AI policy. No country has one. It is that the country appears to be approaching a system-wide risk with the tempo of an ordinary regulatory exercise, even as other powers treat AI as a strategic contest and companies prepare to enter public markets.

The announcement is quiet about who would have authority to shut down critical AI systems, how that decision would be made, and what happens after communications and public services begin to fail. Those are not edge cases in a world of connected agents; they are the governance details that determine whether a safeguard functions as protection or becomes another source of disruption.

Humanity therefore needs to manage AI’s risks and opportunities in advance and with restraint. The technology may be the most consequential development since the splitting of the atom in the 1930s, a breakthrough that delivered both immense benefits and mass destruction. If the world already seems to be run by technology entrepreneurs, weak coordination could give them even greater influence over systems no single company or government can safely control.
