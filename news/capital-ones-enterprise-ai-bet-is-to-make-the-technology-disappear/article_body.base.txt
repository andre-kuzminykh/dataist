# Capital One’s best enterprise AI may be the kind employees barely notice

## When AI takes on execution

That shift makes operational expertise more valuable, not less. An employee who works in credit-card operations may understand the workflow better than almost anyone else in the company. Technical teams need that knowledge to build something useful. AI can help execute a solution, but it cannot decide what outcome a process should deliver. People who know the work have to do that.

At Capital One, engineers are encouraged to focus on strategic decisions: What customer problem are we actually solving? How will the change affect employees? What does the broader system need for the solution to work? How should people prepare to work effectively with AI?

As development cycles shrink, the bottlenecks move. Architecture, integration, testing, validation and launch readiness matter more. Capital One’s answer is disciplined experimentation: form a hypothesis, test it, share the result, then use what was learned to choose the next step. Both successes and failures should shape what happens next, rather than justify a decision already made.

## Technology that fits the work

In enterprise software, success can look like an employee doing the job without thinking about the technology. If people have to stop, switch tools, learn a complicated interface or become prompt specialists, the design still has room to improve.

Maureen compares her team to Q from the James Bond films: Q builds tools and systems so that the person on the mission can focus on the mission. The user should not need to understand how the technology works. It should be simple, unobtrusive and ready when needed.

Capital One builds intelligent platforms and employee solutions while scaling complex cloud financial infrastructure for tens of thousands of employees and more than 100 million customers. The aim is to fit technology into existing workflows and remove unnecessary friction.

That might mean surfacing relevant information in the right place, simplifying the next step or making a complex process easier to follow. A process could shrink from 12 steps to two. On an employee platform, manual prompt work could give way to intuitive automation.

## The hard part is knowing what to remove

Reach is only one measure of an enterprise system. A tool may be available to many people and still fail to help across different workflows, teams and situations. The harder test is whether it remains useful without adding complexity or forcing employees into a new tool.

Maureen says one advantage of building enterprise technology is the short feedback loop. An employee can message her or stop her in the hallway with a problem. Those conversations can reveal details about the user experience that a dashboard misses.

With tens of thousands of employees, small design choices recur across thousands of work situations. Direct feedback helps teams see where a system gets in the way, make adjustments and check whether it works under different conditions.

I think this is the more demanding promise behind “invisible” AI: not that employees never notice a system, but that they do not have to manage it on top of their work. That takes more than a clean interface. It takes teams able to combine operational knowledge, AI skills and careful testing.

Capital One’s proposed approach to leading those teams rests on three principles:

- **Build different kinds of expertise.** AI initiatives need deep subject-matter knowledge, developers who work with AI from the start and employees who have learned new skills. Leaders should make room for people to put solutions into practice and grow professionally.
- **Leave ego out of the work.** AI transformations stall when leaders pretend to have every answer. Openness makes it easier for teams to experiment, test hypotheses and choose what works.
- **Lead with empathy.** In periods of rapid change, effective leaders are not necessarily the loudest. They acknowledge uncertainty, listen closely, make space for different views and remove obstacles so teams can work accurately and safely.

## The advantage is still human

AI can extend what people can do, but it is not a strategy by itself. Capital One’s argument is that lasting advantage will go to organizations that use AI to make work clearer, more focused and more human.

What I’d want to know is how the company measures that promise. Fewer steps and direct employee feedback offer useful signals, but they do not, by themselves, show whether a system works across the many teams and situations it is meant to serve. The quieter the technology becomes, the more important it is to know whose work it is actually improving.
