## Scientists call for computer science to be rethought in the age of AI

## Programming is changing

AI is speeding up development and automating parts of the process, the authors note. Until recently, the labor involved in writing code by hand held the field back. Now generative models can turn a plain-language description of desired functions into a complex first version of a product in days rather than months. AI can also take on some time-consuming tasks, such as integration and testing.

In the researchers’ view, building software is becoming economically viable even for those who previously could not afford it: small cities, mid-sized manufacturers and specialized research projects.

The authors argue that university programs should shift their focus from writing code to checking and managing AI-generated systems. That requires students to have a solid grasp of computer science fundamentals. One proposal is to offer courses with assignments that must be completed without AI assistance. People still need to assess situations where, for example, safety and speed requirements conflict, and take responsibility for their decisions.

## Generative computing raises safety questions

The authors describe the shift from fixed programs to “generative computing” as the most far-reaching change. In the future, systems and AI agents for programming will be able to interpret human intent and generate their own behavior and the code they need while running. At the same time, the line between those who define workflows and those who use them is becoming less clear.

This approach calls for a rethink of computer science fundamentals. The researchers ask how to verify and certify a system’s safety, privacy and correctness when its behavior is formed dynamically at runtime.

The problem is particularly acute for embodied AI, such as robots and autonomous cars. Their physical actions must be calculated in real time on limited hardware, and an action that has already been taken cannot be undone. The authors also call for reducing the heavy energy and resource use of modern AI systems through more efficient algorithms and hardware architectures.

## AI is changing research

AI can also speed up research itself. In some cases, fields that once could have taken years or an entire doctoral project to study can now be investigated and tested in weeks or months.

The authors caution against using AI solely to work faster, however. For scientific progress to be reliable, people need to understand why an AI-generated solution works and where its limits are.

The researchers also criticize their own discipline: in their view, computer science has historically put technological progress first without paying enough attention to its effects on society. The authors argue that AI’s potential impact on the labor market, privacy and democracy calls for rapid change. The future of AI should not be left to technological and economic forces alone: computer science should engage now with other academic disciplines, policymakers and civil society.

Dataist's take: The researchers’ proposal concerns more than curricula: generating code at runtime makes systems harder to verify, especially when they control physical actions. The authors also point to the need to account for AI’s energy use and social consequences, but the paper does not propose specific measures or deadlines for these changes.
