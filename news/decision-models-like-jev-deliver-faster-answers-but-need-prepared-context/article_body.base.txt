Jev from TypeSafe AI, Clef from Cloudflare and OpenAI’s Decisions API are decision models: they skip extended text responses and return structured data in JSON along with a confidence probability. This speeds up results and makes them easier to integrate into programs, since developers do not have to parse ordinary text. But the model does not explain why it reached a particular conclusion, so the context and constraints need to be thought through in advance.

## Decision models like Jev deliver faster answers but require context prepared in advance

## Speed instead of reasoning

Modern large language models use the transformer architecture: they process a prompt and generate text sequentially. Models with billions of parameters are trained on trillions of lines of text and learn complex linguistic patterns. An extended response often includes the reasoning that led the model to its conclusion. Separate reasoning models are designed to make that reasoning explicit.

Decision models deliberately skip detailed reasoning and return a result immediately. The author calls this approach Type 1 thinking; it is useful where low latency and token savings matter. But developers may incur technical debt in return and lose one of the key capabilities of large language models: explaining how an answer was produced.

The author tested the difference by asking different models through the OpenRouter interface: “Will it rain today?” A conventional language model can consider what information a forecast requires and what data is missing. A decision model such as Jev instead returns a value in the `noul` structure, named after the Swiss mathematician and physicist Daniel Bernoulli. It contains only `true` or `false`.

Without an explanation, such an answer can be misleading, especially when the question matters. That does not mean decision models are useless or inaccurate: they simply do not explain their conclusions the way traditional language models do.

## Context has to be prepared in advance

When faced with an unclear question, a conventional AI agent can ask for clarification or describe in detail the assumptions behind its answer. A decision model uses only the context provided: it compares that context with its training data and returns a structured result, often a single value. A developer can use the result and its probability to choose an action in an application, but there are no logs of the decision process, so it is impossible to fully understand why the model answered as it did.

AI developers therefore need to gather in advance, in the context or state, all the information required for a well-founded answer. The author calls designing this context the most important skill for people building AI systems based on decision models.

## Where decision models are used

Among the suitable tasks, the author names evaluating chatbot responses by comparing them with predefined reference answers. Another example is routing support requests to categories and teams. Such requests often contain a lot of text that needs to be understood and assigned to the right category.

Time will show what other major use cases emerge. The author sees decision models as a notable technological update in the development of large language models. Dattaraj Rao is an architect of innovation and research and development at Persistent Systems.

Dataist's take: Decision models shift some of the work from the model to the developer. Answers arrive faster, but their conditions must be carefully specified in advance. The absence of reasoning logs limits the ability to verify why the system chose a particular option.
