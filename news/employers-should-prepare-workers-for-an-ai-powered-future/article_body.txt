Keith Ferrazzi, a Forbes columnist on the future of work, team performance and growth strategies, argues that companies should redefine their responsibilities to employees in the age of AI. Rather than promising to keep jobs unchanged, employers, he says, should help people build skills, find new roles within the company and move into work elsewhere. This matters especially in light of a McKinsey Global Institute forecast: around 11 million Americans in declining occupations could have to change jobs entirely over the next decade, but only about one in seven has a direct path into a growing occupation with little or no retraining.

# Keith Ferrazzi urges employers to prepare employees for AI-powered work

## Preparation instead of promises

Companies usually discuss AI in terms of productivity, automation, efficiency and skills. Employees, meanwhile, have a personal question: what will happen to their jobs if AI learns to perform their tasks? Training alone, or general promises that new occupations will emerge, is not enough, Ferrazzi argues.

According to McKinsey Global Institute’s September 2026 report, around 11 million U.S. workers in declining occupations may have to change jobs entirely over the next decade. About one in seven has a direct path into a growing occupation that will require little additional training or none at all. Ferrazzi argues that companies often restructure their businesses first, then leave laid-off employees to find their next career path on their own.

For the author, this issue is tied to his family history. He grew up in Pittsburgh during the decline of the steel industry. His father, a steelworker, lost his job; his mother started cleaning houses, and his parents took any work they could find to support the family. Ferrazzi says companies can take a different approach in the age of AI: help people find a path forward before they lose their jobs.

## Three employer commitments

Ferrazzi proposes that employers make three commitments: build employees’ AI capabilities, create pathways to new roles and help them move into work outside the company.

The first is to help every employee become an “AI black belt.” Companies should invest in helping workers operate confidently in an AI-enabled environment: apply their own expertise, collaborate with intelligent agents, rethink processes and take on tasks that were previously beyond their reach.

Ferrazzi compares this idea with the Six Sigma movement, which he worked with earlier in his career. Its impact, he says, came not just from teaching methods: it produced many practitioners who applied them in their work and taught their colleagues. AI training should work the same way, he argues, leading to more valuable work rather than stopping at the ability to write better prompts. A finance professional, for example, might move from preparing reports to coordinating AI-supported analysis and advising on decisions.

Training should not depend solely on an employee’s personal initiative. Employers, Ferrazzi argues, need clear development programs, peer-mentoring communities, practice on real business problems and ways to verify skill levels. AI literacy should be available to all employees, not just those with technical experience.

The second commitment is to make internal mobility standard practice. Companies often hire externally for new positions, the author says, even when current employees already have knowledge and skills that could transfer to another role. He therefore suggests starting workforce transformation with the question: which current employees could do the new work after receiving the necessary training?

At Elevance Health, job openings are first made available to internal candidates, and dedicated recruiters help identify suitable employees. Redeploying workers is part of the company’s response to changing roles. According to LinkedIn data cited in the article, employees at organizations with high internal mobility typically stay 53% longer than workers at companies with low mobility.

With AI, internal moves matter for more than employee retention. Employers, Ferrazzi argues, should identify in advance which roles are changing, what skills employees have and what they need to learn—before layoffs begin. Workforce planning, AI skills development and internal hiring should form parts of a single system.

An employee whose role is changing because of AI should not have to wait for a layoff notice to find out where their experience could be useful. Possible destinations include overseeing AI agents, designing customer experiences, operations and other growing functions, the author says.

The third commitment is to help employees beyond the company, too. No employer can guarantee a permanent job for everyone: AI will change demand for some roles, and some positions will disappear. But, Ferrazzi argues, a company’s responsibility should not end when an employee leaves.

An employer can help someone develop AI skills and find a new role within the business. If there is no suitable opportunity, it can help them move into AI-resilient work elsewhere. AI WorkBridge, a nonprofit coalition of employers now being formed, is intended to put this idea into practice by helping people move into new opportunities before they lose their jobs.

Ferrazzi gives the example of a contact-center employee whose duties are becoming increasingly automated. Their knowledge of customers, problem-solving ability and communication skills remain valuable. The task is to connect those skills with openings in healthcare, other growing occupations, new AI-related roles or entrepreneurship. AI WorkBridge is intended to bring together skills assessments, AI literacy, career preparation and employer partnerships to support transitions both within member companies and beyond them.

The goal is not to guarantee protection for new jobs against automation: Ferrazzi believes such a promise would be impossible to make responsibly. Instead, it is to identify fields where demand for workers is growing and actively connect people with employers and career opportunities. Someone laid off by one company may have the skills another urgently needs. Ferrazzi therefore urges employers to build a network of transitions between companies together, just as they collaborate on supply chains, technology standards and innovation.

## How to measure the new commitment

Ferrazzi calls this approach a new social contract for the AI economy. Companies restructured their businesses and cut jobs before AI emerged, while employees often had to look for a new direction almost entirely on their own. Now, the author argues, AI can be used not only to accelerate the disappearance of jobs but also to rethink employers’ responsibility for people’s futures.

Companies cannot promise that work will stay the same. But they can invest in employees’ capabilities, create opportunities for growth and help people move into new roles, including outside the business. Ferrazzi suggests measuring how well companies meet this commitment using concrete indicators:

- the number of employees with advanced AI skills;
- the number of moves into new internal roles;
- the number of employees in at-risk groups prepared before their jobs disappear;
- the number of successful moves into work outside the company.

The author argues that leadership should give these indicators at least as much weight as productivity gains from AI. When employees see that a company is investing in their future, not just automating their work, they are more willing to adopt the technology, try new ways of working and take part in business changes. CEOs should therefore ask not only how AI will transform the workforce, but also what the company will do for its employees’ future. Millions of jobs will change, but action can begin before they disappear.

Dataist's take: Ferrazzi’s proposal shifts responsibility for adapting to AI from individual employees to employers, and calls for measuring career transitions alongside productivity. But he does not promise that new roles will last or be protected from automation. His approach rests on preparing people and finding opportunities before layoffs begin.
