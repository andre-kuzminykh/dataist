## What the ring ships

Recording starts and stops with two presses of the button. Holding the button lets users ask Vocci AI questions, although that feature works only while the app is open. A light and vibration motor indicate recording.

The ring’s inner and outer surfaces are made of titanium, or are likely covered with a titanium layer. Vocci includes a large plastic case that can recharge the ring up to three times.

The hardware has a clear advantage in longer conversations. In testing, more than an hour of conversation in noisy cafés produced an accurate transcript for most of the discussion. Short notes were a weaker use case.

The design also puts Vocci beside other products with different interaction models:

- Pebble’s Index 01 Ring requires the user to hold a button to create a note or reminder.
- Sandbar’s Stream Ring uses a touch panel on top.
- Vocci makes meeting recording the device’s primary function and uses a double press to control it.

## The app is behind the hardware

Vocci’s software is less convincing than its recording quality. The app is difficult to navigate, and AI outputs for short notes were often longer than the recordings themselves.

Each recording is divided into three sections:

- <strong>Chat</strong> — the main conversation transcript.
- <strong>Highlights</strong> — short excerpts saved by pressing a button during the conversation.
- <strong>Notes</strong> — a summary and conclusions from the entire conversation.

The app also includes a <strong>Base</strong> tab containing transcripts, recordings and everything the user creates from them. That makes the product feel more like a collection of separate surfaces than a focused workflow. A direct way to start an AI chat about the transcript from an individual session was not apparent.

Voice-controlled rings make sense for short notes and reminders, but Vocci does not connect directly to reminder services. Software features are also arriving slowly. The company says users can connect Vocci to other assistants through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), but that option appeared in the app only a few weeks ago.

Other meeting-recording services are already building platforms around automated conclusions and work processes. Vocci has not fully developed that layer yet, leaving the ring dependent on a companion app that does not always turn good raw audio into a useful next step.

## The privacy problem is built into the shape

A ring can look like ordinary jewelry until someone notices its button. The recording indicator exists, but it faces the wearer rather than the person being recorded.

People are generally expected to disclose that a conversation is being recorded, and some countries and regions require it by law. With a device this unobtrusive, however, a wearer could fail to disclose the recording without the other people noticing. Vocci says it recommends warning people before recording, but a recommendation does not solve the visibility problem created by the form factor.

My read is that this is the product’s central tension, not a footnote. The ring is attractive precisely because it stays out of the way, yet that same quality makes informed consent harder to verify.

## A $249 bet on personal preference

Vocci costs $249, more than other note-taking devices such as Plaud or Pocket. The company attributes the price to the ring’s lightweight form factor.

The target customer is someone who does not want to attach a device to the back of a phone or wear a bracelet or pendant. At the hardware level, many products in this category reduce to a similar combination of several microphones, a battery and a smartphone connection. The differentiator is increasingly where those components sit on the body.

I think Vocci is making a reasonable hardware bet, but the software makes the premium harder to justify today. If the market fills with more shapes for recording meetings, convenience will get users to try them; reliable workflows and visible consent will determine which ones stay on their hands.
