# CoreWeave changes how it finances AI infrastructure

## Funding backed by customer contracts

CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator said on the earnings call that, at current interest rates, the company would not borrow in the unsecured debt market and did not need to. Instead, it expects to secure loans backed by specific customer contracts and draw on the funds as needed to build infrastructure for its large order backlog.

Over the next three months, CoreWeave expects to receive about $9 billion in customer advances and deposits. More than $3 billion of that amount is tied to new contracts for capacity the company has already built, so fulfilling them will not require additional spending on equipment.

Competitors Nebius and IREN also rely on advance payments from Microsoft for computing capacity. This reflects a broader trend: AI infrastructure providers increasingly expect large technology companies to pay in advance for capacity to be built.

Advances can reduce CoreWeave’s immediate need to borrow. But by accepting the money, the company commits to providing services at an agreed price. If demand for computing continues to grow, that price could later become less favorable for CoreWeave.

## Shorter contracts and higher prices

CoreWeave is also allocating more capacity to short-term deals. These contracts can bring in more revenue, and their prices can be revised more often.

In July, the company raised prices by about 25%, and later in the same quarter by another 10%. Prices are continuing to rise in the current quarter; Intrator said the increases more than offset higher costs.

CoreWeave earns more than $40 million per megawatt per year for capacity under short-term contracts. For new facilities leased under four- and five-year agreements, the price ranges from $20 million to $25 million per megawatt.

In August, the company closed a $2.6 billion loan, the first specifically designed to finance short-term customer contracts. Called DDTL 5.5, the loan allows CoreWeave to draw funds in separate tranches as needed.

Short-term agreements let the company charge higher prices and change them more often. But long-term contracts are more attractive to lenders: even at lower prices, they provide more predictable cash flow and greater confidence that the loan will be repaid.

## Debt and demand risk

Wall Street reacted positively to CoreWeave’s quarterly results, but bond investors are less optimistic. Last month, yields on some of the company’s bonds exceeded 15%. This points to concerns about the debt CoreWeave is taking on to build data centers before those investments begin to pay off.

The company is looking for ways to expand without unsecured debt. But the strategy depends on contract renewals and prices holding up, while short-term demand for computing capacity could change. TechCrunch asked CoreWeave for more details about its credit facility.

Dataist's take: Advances and contract-backed loans help CoreWeave finance growth, but they do not eliminate the risk. The company is taking on service obligations while building infrastructure in anticipation of demand whose durability has yet to be proven. Short-term deals could increase revenue, but long-term contracts remain a more persuasive source of support for lenders.
