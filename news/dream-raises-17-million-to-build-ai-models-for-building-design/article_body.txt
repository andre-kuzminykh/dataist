## A model trained on building records

Dream is developing what it calls a world model of the built environment, aimed at automating architectural, engineering and construction work. Its training data covers more than 100 million square feet of buildings, gathered from building-permit archives across the United States.

The founders say the model can account for what is inside walls, which materials are used, and what beams are made of and how much weight they can bear. Dream calls this approach “structural cognition”: generating designs that incorporate internal elements such as plumbing and heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems.

A standard large language model such as ChatGPT can generate an image of a building, but that image may not meet structural requirements. Dream says its model takes physics and building codes into account. The distinction matters: a plausible-looking image is not a usable building plan.

[INFO:facts] 100 million square feet :: training data | 10 projects :: Texas and California

Dream says its product is already being sold to AEC professionals and developers, who use it to explore building designs and estimate overall costs. The platform is operating on 10 projects in Texas and California.

## The claim behind the funding

Dream is entering a field where Motif, Snaptrude and Arcol are also trying to update AEC software with AI. Eldar and Mathur say their differentiator is a world model that can design entire buildings, rather than handle only parts of the process.

The founders met at Stanford, where they lived together and studied computer science, then each spent 10 years at Palantir. Eldar led product development on an 800-person team building software for US defense and intelligence agencies; Mathur was a senior vice president in Palantir’s commercial division. Neither had direct experience in architecture.

Mathur told TechCrunch that technology had moved from dial-up internet to superintelligent AI while, in his view, the ability to build the physical world had declined. The founders were concerned that the US builds fewer buildings than it did decades ago as construction costs have risen.

Andreessen Horowitz partner Kimberly Tan said the firm first invested in Dream more than a year ago, when the idea was still taking shape. She said the company’s progress had impressed her and that she had not seen other teams applying the same technical approach with models of their own.

I think the key uncertainty is not whether Dream can generate a whole-building design, but whether professionals can trust that design enough to use it in projects where a structural mistake carries real costs. The announcement gives a training-data scale and a count of active projects, but no evidence yet about how often the model’s designs survive engineering review. That gap is where the promise of replacing legacy tools meets the demands of construction.
