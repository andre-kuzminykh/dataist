## A large network, a divided public

Flock’s system spans 49 states and includes about 120,000 AI-equipped cameras on streets and highways. The cameras automatically read license plates and record passing vehicles. Flock says its technology helps law enforcement investigate and solve crimes.

The company supplies cameras and software to more than 4,800 law-enforcement agencies and nearly 1,000 businesses. But a recent Reuters/Ipsos poll found that Americans are divided over the cameras: 38% support their use in their communities, while 47% oppose it.

In September, Flock offered employees the option to apply for voluntary departure, Wired reported. The reported plan to cut about 270 jobs comes after that step, but the company has not publicly confirmed either move.

## Investment and resistance

Founded in 2017 and based in Atlanta, Flock has raised more than $950 million from venture investors. In March, Andreessen Horowitz led a $275 million funding round that valued the company at $7.5 billion. Flock says the funding helped it open a new U.S. manufacturing facility and prepare to enter the drone market.

The pushback is no longer limited to privacy advocates:

- Florida banned automatic license-plate readers on state highways in September, citing privacy risks associated with Flock cameras.
- Home Depot investors recently called for a review of the retailer’s partnerships with surveillance-technology providers, after reports that Flock data had been used to enforce immigration laws.
- A lawsuit filed in Virginia alleges that the widespread use of Flock’s AI cameras amounts to warrantless surveillance.

Flock cameras, like AI data centers, have also become a subject of debate ahead of November’s midterm elections. Candidates are increasingly criticizing opponents by linking them to the technology.

I think the central tension is that Flock’s growth and its political exposure are happening at the same time. The company has funding, a broad customer base and plans to expand; the public response and legal challenges point to limits on how much surveillance communities will accept. What the announcement leaves quiet is whether the cuts are tied to that backlash, or to something else. Without a public explanation, the layoffs are a signal of change at Flock, but not yet an explanation of why.
