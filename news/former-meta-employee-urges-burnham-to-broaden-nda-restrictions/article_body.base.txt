## Why Wynn-Williams cannot talk about Meta

Wynn-Williams delivered the annual Orwell Lecture in London. She said she cannot discuss her own case: after leaving Meta, then called Facebook, she signed a severance agreement. The restrictions imposed by the company bar her from discussing a number of claims in her memoir and expose her to serious legal consequences.

Wynn-Williams says NDAs have become a tool for influential people to control public debate. Their purpose, she said, is not to win an argument but to make sure there is nothing to argue about: to leave no records or facts, and no shared basis for scrutinising the actions of people in power.

She welcomed the prime minister’s promise to restrict the use of NDAs. Earlier this week, Burnham said he was preparing changes to protect victims of crime and workplace harassment, allowing them to choose freely whom to speak to, even after signing a non-disclosure agreement.

Wynn-Williams says that is not enough. She called for the changes to apply retroactively, for NDAs to be banned, and for people to be able to speak freely. The public can push for this by contacting MPs, people with political connections and neighbours, she said. Even after the announced changes, she warned, many people with information important to the public will remain silenced.

## NDAs and the “memory hole”

Wynn-Williams compared non-disclosure agreements to the “memory hole” in George Orwell’s novel *1984*, where documents inconvenient to those in power are sent to be destroyed. She says employees who could report problems related to the rapid development of AI also need protection from this mechanism.

Lawmakers, journalists and courts are outside tech companies, Wynn-Williams said, while the information they need is held by people working inside them: researchers and engineers. But to get through the door, employees have to give up their right to speak. She added that the industry buys silence through contracts, courts enforce them, and society relies on the very people barred from reporting potentially dangerous products.

## Legal dispute and allegations against Meta

In her memoir *Careless People*, Wynn-Williams made allegations against Meta, including that the company worked with the Chinese government on censorship tools, and described the negative effects of its work on teenagers. Meta disputes the book and says it contains several false claims.

Because of an interim arbitration ruling obtained by Meta, Wynn-Williams cannot discuss the book. Earlier this year, at the Hay Festival in Wales, she sat silently on stage on her lawyers’ advice. She is now suing Meta over the restrictions the company imposed. Meta calls the lawsuit an attempt to sell more copies of the book.

Wynn-Williams also mentioned reports of non-disclosure agreements in the cases involving David Walliams and Russell Brand. Several women have accused Walliams of sexual harassment; he denies the allegations. Brand was recently found guilty of rape and sexual assault. In both cases, Wynn-Williams said, it was reported that NDAs had been used to silence women and conceal information in the public interest. She added that this information may still be hidden.

In closing, Wynn-Williams noted that people in very different positions sign NDAs, from senior executives to gig workers, interns, content moderators, production assistants, junior public relations specialists and stylists. Often, she said, people sign when they are especially vulnerable: they have a mortgage to pay, and a box of family photos is already in the car after they have been fired.

Dataist’s take: The dispute is about whether people inside tech companies can speak out about risks when contracts restrict their right to do so. Burnham’s proposed changes are meant to protect victims of crime and workplace harassment, but Wynn-Williams points to the limits of that measure: without retroactive effect, people already bound by NDAs may still have no way to speak.
