# Nvidia faces questions over Jensen Huang’s daughter and executive departures

## Rapid rise and colleagues’ complaints

Scrutiny of how Jensen Huang ran Nvidia intensified amid a scandal over his alleged connection to a Chinese businesswoman whom US authorities suspect of espionage. A separate question is Madison’s rapid rise at the company and her actual role alongside her father.

According to reports by The New York Times and The Wall Street Journal, Madison often raised her voice at employees during group meetings, including at executives. If someone could not recall the information she wanted, sources told the publications, she called them a “dead face” or a “goldfish.” Madison also criticized senior executives who had worked at the company for decades.

She also scolded her 36-year-old brother Spencer, who works at Nvidia as well. The report describes him as quiet-natured.

Before joining Nvidia, Madison studied culinary arts. She started at the company as an intern in 2020. Her rapid rise, along with the career advancement of her husband, Nico Caprez, caused tension among employees.

## Influence inside the company

According to the publications, complaints about Madison’s treatment of colleagues did not lead to action by HR. One anonymous employee told The Wall Street Journal that Madison and Jensen Huang’s other children were treated like “royalty.”

Huang himself was also known as a demanding manager, but reports say he combined that with personal charm. He knew his daughter had a temper and, The New York Times writes, sometimes threatened other employees that he would sic an enraged Madison on them. Even senior executives, the publication reports, were wary of contradicting her because she had direct access to her father.

One episode took place ahead of a conference in New Delhi. Huang withdrew at the last minute and asked longtime associate Jay Puri to go in his place. Madison accompanied her father and handled his speaking engagements; she reprimanded Puri for being unprepared. Puri replied that he was willing to answer her questions if she stopped shouting at him. According to The New York Times, he left Nvidia less than six months later.

In total, the publications’ authors linked Madison to the departure of more than a dozen senior executives. Harvard Business School professor David Yoffie, who has served on the boards of more than a dozen companies, told The New York Times that nepotism is almost always a bad idea and rarely works even moderately well.

Dataist’s take: The reported incidents point to a possible gap between Madison’s formal title and her actual influence at Nvidia.
