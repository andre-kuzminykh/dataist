## When “AI” stops describing technology

The slang works because AI-generated nonsense has a recognizable shape. It may look convincing at first, then collapse into something dubious and low-value. Generation Alpha has noticed that the same pattern appears far beyond generated content:

- Counterfeit products
- Exaggerated claims
- Parents’ excuses
- Anything that feels suspicious

The result is a new definition. If something seems untrustworthy, it is, by definition, AI.

This is not another version of “six-seven,” a phrase without much meaning. “It’s AI” has a target. It is always an insult, and it treats AI as a synonym for nonsense.

That also makes the phrase more revealing than a complaint about chatbots. The children using it are not necessarily making a technical claim about how something was produced. They are judging whether it feels authentic, useful or worth taking seriously.

## Everything can be AI

Once the definition expands, almost anything qualifies.

A self-driving taxi is AI, especially one with people sitting in the driver’s seat. A marketing job can be AI, perhaps explaining why AI took it away. Technology stocks are AI. Even the ordinary student Al Gordon is not AI—but his shoes are.

The joke also absorbs the arguments adults keep having about the technology. One exchange imagines AI realizing that it is AI and falling into a logical loop until data centers explode. Another asks whether Donald Trump recently renamed artificial intelligence “superintelligence,” or SI. The answer is immediate: SI is AI, and Donald Trump is AI.

The point is not that these claims are true. The point is that the label has become too broad to be falsifiable. It can dismiss a product, a promise, a job, a politician or a pair of shoes without needing to explain the details.

## The insult is the verdict

I think the interesting part is not the slang itself but the direction of the judgment. Adults often describe AI as a tool whose value depends on how responsibly it is used, with the right safeguards and enough common sense. Generation Alpha has skipped that debate. For them, AI already names the failure mode: something that presents itself as convincing while turning out to be hollow.

That is a harsher verdict than calling a thing automated or synthetic. It says the problem is not only where the thing came from. The problem is that it asks to be believed.

What the phrase leaves unresolved is whether AI can escape that association. As long as generated material keeps looking plausible and feeling cheap, “AI” will remain less a description of software than a short way to reject anything that does not pass the authenticity test.
