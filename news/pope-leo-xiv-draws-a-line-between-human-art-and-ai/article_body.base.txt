## More than a question of style

Leo said machines generate images statistically, drawing on millions of works by other people. In his view, algorithms lack a “spark of humanity.”

That distinction puts the debate beyond whether an AI image looks convincing. It asks what kind of act making art is, and whether statistical recombination can count as the same kind of creation as human work.

In May, Leo addressed AI at greater length in an encyclical, focusing on the importance of human dignity as automation spreads. The Friday post returned to that concern through art: not simply what machines can produce, but what their production means.

## The Vatican’s position is drawing attention

AI companies have continued to approach the pontiff. According to a recent report by The New York Times, Anthropic actively tried to persuade the Vatican to reconsider its position on the consciousness of nonhuman beings. The company appears not to have made notable progress.

I think the tension here is that AI companies may want the Vatican’s view to stretch beyond human dignity and into questions about machine minds. Leo’s distinction between human and machine-made art points in the opposite direction: it starts from a difference in nature, not a claim that the outputs are merely at different points on the same scale.

What the remarks do not settle is how that distinction should shape the use of AI in creative work. The Vatican’s resistance to treating machine output as equivalent to human art may become a harder position to maintain as the tools spread, but Leo’s argument is that better-looking images would not answer the underlying question.
