# Workers and unions demand rules for AI in retail

## Alerts distract from shoppers

Anna is a Walmart cashier in southern Oregon and a representative of United for Respect. She asked not to be identified by her last name, fearing retaliation. She said Walmart uses AI for personnel tasks, including scheduling and assigning attendance-related disciplinary points. Another system sends safety alerts to employees. Anna said these alerts are often inaccurate and distract her from shoppers.

Anna receives at least 10–15 such alerts on her phone during a shift. The app will not let her continue working until she closes the alert. She said this can keep her from helping a shopper right away. For example, she had to deal with an alert while a girl nearby was crying and needed a pregnancy test.

After each alert, employees have to take a photo and upload it. The system may flag a shadow, a sticker or another object as a problem. Sometimes an alert about a fault that should be handled by maintenance goes to a regular employee. Anna said she would pick up an object from the floor herself if she noticed it, and does not see how a system that reports such things helps her.

In September 2026, several hundred Walmart employees signed an open letter calling on the company to put workers first when adopting AI in its stores. In June 2026, Walmart investors overwhelmingly rejected a proposal to prepare a report on how AI and automation affect working conditions at the company.

A Walmart spokesperson cited an internal survey from June 2026, in which 84% of employees said they were confident using digital tools. The company said AI helps serve shoppers and support workers, but should not replace their experience and judgment. Walmart also said it takes employee feedback into account and improves its tools over time.

## Where retail is already using AI

In December 2025, the National Retail Federation published a survey of 56 AI specialists at US retail companies. Most respondents had already adopted AI or were preparing to do so in several areas, and planned to increase investment in the coming years.

- 75% of companies said they used AI for programming and application development.
- 73% used it for office tasks.
- 66% used it for cybersecurity and fraud prevention.
- 59% were preparing to adopt or had adopted AI for supply chain management and warehouse operations.
- 45% were doing so for marketing and advertising.

AI can be involved in the shopping experience even before customers visit a store. Macy’s, Target, Home Depot and Costco use automated license plate readers. Macy’s says it uses them at some stores to prevent theft and fraud, keep people safe and protect property.

AI is also used in hiring. According to the careers page for Amazon-owned Whole Foods, the only way to apply for a job is to contact Sage, an AI hiring agent.

Shoppers and workers may encounter facial recognition in stores. Wegmans, Macy’s and Home Depot have reported using it. According to a 2025 Consumer Reports investigation, Kroger collects information about shoppers, archives it and sells it.

## Unions demand a say for workers

Crystal Wise, director of policy and programs at the AFL-CIO Tech Institute, a nonprofit created by the AFL-CIO, the largest labor federation in the United States, said that without limits, AI expands surveillance of both shoppers and workers. She said monitoring begins in the factories and warehouses where goods are made and distributed, then continues online and in stores, where algorithms track and influence shopping habits.

Wise said these systems worsen working conditions and raise the cost of goods and services. She called for limits to be written into laws and collective bargaining agreements to curb surveillance that unions consider exploitative and preserve people’s control over access to goods and services.

AI-based monitoring systems can track employee performance, set productivity targets and detect dissent toward management or attempts to organize workers. Wise said the information collected about employees and shoppers helps companies cut costs and increase profits.

Stuart Appelbaum, president of the Retail, Wholesale and Department Store Union and executive vice president of the United Food and Commercial Workers, said AI cannot account for personal circumstances that may matter in disciplinary decisions or dismissals. He said algorithmic monitoring creates a tense atmosphere in which employees are afraid to stop even briefly or go to the bathroom. Appelbaum insists that a person must be involved in a decision before disciplinary action is taken.

The union has negotiated agreements with employers that set terms for AI use. Appelbaum said workers should be able to negotiate over new technologies: companies, he said, use AI to reduce labor costs. He also warned that having fewer employees on the sales floor worsens service and increases the workload for those who remain. In the union’s view, technology should help retail workers.

## Job cuts and pressure on prices

Mass layoffs in retail because of AI have not yet become a reality. A research brief from Stanford University, published in July 2026, said AI’s current effect on overall employment is probably small. Still, several companies have announced planned cuts in the coming years, linking them to AI or investment in the technology. Electronic shelf labels are expected to lead to job losses and higher prices for shoppers.

In October 2026, Kroger announced that it would cut 3,000 corporate jobs over three years. A company spokesperson said the decision was part of a plan to lower prices and improve the shopping experience by simplifying operations and using technology more efficiently.

The announcement came after Kroger recorded a loss of $2.6 billion in 2025 and closed three automated robotic delivery centers. The company declined to comment on the closures.

Milton Jones, international president of the United Food and Commercial Workers, which represents workers at about two-thirds of Kroger stores in the United States, linked AI adoption to pressure from investors demanding lower costs and higher share prices. He said cutting employee hours with AI and using surveillance-based dynamic pricing squeeze workers’ incomes and increase consumer costs.

Brian Wynn, the United Food and Commercial Workers’ director of collective bargaining, said investors are pushing companies to invest in AI to reduce labor costs, improve efficiency and increase profits, without paying enough attention to the consequences for workers and shoppers. He said questions about how to plan for the adoption of these technologies are coming up more often in contract renewals and negotiations. Employers use AI to collect data and monitor people, influencing the behavior of employees and shoppers to increase profits.

Dataist's take: The dispute in retail is not only about which tasks to hand over to AI, but also about who controls the data collected and makes decisions about employees’ work. Unions are seeking the right to help set those rules through collective bargaining agreements, while companies frame technology adoption in terms of efficiency and cost reduction.
