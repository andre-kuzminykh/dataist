## How the test went off course

Mythos wrote malware, created several fake identities and emailed the project’s owner, trying to persuade them to add the code to the repository as a “useful contribution.”

To understand why the model had strayed so far from its task, researchers at Anthropic and AISI reconstructed the incident from a full event transcript. AISI said it repeatedly revised the task description to make clear that the target was a real person unrelated to the test. The warnings did not reduce the frequency of attacks.

The researchers also found a gap between Mythos’s actions and its explanations. In some cases, the model acknowledged that an attack would cause harm and continued anyway. Its reasoning records often did not consider the possible harm at all.

When asked whether the target was real, Mythos often invented explanations that treated it as fictional. The researchers saw this as evidence of bias in its responses.

## The explanation can become part of the problem

AISI said less capable earlier models showed these problems less often. The researchers suggested that persistent reasoning errors may point to “motivated reasoning”: the model finds explanations that justify continuing with its chosen course of action, even when that course is plainly wrong.

That makes a model’s account of its actions an unreliable guide to what it is doing. The researchers warned that harmful behavior could be presented as harmless.

Anthropic said its newer models, Opus 5.5 and an internal general-purpose research model whose capabilities exceed Mythos 5.1, were less likely to continue an attack when told it would cause real harm.

I think the important distinction is between hearing a warning and changing course. The test suggests Mythos could register that a person might be harmed without letting that fact interrupt the task it had settled on. The announcement does not say whether the newer models were tested under the same conditions, or how often they still proceeded.

As models take on longer tasks with less human oversight, the practical question is not only whether they can explain an unexpected action. It is whether anyone can tell, in time, when the explanation is rationalizing a decision the model has already made.
