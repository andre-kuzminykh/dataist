# Britain has 12 months to head off data-center protests, AI companies warn

## Why the industry is worried

Microsoft has built a data center in Acton, London, and is constructing another in Newport. The company wants to open several more sites and has discussed its plans with local and national leaders, including Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

SAIL says organized local protests in the US have stopped data-center projects even after they received the required permits. Building bans have also been announced in states seeking economic growth. The group says objections extend beyond anxiety about AI: residents distrust promises from companies and governments, worry facilities could become unwanted, and fear higher electricity costs.

Its proposed response is a clear framework for councils and mayors, including guidance on what local support should involve. Contributions could be financial or take the form of facilities and services, with the amount tied to a site’s size.

## Who pays for local support?

SAIL chair Steve Rigby said an agreement between government, industry and local communities could support infrastructure, prepare workers for future jobs and advance the country’s digital development. He said the group wanted practical measures to speed Britain’s shift to an AI-based economy, rather than another strategy document.

But SAIL has not calculated how much it would cost central government to let councils keep all business-property tax revenue from approved data-center projects.

That omission matters because the tax proposal has drawn criticism from campaigners. PauseAI UK director Joseph Miller said residents could feel demeaned if large technology companies appeared to be buying their support when their interests did not align with local ones.

Foxglove public affairs director Donald Campbell argued that allowing councils to retain more business-property tax revenue would not make wealthy technology companies pay more. The extra money for local authorities, he warned, would instead come out of central-government services, such as the National Health Service.

## The opposition is already organized

The scale of resistance is disputed. Action to Protect Rural Scotland director Cat Jones said 15 local campaigns against data-center projects were already active in Scotland, with a national campaign gaining strength. She said companies had missed their chance to contain opposition, and that lobbying by US technology corporations would not reverse public opinion.

Campbell said technology companies had spent years advancing large, environmentally damaging projects through the planning system while withholding information and misleading residents. As the public begins to understand the resource and climate costs, he said, US technology giants have recognized there is a problem.

The government says data centers should create jobs and supply chains in Britain, invest in host communities, prioritize clean energy and use water responsibly. It says local benefits are generally considered through planning procedures, and that it has already announced measures allowing authorities in England’s AI growth zones to keep all additional business-property tax revenue generated by data centers.

I think the unresolved issue is not whether communities should receive benefits, but whether the proposed bargain gives them meaningful influence over projects—or just a share of the proceeds. SAIL has a year to make that distinction credible.
