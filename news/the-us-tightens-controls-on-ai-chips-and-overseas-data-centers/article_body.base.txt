The Donald Trump administration is preparing new restrictions on exports of AI chips and servers to data centers outside the United States. Under an interim rule, companies will need licenses if their equipment could provide computing capacity to organizations in China and other countries subject to US restrictions. The rule covers data centers and networks with more than 12 million TPP — roughly equivalent to the peak performance of 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips. It also introduces new requirements for chip shipments to five countries and rescinds restrictions adopted under Joe Biden in January 2025.

## The US will tighten export controls on AI chips and overseas data centers

## Access to computing from abroad

The US Department of Commerce will require licenses to export, re-export, or transfer AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if they could provide computing capacity to companies in China or other restricted countries. The new rules are intended to prevent companies from circumventing export controls by remotely connecting to infrastructure in third countries.

The restrictions apply to organizations based in Group D:5 countries: China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and 18 other states. The US already requires licenses for shipments of AI chips to companies located in these countries or owned by organizations registered there.

The interim rule applies to data centers and networks with a combined computing capacity of more than 12 million TPP. The US uses TPP to assess computing power; 12 million TPP is roughly equivalent to the peak performance of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips.

Indirect access to computing capacity, through intermediaries or brokers, is also covered. Exporters and data center operators must monitor the ownership structure and activities of their customers to ensure they are not controlled by people from Group D:5 countries.

Existing data center operators whose services fall under the new rule must obtain licenses within 90 days of its taking effect. Commercial recommendation systems — such as those used in e-commerce and social networks — are excluded from the rule.

## Additional restrictions for five countries

New licensing requirements also apply to exports, re-exports, or transfers of advanced semiconductors to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil. The Department of Commerce said it selected these countries because the risk of shipments being diverted from them is higher than usual.

No license will be required for these five countries if the chips are intended for US-based companies.

## The Biden rule will be rescinded

The new document rescinds the AI Diffusion Rule adopted by the Joe Biden administration in January 2025. It set country-specific restrictions on exports of advanced AI chips and banned the transfer of some powerful AI models.

In May, the Trump administration said it would abandon the system, saying it imposed unnecessary restrictions on US companies and allies.

The interim rule says its restrictions will affect the use of certain components and computing capacity suitable for training and running advanced models. According to the document, access to such capacity could help create and run models for military and intelligence tasks that threaten US national security or foreign policy interests.

## Concerns about circumvention prompted the move

The decision came days after Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang resigned amid a scandal involving an alleged Chinese spy linked to one of the company’s customers. Media reports said the US government wanted to add the customer, Megaspeed, to a special list of organizations. Authorities suspect the company bought thousands of Nvidia chips and provided computing capacity to Chinese businesses, helping them circumvent existing restrictions.

Last week, Reuters reported that Chinese AI companies were racing to secure computing capacity overseas, fearing further tightening of US rules.

Dataist’s take: The new requirements shift the focus from the shipment of chips itself to who ultimately gets access to computing capacity and through which intermediaries. Data center operators will have to check ownership and customers, while the exemption for commercial recommendation systems marks one boundary of the rule’s scope.
