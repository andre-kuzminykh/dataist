## The data problem

The investigation examined nearly 600 towers, compared with the 800 the government says are operating. Reporters reconstructed tower locations using mapping data from the Electronic Frontier Foundation and checked individual sites and approximate installation dates against satellite imagery.

That reconstruction was necessary because CBP does not publish a complete map of the “virtual wall.” It also means the estimate of more than 1,050 deaths near towers is probably low, and that the most dangerous stretches of the border cannot yet be identified with confidence.

New Mexico state representative Sarah Silva called for a federal audit after MIT Technology Review told her that more than 18 people had died in 2024 near surveillance towers on a small stretch of desert close to her district. She said the system appears not to be working.

CBP should be able to conduct a more complete review than outside reporters can. In theory, it tracks when each tower was installed and when it was operating, and could compare tower performance with deaths nearby as far back as 2015, when this type of equipment began to be installed. Sources told the reporters that CBP has never conducted such an audit.

## Four missing controls

The investigation points to four practical repairs.

1. **Audit deaths near the towers.** A federal review should establish where and when deaths occurred, which towers were nearby, and whether the pattern is concentrated in particular areas.

2. **Fix the death records.** No national agency collects and publishes all available information on migrant deaths. Reporters reviewed thousands of pages from dozens of agencies in Texas and still found an incomplete picture because public records have no common protocol for documenting these deaths.

3. **Track technology-assisted arrests.** In 2014, the Government Accountability Office recommended recording every case in which technology helped agents detain someone attempting to enter the U.S. CBP agreed to require the reporting, but GAO found in 2017 that the data were inaccurate and unreliable. Texas agents had identified one type of tower hundreds of times even though that type was not present in the state.

4. **Treat deaths as possible surveillance failures.** When a body is found, CBP should check whether the person was within the field of view of a tower or another surveillance system, determine what the system recorded and review how agents responded.

The recordkeeping failure extends beyond the towers. The Border Patrol has operated the Border Safety Initiative Tracking System, or BSITS, since 2007, but nationwide data-entry rules for different sectors did not arrive until 2021. GAO reported the following year that many cases still did not enter the system, particularly deaths first discovered by someone other than the Border Patrol.

CBP’s Missing Migrant Program, launched in 2017, improved the collection and tracking of 911 calls, rescue operations and other events, according to GAO in April 2025. But it still lacked a plan to determine whether the program was increasing rescues or reducing deaths. GAO proposed two ways to fix that gap; neither has been implemented.

## A system that measures expansion, not effect

The same pattern appears in the tower data. In August 2021, more than seven years after agreeing to evaluate how surveillance technology contributed to arrests, CBP still had not produced the analysis. GAO says the agency completed it in 2022 but did not publish the results.

Agents told reporters that documenting technology-assisted arrests remains a low priority. A former CBP employee called the requirement useless, arguing that the messy reality of arrests does not translate neatly into clean metrics. The source, who worked during the administration of Jamie Biden, asked not to be named and was not authorized to discuss confidential matters.

That is more than an administrative flaw. <strong>The virtual wall is growing faster than anyone is measuring its effect.</strong>

My read is that this makes the $1 billion expansion difficult to evaluate on its own terms. The government can count towers, but the public cannot reliably compare their deployment with rescues, deaths or arrests. The missing result is not a better promotional metric; it is evidence of whether the system changes outcomes.

The quietest part of the story is the absence of a failure log. CBP does not routinely disclose how often a tower should have seen someone, whether it issued an alert, or whether agents acted on one. Without that information, a death near a tower can remain a border tragedy rather than becoming evidence about a system that taxpayers are being asked to enlarge.

## Preserve the evidence

CBP should automatically compare every migrant death with its tower database and open a separate review for deaths inside a surveillance area. Agents already record GPS coordinates when they detain a group, find tracks and document other events, so the basic location data exists.

At present, officers are not required to check whether a recovered body was close to surveillance infrastructure, and the agency has no standard procedure for doing so. That leaves potentially important evidence unexplored.

The timing matters. CBP generally deletes tower video after 30 days unless an internal investigation has begun. Those investigations are most often opened after the death of someone who was in custody. Registering deaths near towers would at least preserve the relevant footage for investigators and give families a way to request it.

The investigation found several deaths in one New Mexico area that occurred in view of a modern tower equipped with AI. They happened over several months, sometimes only a few hundred feet apart. A formal review could determine whether the camera repeatedly failed to recognize people in a specific location, or whether it issued alerts that agents did not act on.

That is the distinction the current system cannot make: whether the cameras saw people and no one came, or whether the system never detected them at all. Until CBP records that difference after every relevant death, building a larger virtual wall will produce more infrastructure without producing accountability.
