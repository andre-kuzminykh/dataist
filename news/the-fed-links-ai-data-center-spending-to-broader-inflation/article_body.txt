# The Fed sees AI data-center construction adding to inflation

## From chips to the wider economy

An economy overheats when demand outstrips its capacity to produce. Prices rise, and the resulting squeeze can weaken demand and consumption; in the worst case, overheating can lead to recession. Goolsbee said that this kind of demand pressure builds slowly but is difficult to stop once it has taken hold.

Food prices have not yet been directly affected, but economists are asking how long that can last. It is also unclear whether higher interest rates can meaningfully slow inflation. More expensive borrowing usually restrains consumer spending, but that may not be enough now.

The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in three years, as inflation proved difficult to contain. Reuters reported that futures markets expect another increase later this month.

## The costs beyond data centers

The tens of billions of dollars going into AI data centers are beginning to overshadow deeper problems in the US economy, adding to fears about what could follow if the AI bubble bursts. The construction boom is already affecting other sectors:

- Consumer electronics are becoming nearly unaffordable.
- Housing construction faces a shortage of skilled workers.

If inflation persists, Goolsbee said, the Fed will have to keep raising rates. Yet technology companies show no sign of cutting their enormous spending. That leaves the central bank facing a difficult trade-off: rate increases intended to cool demand could also make investment more expensive for the AI companies driving part of the boom.

I think the announcement is quiet about how much of this inflation is temporary construction pressure and how much reflects demand that will persist. That distinction matters: if the spending continues while prices spread into everyday goods and labor, the Fed’s usual brake may hit both the wider economy and the AI buildout at once.
