As technology stocks and AI-related companies sell off, the S&P 500 is down 11.5% from its peak, while the Global X AI & Technology ETF (AIQ) has fallen 23.9%. Bill Stone, a Forbes contributor who writes about investing, markets and Berkshire Hathaway, argues that investors should not treat recent gains as the norm or assume the current decline must continue. What matters more is assessing companies’ profitability, their reliance on AI spending and their ability to weather a slowdown in that spending.

# The AI stock sell-off does not erase the market’s long-term prospects

## Why trends can mislead

Nassim Taleb explained the idea of a “black swan” with the example of a turkey. It is fed and protected every day, so its past experience leads it to expect that its safety will continue. But the risk to the turkey peaks just before Thanksgiving. Stone applies the metaphor to the market: it is dangerous to assume that a trend is obvious and easy money is business as usual.

Capitalism creates wealth, but it rests on fierce competition: weaker companies give way to others. Berkshire Hathaway grew out of a struggling textile business and a department store. Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger redirected capital from a loss-making business to acquire other companies.

People tend to look for patterns and give too much weight to recent events when trying to predict the future. In Your Money & Your Brain, Jason Zweig notes that pattern-seeking helped people make sense of a world governed by physical causes and effects: for example, thunder follows lightning. In financial markets, that habit can lead to mistakes.

Data supports the idea that momentum and trend-following strategies can work. But this is not an unchanging law: the signal weakens and can reverse. Such strategies require systematic application across the entire portfolio and discipline when selling assets. The urge to get rid of a falling stock or buy what has become fashionable often does little for long-term wealth.

## Technology stocks took a harder hit

Technology companies and AI-related businesses were at the center of last week’s sell-off. From its October 6 peak, the S&P 500 fell 11.5%, while AIQ lost 23.9%. The average stock in the index declined just 2.3% over the same period.

That gap reflects the S&P 500’s high concentration: the ten largest companies account for 38.6% of its market capitalization, and Stone classifies eight of them as technology companies.

Semiconductor makers were hit hardest. The problems extended beyond NVIDIA: since their October 6 peaks, AMD shares have fallen 34.7%, while SanDisk shares are down 61.8%. Semiconductor companies benefited from the boom in AI spending and are therefore especially vulnerable if that spending slows or export restrictions tighten substantially.

## Valuations and company profits

After the market decline, the S&P 500’s forward price-to-earnings ratio moved close to its median level. Earnings growth expectations remain resilient, however, and projected returns on equity are high. Historically, high returns on equity have usually gone hand in hand with higher company valuations.

Stone compares today’s market with the technology bubble of 2000. Back then, internet companies often delivered high stock returns without making a profit. US companies are more profitable now. The technology sector is one of the main sources of the S&P 500’s high profitability, and its free cash flow has grown especially quickly.

Before the sell-off, free cash flow yields were falling: stocks were getting more expensive as companies invested large sums in AI. After share prices fell, the measure improved. The consensus forecast points to further growth in free cash flow.

## Long-term growth and corrections

Despite the downturn, Stone remains optimistic about American businesses and stocks. As a long-term example, he points to the Dow Jones Industrial Average’s growth in the 20th century: the index rose from 66 to 11,497 points, a gain of 17,320%. That happened despite four costly wars, the Great Depression and numerous recessions; investors also received substantial dividends.

Since the start of 2019, even after the latest decline, the S&P 500 has risen 211%, the S&P 500 technology sector nearly 419%, and NVIDIA 3,661%. At the same time, NVIDIA shares have nearly halved since October 6. Since 1928, the S&P 500’s average annual total return has been 9.7%, despite wars and recessions.

Stone also examines 22 corrections in the S&P 500 since 1980. He defines a correction as a decline of at least 10% from a peak. The median return after such declines was 5% over the following three months, 12.1% over six months and 18.1% over 12 months.

The main factor shaping what happens next is whether a recession begins within 12 months of a correction. Over the following year, the worst result without a recession was −7.3%, compared with −34.8% when a recession occurred; the best results were attractive in both cases. The market rose in 93% of the cases examined when there was no recession, and in 57% when the economy contracted.

On average, declines last five months, with corrections that do not coincide with a recession usually shorter. The longest was a 30-month drawdown during the 2000 technology bubble. The shortest was the 2018 correction, which lasted less than a month. At the time of publication, the current downturn had lasted 1.5 months.

Interest-rate markets did not indicate a substantial increase in the probability of a recession by the end of 2027. Stone says that is consistent with resilient data on the US economy. If a recession can be avoided, he estimates that the market downturn is probably closer to its end than its beginning.

## How to assess AI-related companies

Stone advises investors to understand exactly what they own and how dependent a company’s profits are on the AI spending cycle. A slowdown in that spending could be significant. High net leverage magnifies gains during an upswing but also increases the risk of serious consequences if conditions deteriorate.

Stone names CoreWeave and Oracle as companies with elevated net leverage. In his assessment, Amazon, Meta Platforms, Microsoft and Alphabet have low leverage, and their substantial free cash flow strengthens their balance sheets. Amazon’s free cash flow over the past 12 months was negative, but the author attributes that to its capital spending cycle rather than to a permanent feature of the business.

For NVIDIA, Stone points to risks related to leadership changes and regulation. At the same time, he describes the company’s balance sheet as one of the strongest among large technology companies: NVIDIA has negative net debt and substantial free cash flow.

The main lesson for investors is to assess business quality and earnings prospects, rather than relying only on share price movements or an appealing forecast. After the technology bubble burst, even Amazon shares fell in value: they dropped to $0.30 and did not return to their 1999 high until 2009. But the ability to avoid bankruptcy matters for a subsequent recovery.

Stone considers many of today’s largest technology companies exceptional businesses: for companies of their size, they have unusually high profitability and rapid earnings growth. In his view, improving fundamentals and profits from AI use explain much of their outperformance since 2019.

Rather than simply declaring the situation a bubble, the author suggests watching the future profitability of technology leaders as capital spending on AI infrastructure grows. The recent fall in AI-related stocks has eased pressure on their valuations. The price paid for a quality business affects future returns.

Stone draws on Warren Buffett and Benjamin Graham’s The Intelligent Investor as the basis for his approach, especially Chapters 8 and 20. Chapter 8 calls for treating stocks as ownership stakes in businesses, rather than merely as securities, and not reacting to short-term market fluctuations. Chapter 20 sets out the principles of value investing: buying shares below their estimated value.

The book defines a company’s value as the present value of the money its owners will receive. Buying with a sufficient margin of safety should produce an acceptable result even if the business valuation is not entirely accurate.

## AI spending may grow more slowly

Stone believes the current situation looks more like a shift to slower growth in AI capital spending than the end of the cycle. Companies continue to adopt AI, and spending on AI services is growing. Demand for those services is therefore unlikely to fall, though its growth rate may slow.

Regulatory changes could constrain data center expansion, but the author believes demand for computing power will remain resilient. Glenview Trust’s base-case scenario assumes there will be no recession. Downside risks remain, however: AI spending has provided significant support to the economy.

The market is trying to assess how much AI investment will slow and how that will affect the profits of companies that depend on such investment. Companies counting on spending to ramp up quickly may face pressure on their balance sheets. At the same time, the revaluation of AI-related stocks gives investors more opportunity to be compensated for risk if they choose the right technology companies.
