# Nvidia’s parallels with Enron deepened after Jensen Huang’s return

## Parallels with Enron’s history

A year ago, Nvidia rejected investor accusations that it was propping up an AI bubble through questionable financing schemes, saying it was nothing like Enron. The Houston-based energy corporation went bankrupt in 2001 after some of the largest accounting frauds in history came to light.

In late 2001, Nvidia took Enron’s place in the S&P 500—just as Enron began bankruptcy proceedings. The coincidence proves nothing on its own, but the two companies’ histories also intersect in the circumstances surrounding their leaders.

In the summer of 2001, as questions arose about Enron’s accounting and its stock plunged, CEO Jeffrey Skilling abruptly resigned. The company’s founder, Kenneth Lay, was seen as his successor. Lay returned to the helm of Enron and assured Wall Street that everything would be all right. Skilling had held the job for just six months.

On Lay’s first day back, hundreds of Enron employees gave him a standing ovation. Lay insisted that Skilling’s departure had “absolutely nothing to do with any accounting problems.” It later emerged that this was untrue: Enron filed for bankruptcy four months later.

## Huang’s return and questions about Nvidia

Jensen Huang was also welcomed as a hero this week. Hundreds of employees gathered on the staircases and balconies in the lobby of Nvidia’s headquarters in Santa Clara, applauding him and chanting his name. Among the tech executives pleased by his return were Sam Altman and Elon Musk: they are counting on the company getting back to normal with Huang at the helm.

One reason for the Enron comparisons is that both companies used legal structures known as special purpose entities. But the allegations are different. Enron used these structures to conceal its true financial position and report income it had not earned.

Nvidia, by contrast, does earn substantial revenue from selling sought-after AI chips. The questions concern what share of its revenue may have been tied to illegal sales to Chinese companies, and whether Nvidia concealed those transactions. Concerns are amplified by the company’s role in supporting the AI boom and the global economy: if that growth turns out to be a bubble, the fallout could affect Nvidia and other connected sectors.

Nvidia may turn out to be nothing like Enron. But if the AI market really is a bubble, the consequences of its collapse could be even more serious.

**Dataist’s take:** For now, the parallels between the companies are limited to coincidences in their leadership histories and certain financial mechanisms; the source does not claim that Nvidia committed fraud on Enron’s scale. The main risk discussed is possible illegal sales to China and dependence on the AI boom.
