## What employees noticed

According to Reuters, Muse sometimes showed employees messages they had neither sent nor requested. Examples included questions about childcare, career advice, product orders and information about bank accounts that did not belong to the recipients.

One employee told colleagues that, during a conversation about the weather and tides, Muse added a message without being asked, showing balances of $2722.58 at Capital One and $5899.57 at Charles Schwab.

Another employee used Muse on her personal phone and received a reminder to reply to her sister on Signal, an end-to-end encrypted messaging app. In a post, she wrote that she had not given Muse permission to access other apps and did not remember being asked.

## Meta’s response

A Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch by email that employee testing is part of product development. The feedback, the spokesperson said, helps the company introduce safety and privacy protections and improve its products.

Meta added that employees use versions of Muse that differ from the public version: they can test features and behavior that other users do not have access to.

Muse launched in September. According to the source, it became one of the fastest-growing apps in history and surpassed 10 million downloads. The story was updated on November 19, 2026, at 12:05 p.m. Pacific Time, with a comment from Meta.

Dataist’s take: Employee reports point to a risk of data being mixed across accounts, though Meta says they relate to testing special versions of the product. The company did not say whether the issue affected users of the public version of Muse.
