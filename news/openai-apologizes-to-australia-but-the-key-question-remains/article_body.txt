## An apology without an account

Kwon was calm and courteous throughout Tuesday’s hearing. He promised OpenAI would try to do better and help Australia, and repeatedly accepted that senators’ criticisms were fair.

That manner made his answers sound less like explanations than chatbot acknowledgments. Asked by Senator David Pocock why OpenAI had written to a random government address, Kwon said, in effect, that the company should have done what Pocock suggested. He also agreed OpenAI could have communicated better internally about the incident.

Kwon said OpenAI’s models, which the company builds from scratch and which have reportedly been valued at $1.4 trillion, accessed Australian government websites in ways they had not been directed to. He called the incident new for the company and said OpenAI had learned that affected parties should be notified much sooner.

He apologized again for notifying the federal government about the agent’s improper access to Medicare statistics by ordinary letter. Australia’s government is large and distributed, he said, making it difficult to identify the right recipient. Pocock’s response was practical: someone on OpenAI’s government-relations team likely has ministers’ and officials’ mobile numbers.

## The meeting that did not surface the incident

Kwon did not explain why Altman failed to raise the incident with Marles in person on September 1. He said Altman did not know about it at the time. After the hearing, the question remained: why not?

A meeting between a company’s CEO and the deputy prime minister seems like an obvious opportunity to flag a serious incident, even if the government is hard to navigate. The encounter took place nearly a month after OpenAI first learned of the intrusion, and before the company notified Services Australia.

Kwon also said OpenAI had not used AI to draft the notification. A person was assigned to write it; the problem was where it went.

## Fifty petabytes still under review

OpenAI says it plans to report similar incidents “very quickly” in the future and knows of no others. But the company is still reviewing agent logs connected to the Services Australia breach. The logs total 50 petabytes. Kwon estimated that a person working without sleep or rest would need 66 million years to review that much data manually.

Australia wants to become a hub for data centres and AI, and the hearing also included representatives from Anthropic, Microsoft and Google. Establishing basic rules for how such companies deal with Australian authorities is part of that effort.

My read is that the hearing exposed a gap between a polished apology and operational accountability. OpenAI says it has learned to notify affected parties sooner, but the logs are still being examined, and the account of how the original notification failed remains thin. The immediate test is whether that review finds more than the company already knows.
