In a Zillow listing, a three-bedroom house in Cheboygan, Michigan, looks like a well-kept cottage with a stone walkway and a spacious deck. But photos of the interior and back of the building show an abandoned shack filled with trash, water damage and a huge hole in the wall. The seller is asking $80,800; in January 2020, the house was unsuccessfully listed for $23,000. The listing explains that some images are concepts of a future renovation, but the finished images bear little resemblance to the actual building.

## What the photos show

Images presented as showing the house after renovations depict a craftsman-style facade with two dormer windows, a manicured lawn, a flagstone path and a large deck with a rocking chair. The interiors also look cozy, with warm lighting.

[FIGURE:1]

The building’s actual condition is very different: trash is scattered inside, and drywall is falling from the ceiling, a sign of serious water damage. At a neighboring shack, part of the structure appears to have been brought down by an overgrown tree. Another angle shows a huge hole in the back wall, roughly the size of a double doorway. The house looks nothing like the one in the altered images and, according to the source, would not even fit on the same foundation.

[FIGURE:2]

The seller describes the property as a renovation opportunity and says the current owner started restoring it but cannot finish because of health problems. The house is being sold “as is” and needs further work. The listing also says the photos show the building before renovations, during the work and as concepts illustrating what it could look like; the concept images are intended only for inspiration and do not reflect completed work.

[FIGURE:3]

Among possible uses, the seller lists a hobby farm, a smallholding, keeping animals, a petting zoo or other rural projects.

## Reaction and rules for listings

Using generative AI to alter real estate photos has happened before, but in this case the images diverge especially sharply from reality. Social media users were outraged by the listing. One Reddit user questioned whether it was real; another wrote that misrepresenting a home’s condition with fake photos should be considered fraud. Some commenters noted that people searching for housing often come across images that show only a property’s potential.

In another case, a real estate agent accidentally uploaded an altered bathroom image in which a demonic figure seemed to appear in the mirror.

It remains unclear whether state or federal authorities will start restricting the practice. Some states have already introduced requirements to disclose digital alterations to photos: such rules are in effect in California, for example, and are due to take effect in Wisconsin on January 1, 2027. In the Canadian province of Manitoba, a plan to ban AI-generated images in real estate listings was introduced this week.

Dataist’s take: Labeling altered photos could help buyers distinguish a home’s actual condition from its projected future appearance. But the Zillow example shows just how wide the gap can be between a concept image and the real property: the listing explicitly warns that renovations are unfinished, while regulation is still taking shape across different regions.
