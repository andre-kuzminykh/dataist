# Dario Amodei discussed video games as a way to contain protests over AI

## From video games to basic income

Dario Amodei’s views were described in a profile in The Wall Street Journal about him and his sister Daniela, who also holds a senior role at Anthropic. The outlet reported that the scientist, now a tech billionaire, once saw “immersive video games” as a possible way to manage social unrest if AI left people without jobs.

It is unclear whether Amodei still holds that position. According to the WSJ, he argued for the idea at length in discussions with researchers and employees at AI companies. This was before he left OpenAI in 2020 and founded Anthropic.

Amodei later became a supporter of universal basic income as a way to soften the effects of mass unemployment. In an essay published earlier in June, he wrote that a hypothetical program could be funded through taxes on companies connected to AI or an increase in capital gains taxes. He also named universal capital accounts as another option. In his view, rapid economic growth should expand the tax base and help share prosperity, even as it displaces people from the labor market.

## What the GTA VI example shows

Mass automation of work is still a long way off, the authors note. But the release of Grand Theft Auto VI, they argue, offers a glimpse of how video games can already affect people’s behavior.

In the United States, where major technology companies, the source claims, control retirement savings and use AI to worsen working conditions, polls showed that as many as 12.5 million people might rearrange their work and personal schedules around the game’s release.

If all of them took to the streets with specific political demands, they could form a mass protest movement. The authors give as an example a demand to repeal Donald Trump’s financial laws that open 401(k) retirement accounts to risks involving companies such as Anthropic.

For comparison, on the largest day of protests against the US invasion of Iraq, roughly 10–15 million people took to the streets in 600 cities around the world. Dissatisfaction with major technology companies played a notable role in recent US midterm elections, but, in the authors’ assessment, it did not produce a movement capable of threatening the existing order, in which corporations play a leading role.

Dataist’s take: The GTA VI example illustrates the difference between mass enthusiasm and political mobilization. The number of people willing to change their plans for a game does not, by itself, mean they will unite around shared demands. Amodei’s idea of video games as a way to soften the effects of automation remains a hypothesis; the source does not confirm that he still supports it.
