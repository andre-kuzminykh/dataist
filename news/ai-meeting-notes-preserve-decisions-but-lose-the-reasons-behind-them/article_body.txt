# AI meeting notes preserve decisions but lose the reasons behind them

## What gets lost in a summary

At HumanX Amsterdam, Zoom CTO Xuedong “XD” Huang invoked Socrates: in conversation, people notice things they might miss in a letter or written account. Huang wants Zoom to carry the meaning of conversations into subsequent work while leaving people in control of the decisions AI helps carry out.

An unprepared question can uncover a connection no one had noticed before. An author who has advised governments and boards on geopolitics recalls that some of his most accurate forecasts emerged during conversations like these with clients or officials. The forecast itself can be added to the notes after the meeting, but the question that led to it usually goes unrecorded.

This resembles a dialectical pattern: a thesis meets an antithesis, and a synthesis responds to both. Hegel called this movement Aufhebung, a term that combines the meanings of cancellation, preservation and elevation. The synthesis retains both the thesis and the antithesis. If only the conclusion remains, readers will not know which objection shaped the decision or what it needs to withstand.

In the preface to *The Phenomenology of Spirit*, Hegel called a bare result “the corpse of the system.” He was writing about philosophy, but the phrase also fits a decision record: it continues to shape an organization even when the arguments that led to it are no longer visible.

## One decision, different reasons

Suppose the board of an energy company approves a pilot at one site. The people carrying it out propose running it at two sites while keeping the original budget. If the board was concerned about the size of the capital investment, the proposal needs to go back for discussion. If the directors wanted to learn from one site before expanding the project, a cheaper option does not address their concerns. Different directors may also have supported the pilot for different reasons.

If AI agents negotiate with suppliers, the board’s intent matters to any system assessing a new proposal. A polished summary that assigns all the directors the same motive can give the people carrying out the decision a false sense of what the board wanted.

Short records are useful: they give a colleague who missed the meeting a starting point and help avoid disclosing details of confidential negotiations. But what gets recorded should depend on what the next person needs to do. For the pilot, one line about what the board wanted to learn and who has authority to revisit the decision may be enough.

In *Phaedrus*, Plato recounts Socrates’ comparison: writing is like a painting. Its figures look alive, but stay silent when questioned; the written word always gives the same answer. An AI assistant answers questions, but its answer may vary. If the directors had different reasons, whose position will it convey?

## From recording to action

In the same conversation, Huang and his colleagues demonstrated ZoomMate. According to Zoom, after a customer meeting, the system drafts a message and updates a sales record. This is one way to use a meeting recording. But if a program is allowed to take actions across different systems, a reviewer needs to be able to return to the relevant part of the conversation.

The QMSum meeting benchmark asks systems to find excerpts that answer a specific question. In the board example, such an excerpt would show that the directors had two different reasons for supporting the project. If the scope of the decision is still unclear, a person with the appropriate authority should determine it. Human approval of an AI action makes sense only if the person can see enough of the discussion to exercise that authority.

Huang’s goal of keeping people in control can be tested in practice: give the notes to someone who did not attend the meeting, then change one fact that mattered to the participants and see whether the reader can tell whether to keep acting or bring the question back for discussion.

In Dataist’s view, for AI systems that not only record meetings but also carry out tasks, an accurate summary is not enough. They need access to the part of the discussion that explains why a decision was made and who can revisit it. The test proposed by the author checks for this, but does not offer a ready-made way to store and pass on that history.
