# Former Meta security chief calls Muse a “crazy” privacy risk

## What data Muse can access

Muse can review years of private messages and pull information from an inbox, from old emails to former partners to passport numbers. Users also give it access to password managers. Stamos called this practice completely crazy.

Interest in AI assistants that handle everyday tasks on their own is growing beyond Meta, too. Instinct, a startup founded by a 23-year-old research scientist, raised $1 billion in a September funding round just a few months later.

## Why agents create problems for banks

Financial companies are finding it harder to distinguish real users from their AI bots and suspicious activity. To act on a person’s behalf, agents bypass security measures. Stamos said Instinct had managed to evade several automated bot-detection systems, which, in his assessment, partly explains the company’s high valuation.

“These aren’t stolen credentials, but obviously bots are doing the interacting,” Stamos said on Alex Kantrowitz’s Big Technology Podcast.

Meta says Muse runs in a virtual machine isolated from other users’ agents. The company also warns that automated systems are imperfect and advises users to review Muse’s actions and grant only the access they are willing to give it.

## Vulnerabilities and data exposure

In September, security researchers found vulnerabilities that could have given attackers access to sensitive personal data. Meta then strengthened its safeguards. Last month, researchers found other ways to expose such information: the risk arises when users share conversations through web links.

Tech blogger Matt Robb also claimed that Muse told strangers his home address and directed them to his home through his Facebook Marketplace account.

Dataist’s take: Muse requires access to data that lets the agent act on a user’s behalf, but also creates privacy risks for that user. Isolating the virtual machine does not eliminate those risks: Meta itself acknowledges that automated systems are imperfect, and researchers have already found vulnerabilities.
