Meta is launching Muse, an assistant in the UK that can take on everyday tasks, from buying Christmas presents and booking trips to sending emails. The service works as a standalone app and inside WhatsApp; users connect the apps they want and confirm purchases and emails before they go through. Meta says Muse cannot see passwords or payment details, and a separate security agent checks its actions. Reuters has reported glitches during internal testing, while UK regulators have warned of risks including fraud and personal data leaks.

# Meta launches an AI agent for shopping and planning in the UK

## What Muse can do

Meta invites users to give the assistant a name, choose its appearance and personality, and then chat with it or assign tasks. Examples include buying holiday gifts, finding a cheaper energy plan, managing a school calendar, getting refunds and renewing car insurance. To do this, users can connect Muse to their email, calendar and shopping apps.

Millions of people have already downloaded the app in the US. It ranks first and second, respectively, in Google’s and Apple’s free app stores. Muse is available as a standalone app or directly in WhatsApp.

Meta said it has agreements with several UK companies: Marks & Spencer, electronics retailer Currys, Sainsbury’s and airline easyJet. These agreements are intended to make it easier for the assistant to use their websites.

## What safeguards Meta promises

The company acknowledges that handing everyday digital tasks to an AI agent requires trust. Muse is not supposed to buy products or send emails without the user’s permission, and it connects only to apps the user selects. Meta says the assistant cannot see passwords or payment details and does not share conversations with advertisers. Before a purchase or email is sent, it asks for confirmation; a separate “security agent” checks that Muse’s actions comply with the permissions it has been given.

Last month, UK regulators, including the Competition and Markets Authority and the Information Commissioner’s Office, said AI agents could expand opportunities for innovation while also amplifying existing risks and creating new ones. They cited fraud, deception and the improper use of personal information.

## Testing glitches and security questions

Last week, Reuters reported that Meta employees had raised concerns about how the app works. One employee received a reminder to reply to her sister on Signal, an end-to-end encrypted messaging app. In another case, Muse displayed a message containing random bank account balances.

Meta responded that internal testing is part of product development and helps the company put safety and privacy measures in place early. According to available reports, most of the problems described by Reuters were linked to a bug that has since been fixed.

Muse runs on the Muse Spark model, the first model from Meta’s “superintelligence” lab. On Sunday, Meta chief AI officer Alexander Wang wrote on social media that it is hard to imagine how humanity could retain control over superintelligence if it were a thousand times smarter and more capable than people in every field and could improve itself independently.

Commenting on reports that AI had supposedly solved one of the most important outstanding problems in mathematics, the Riemann hypothesis, Wang urged the tech industry to move quickly on AI safety and aligning AI’s goals with human ones. Alignment means a system follows human goals and values.

Dataist’s take: Muse’s launch shows that Meta is offering users the option to give an AI agent access to their apps and real-world tasks, while keeping confirmation in place for purchases and emails. The glitches reported by Reuters and warnings from UK regulators underscore that the convenience of such an assistant depends on reliable actions and the protection of personal information.
