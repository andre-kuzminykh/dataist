Google opens Antigravity’s multi-agent tools to researchers

Dataist

Google expands the reach of science and engineering with /teamwork in Antigravity

Google has presented new results from /teamwork, a multi-agent system built on Gemini 4 models. Researchers and engineers used it to tackle problems in mathematics, chemistry, software development, and machine learning, including formalizing mathematical proofs and optimizing code for TPUs. Google is opening /teamwork and the /boost team to external users in Antigravity. It is also accepting applications for six months of free access to its top-tier plan for 10,000 academic researchers.

Google opens Antigravity’s multi-agent tools to researchers and developers

Google describes the system as a collaboration between people and AI: specialists define a task and set theoretical or physical constraints, while AI agents write solvers, explore options, check solutions, and connect long chains of reasoning. The projects involved teams from Google and academic partners. In recent weeks, they have taken on problems that had remained unsolved for years and produced results in information theory, formal verification, molecular structure search, and operating-system kernel optimization.

To check candidate solutions, the system uses independent tools: the Lean 4 proof checker, unmodified software test suites, execution simulators, web search, and hardware counters. Agents also work with limited context: each receives only the files and tools needed for its step. Coordinators hand work to new agents, while critics and auditors check results in separate contexts without relying on the solution author’s reasoning.

How /teamwork works

The system routes a user’s request to a suitable workflow template. These include parallel code development, sequential solving with verification, long proofs, and document analysis. The /boost team separately packages a sequential solve-and-check cycle for everyday programming tasks.

Google says many of these capabilities are already available in Antigravity, with new features for external users to be added in the coming weeks. The new results presented in the publication include tasks completed using versions of /teamwork and Gemini 4 models.

Mathematics and computer science

Researchers from Google, CUHK, USC, and CMU used the Long Proof template in /teamwork to prove the Cortade–Kumar conjecture in full generality. Formulated in 2013, it concerns transmitting a random bit string through a noisy channel: the conjecture states that compressing the input to one bit cannot preserve more information than a dictator function, which copies one of the input bits.

Gemini first found counterexamples to a generalized conjecture from earlier work by the CUHK team. The researchers then proposed a new candidate function, reducing the problem to optimization over 14 variables and obtaining an important analytical lower bound. Gemini 4 Argon and earlier models divided the parameter space and derived most of the new analytical bounds and interval-arithmetic certificates. They also extended the proof from balanced functions to unbalanced ones without human involvement. The team formalized the full proof in Lean 4; it took 50 million lines of code.

In other problems in mathematics, statistics, and computer science, researchers posed open questions to Gemini 4 and the Stellar Colosseum and /teamwork systems. The resulting algorithms and proofs, including those formalized in Lean 4, were checked, simplified, and edited by the authors before publication.

In adaptive data analysis, a multi-agent system on Gemini 4 Argon independently proved nearly optimal sample-complexity bounds for statistical queries chosen based on previous answers. The result settled a question that had been open since 2014 and produced the first nearly memory-optimal algorithm for data streams with insertions and deletions that maintains accuracy against adaptive adversaries.
In quantum computing, a new construction settled an open question from 2014: the first Boolean functions on n bits were found that require strictly more than n/2 quantum queries at constant error. The work also precisely determined the query complexity of symmetric functions.
For Boolean matrix multiplication and triangle detection in graphs, combinatorial algorithms had beaten cubic time by only logarithmic factors for fifty years, through STOC 2024. A new deterministic algorithm resolved STOC 2024’s main open question and achieved the required speedup.
In high-dimensional geometry, a lower bound was obtained for sparse networks: approximating the distances between all pairs of n points within any constant factor may require polynomially more than n edges—even with Steiner points and paths of unrestricted length.
In randomized linear algebra, sets of sparse projections settled an open question from 2025. They compress a matrix to linear dimension without collapsing the directions of an r-dimensional subspace, using only √log r nonzero entries per row. That is quadratically fewer than the log r needed to preserve all lengths, and comes close to a new lower bound.
For weighted graphs with dynamic edge insertions and deletions, Stellar Colosseum settled a question left open after FOCS 2022. The system developed the first single-pass streaming algorithm for cut sparsification that provides nearly optimal memory usage, update time, and recovery time at once.
In online inverse linear optimization, where an expert’s hidden objective must be inferred over time from their decisions, a deterministic algorithm with optimal regret was proposed. Its running time was reduced from exponential in the dimension to polynomial.
For multiplying n-bit integers, multi-agent computational campaigns simplified the theoretical bound for a Turing machine to below n log n. A separate work proposed an O(n log n) algorithm for a single two-dimensional grid; its C implementation matched the GNU Multiple Precision Arithmetic (GMP) library bit for bit on numbers up to one billion bits.
In data structures, a new upper bound was obtained for the amortized overhead per operation in Tarjan’s 1985 deque conjecture. The question is whether self-adjusting trees with links, known as splay trees, can support insertions and deletions at both ends in constant amortized time. The result is better than any finite iterate of the inverse Ackermann function and was verified in Lean 4.
In high-dimensional statistics, researchers proved that approximate leave-one-out cross-validation, which avoids retraining a model from scratch, remains accurate for nonsmooth sparse penalties, including the elastic net.

Formal proof verification

Manually checking a manuscript alongside computer calculations makes reviewers’ work harder, and errors can hide in long derivations. Formalization in Lean 4 turns a proof into code checked by a computer kernel. Once mathematicians confirm that the formal statement matches the original problem, the proof compiles only if every logical step is correct.

The LEAP multi-agent framework, developed with colleagues from Google Cloud AI and running on Gemini 4 Argon, was applied to problems involving the propagation of physical waves, dynamical systems, and integer solutions to polynomial equations.

The /teamwork-LEAP system autonomously produced an end-to-end formalization of the Weil conjectures, results on counting solutions to polynomial equations over finite fields that culminated in Pierre Deligne’s proof in 1974. The formalization took about 1.8 million lines of Lean and required 28,800 runs of subagents. The system also synthesized more than 919,000 lines of Lean to formalize, for the first time, a recent proof of Hilbert’s tenth problem over general rings of integers.

The system also independently reproduced a complete formalization of a recent proof that solutions to the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations can become unbounded in finite time under smooth external forcing. The problem is one of the Millennium Prize Problems and had been open for about 90 years.

Other results include the first formalization of the Birkhoff conjecture on dynamical systems, open since 1927, and the Bochner–Riesz conjecture for three-dimensional space, a central problem in harmonic analysis that had remained open for more than 50 years. While working on the latter, the system found and corrected a gap in a recently published informal proof. It also resolved a question about C¹ regularity for infinity-harmonic functions in three or more dimensions, open for about 20 years; formalized the Killip–Visan theorem on the well-posedness of the Korteweg–de Vries equation in H⁻¹; and checked informal proofs from a UCLA group for the 40-year-old Jamison “Caterpillar” conjecture, the Aghevli–Ollis conjecture, and an improved upper bound for Erdős problem No. 1063.

Before the STOC 2027 conference, authors of 578 papers—more than a third of all submissions—agreed to receive Deep Review feedback on their drafts. In an ongoing author survey (N = 70), 90% of respondents rated the feedback as mostly or almost entirely factual, and 90% said they would use the tool again. More than 30% reported that Deep Review found substantial errors in proofs or mathematically significant typos; 14% said the system found technical gaps that took more than an hour to fix. Authors also noted that tools like this could help produce more accurate research faster and increase confidence in scientific results. Users receive two free Deep Review requests per week. The tool is also available in Antigravity as a document-analysis template for /teamwork.

Finding molecular structures

Determining a molecule’s structure from experimental nuclear magnetic resonance data is one of chemistry’s difficult problems. For HIV protease inhibitors, one misplaced bond can be the difference between a drug and an inactive molecule that resembles it.

Google combined 30 specialized agents in a self-correcting /teamwork cycle and connected them to a physics-based spectrum simulator. On difficult molecules from the benchmark, the system achieved 95% accuracy in selecting the single best candidate and 100% among its top three—almost twice the result of a single agent. The work lays the groundwork for further research into protein dynamics and complex structures in three dimensions. Google also reports that /teamwork and the Argon model helped with nuclear fusion and quantum algorithm problems described in the Gemini 4 publication.

Causal inference benchmark

Google also used multi-stage verification to prepare an empirical benchmark. Starting with five criteria set by people, including data anonymization, 29 agents on Argon turned 13 publicly available tables into a benchmark of 50 causal-inference tasks in 11.5 hours.

For each task, models must determine whether a causal effect can be identified and estimate it, given the correct answer. After initial checks, separate reviewer and auditor agents found simplified workarounds, wording clues, and fabricated scenarios. The problems they found were turned into permanent tests. Automation helped prepare the challenging tasks needed to answer Gemini’s counterfactual “what if” questions.

Software development and systems engineering

In software and hardware projects, /teamwork combines long autonomous campaigns with objective verification: network benchmarks, game engines, unmodified test suites, and hardware timers.

Distributed systems

Using /teamwork and Argon, Google created FarArray, a low-latency, kernel-bypass system built on a distributed global address-space model and the SNAP microkernel. FarArray runs, among other things, lock-free connected-components algorithms. They scale to graphs with one trillion edges, delivering 60–90 times the throughput and 40–180 times the speed of TCP-based key-value stores while using six times less RAM.

The InstantEmbedding system for personalized PageRank reduced peak dynamic-memory use by up to 14 times and running time by 11–27 times compared with accelerated MapReduce implementations. The team also developed custom TPU kernels for graph neural networks using /teamwork and Argon. They cut high-bandwidth memory use by up to 185 times and sped up forward and backward passes by 8–15 times.

Autonomous optimization of a Go engine

To test long-running autonomous optimization, the team used Argon to train a 9×9 Go engine from scratch, starting with a basic AlphaZero implementation. People set the compute-resource distribution and evaluation scheme, after which the system independently accelerated self-play training and Elo rating calculations. Winners were selected only through statistical tests on held-out game records.

Self-play training became 2,300 times faster. In less than eight days, the engine’s rating rose by about 2,600 points to 1,885—between KataGo’s results at 16 and 32 visits per move. For the publication, the final champion played black against the network that had started Elo optimization, playing white. The champion won by 22.5 points under territory scoring with 7.5 komi.

Rust porting and TPU optimization

Large codebase migrations can fail if agents replace dependencies with stubs or change tests to hide errors. A new /teamwork template, coming soon to Antigravity, starts work on a module only after its dependencies build. Each leaf module is checked against the original, unmodified test suite.

After the initial port of 25% of the Zircon microkernel, a two-week campaign on Argon rewrote the remaining C++ code: it added about 800,000 lines of safe Rust that passed all kernel unit tests and booted on arm64 hardware. /teamwork also rewrote Google’s AV1 video decoder, libgav1, from C++ into about 170,000 lines of Rust. The new version matched the original bit for bit across a broad conformance test suite. At first, the port ran about four times slower than the original. After the system was given profiling tools and the task of maximizing speed on short videos, the Rust version became 20–30% faster than the original C++ and hand-optimized assembly on x86_64 and ARM.

For everyday tasks, the /boost team in Antigravity uses /teamwork’s iterative approach to programming. Analysis of past runs showed that tightly coupled code is better written and reviewed sequentially than split into parallel pieces. Based on that analysis, Google improved /boost for programming tasks. This is an example of one workflow template optimizing another in terms of token use.

Full evaluation of custom JAX and Pallas kernels for TPUs takes 7 to 32 minutes, limiting the number of candidates optimization algorithms can test. In the DuoDiscover template on Argon, four parallel agents found bottlenecks and built fast evaluators—“rulers”—that run in 23 seconds. They rank candidates, and their results are regularly checked against full hardware runs using Kendall rank correlation.

DuoDiscover achieved a geometric mean speedup of 4.09× over reference implementations on 17 JAXBench kernels and 2.99× on seven manually optimized Pallas kernels for TPU inference with vLLM on leading open-weight models.

Use across Google’s infrastructure

Google also used /teamwork and /boost for production tasks and hardware modeling. For fleet-wide memory optimization, the team analyzed internal metrics and past successful approaches to reducing memory use, then created reusable workflows. They identified savings opportunities exceeding the initial estimate of 1 PiB. Further savings came from a memory-efficiency monitoring agent operating with human involvement. Techniques used included removing struct padding, narrowing scalar types, using cross-size allocation jumps, code outlining, and tagged pointers.

A /teamwork- and /boost-based scheduling optimization workflow was also launched in Borg. It led to the deployment of two changes: on some hardware types, TPU task preemptions fell by 24% while service-level and cluster-utilization targets were maintained.

Access for researchers and developers

External researchers and developers can already use Gemini 4 models with /teamwork, /boost, and Deep Review in Antigravity. Google has also opened applications for six months of free access to its top-tier plan for 10,000 academic researchers.

Upcoming Antigravity updates will add a formal-proof template and other specialized workflows. In the coming weeks, Google says it will publish detailed tutorials on the Antigravity blog to help developers and researchers build, verify, and steadily improve their own multi-agent teams.
