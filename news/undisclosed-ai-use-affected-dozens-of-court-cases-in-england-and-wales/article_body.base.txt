# AI affected at least 25 court cases in England and Wales

## Fabricated precedents and laws

AI-related problems arose in cases where people tried to speed up legal work, as well as in cases where people represented themselves in court without a lawyer. In one dispute between tax lawyers, the High Court faced what a judge called an unacceptable use of generative AI hallucinations and references to non-existent cases.

In a $21 million dispute — about £16 million — between two Nigerian oil companies, a lawyer based an argument on a ruling supposedly handed down by a senior judge. In fact, AI had fabricated the entire ruling. The judge warned of the dangers of relying on AI answers without checking them.

In another case, senior insolvency judge Mark Mullen found that a junior solicitor had used a chatbot to look up the answer to a legal question and had included in court documents a fabricated quotation from a non-existent statute. After examining the AI chat history, the judge concluded that it had produced nonsense and warned that AI could be completely unreliable.

Earlier this month, the Crown Prosecution Service also faced a problem when one of its lawyers cited a fabricated legal precedent.

## A statement prepared by a police officer

The Court of Appeal considered whether to allow a man from Derbyshire to challenge a four-year sentence for several offences, including causing grievous bodily harm. He pleaded guilty after smashing a patio door with a spade and entering a house. His children’s mother had taken shelter there with a friend and a child. The man threatened them with a large kitchen knife and broke a police officer’s shoulder while fleeing.

An investigator uploaded a transcript of the mother’s interview to Microsoft Copilot and asked it to prepare a victim personal statement — VPS — that would secure the harshest possible punishment and be so moving that a judge or reader would cry. The officer was subsequently removed from frontline duties. Appeal court judges said police officers should not prepare witness statements of any kind in this way.

## Warnings and new rules

The cases came amid warnings from legal professionals that AI could encourage careless preparation of court materials and arguments based on fabricated information. Law Society president Brett Dixon told the Guardian that the lengthy documents generated by AI are particularly obstructive when people represent themselves in court without a lawyer. Instead of three pages prepared by a lawyer using human judgment, judges receive 60 pages of rambling text from commercial AI and spend more time on it, making it harder to reduce court backlogs.

Last week, Supreme Court judge Lord Sales said the courts were increasingly concerned about the use of AI. He also pointed to the risk of losing professional skills: the long-term danger, he said, is not only that a machine gives a wrong answer, but that people gradually lose the ability to recognise such errors.

Brian Flanagan, an associate professor at Maynooth University’s School of Law and School of Criminology, said the justice system was vulnerable to the introduction of AI. In his assessment, the temptation to use the technology would persist, and people would continue creating fabricated legal grounds — a serious problem.

Last month, the Civil Justice Council recommended requiring witnesses in civil cases to sign a declaration that their statements had not been prepared using AI that created or altered their content. This includes facts, reasoning, arguments and opinions. The council’s working group, led by Sir Colin Birss, the second most senior judge in England and Wales, also noted the need for urgent guidance for people who represent themselves in court without a lawyer.

A Ministry of Justice spokesperson said the department was aware of the growing use of AI in courts and was taking steps to ensure it was safe, appropriate and monitored. The measures listed included additional support for courts through new judges and legal staff, modern case-management systems and technology for remote hearings.

Dataist's take: The cases show that AI is already affecting not only how quickly court documents are prepared, but also their reliability. Recommendations on witness declarations and guidance for people without lawyers should set boundaries for this use, but the Ministry of Justice has so far described its measures only in general terms.
