## Following the destination

Meta says bad actors have increasingly used “suggestive” ads, changing tactics to evade detection. An ad may contain nothing illegal itself, yet lead users to a site hosting child sexual abuse material or to other harmful activity online.

The new large language model system checks both the ad and its destination. Meta can block sites and other resources that violate its rules, and take action against accounts connected to them.

The company is also adding AI-based checks to find child sexual exploitation material that earlier systems may have missed. Meta says it plans to add signals as it learns more about how these networks operate.

A separate AI agent will act as a red team for Meta’s own safeguards, looking for weaknesses that offenders could exploit. Meta says the work should help identify new forms of abuse before they become more widespread. It is also improving systems for finding people who return to its platforms with new accounts after earlier ones were removed.

## A broader safety push

The announcement sits within a wider effort to protect younger users. This year, Meta introduced parental controls for Meta AI, accounts for children below the teenage years in WhatsApp, and notifications to parents when children search Instagram for self-harm material.

In September, WhatsApp added parental settings that let parents limit teenagers’ use of channels, choose who can see status updates, and decide who can add children to groups. Parents can also turn on notifications about some group activity.

That product work comes amid lawsuits and criticism from lawmakers over risks Meta’s social platforms may pose to young users. In August, the company agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle a child-safety lawsuit brought by 29 US states. The settlement concerns online child protection.

## What the systems can’t show yet

Meta says that in India, it took action against 5.3 million such items over the same period, with its systems finding more than 98% before users reported them. That figure is striking, but the announcement does not say how many people encountered the material, how much of it came through ads, or whether blocking destinations stopped the underlying networks from moving elsewhere.

I think the most consequential test is not whether Meta can identify more suspicious links, but whether it can connect those links to the accounts and networks behind them—and keep them from returning under new identities. The announcement describes better detection and enforcement tools; it does not yet show how much harm they prevent.
