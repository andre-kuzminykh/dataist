## A chat interface for genetic code

DNA stores information in four letters: A, C, G and T. A human genome contains 6 billion of them. Scientists have read the genome, but still do not understand how much of it works.

Carbon-X aims to make parts of that code easier to explore. Researchers could ask whether a mutation is harmless or associated with disease, helping them choose a target for a drug or gene therapy. Targeting the wrong mutation could make treatment ineffective or cause unintended harm.

Hugging Face built the model by extending an existing language model’s vocabulary so it could read DNA in groups of six letters, then training it on 300 billion tokens. The data combined DNA sequences, English text and examples linking sequences to biological descriptions. It covered mammals, including humans, other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, fungi and protists. Lewis Tunstall, a senior research scientist at Hugging Face, said viruses were excluded to reduce the risk of dangerous use.

Carbon-X sits among several approaches to biological AI, but the interfaces differ:

- Google DeepMind’s AlphaGenome, released last year, predicts the effects of changes to DNA sequences and genetic mutations. It returns numerical results and requires more computing resources.
- Evo 2, built by Arc Institute and Nvidia with academic partners, works with DNA and can generate entire genomes from scratch.
- Carbon-X combines DNA with natural language in a conversational interface — a kind of ChatGPT for DNA.

## The constraint is the point

A full human genome will not fit into Carbon-X. The model handles sequences of roughly 50,000 base pairs, so users can ask about only small regions at a time. That makes it a tool for focused investigation, not a way to ask a model to interpret an entire genome in one go.

The 14-billion-parameter size is also a deliberate choice. Patient data can be difficult to share with advanced AI systems, so Hugging Face designed Carbon-X to run on a user’s device rather than depend on a cloud model. Tunstall said data is not sent to Hugging Face or other companies.

My take: that privacy choice is one of the more consequential parts of the release, but it does not answer the harder question of whether the model’s interpretations are reliable. The developers say they have not tested Carbon-X in laboratory experiments with biological samples, so its predictive accuracy remains unknown. A conversational interface may make genetic questions easier to ask; it does not, by itself, make the answers safe to act on.

Hugging Face is best known as a platform for open-weight and open-source AI tools. In September, Nvidia agreed to buy the company for nearly $13 billion. Carbon-X extends that ecosystem into biology, while its local execution points to a tension the field cannot avoid: the more useful these models become for individual genetic data, the more their value depends on what they can do without sending that data elsewhere.
