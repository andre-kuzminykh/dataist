## Three levels of AI involvement

Partners can assign one of three labels:

- Fully Gen AI: tracks created entirely by AI.
- Partly Gen AI: work that uses AI-generated elements alongside the artist’s own performance.
- No Gen AI: recordings processed with tools such as Auto-Tune.

The labels make a distinction that a simple AI-or-not tag would miss: using pitch correction does not make a recording AI-generated, while an artist’s performance can coexist with AI-made elements.

## Platforms are choosing different rules

YouTube’s approach is part of a broader response to the rapid growth of AI-generated music, but services are not treating it the same way.

- Apple Music plans to add Made With AI labels through its AI Transparency Tags system later this year.
- Spotify already has labels for AI-generated music and a filter for music spam.
- Deezer said in July that it receives nearly 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks a day. It labels detected tracks and excludes them from algorithmic recommendations and editorial playlists. It also withholds royalties for streams associated with fraud and is developing an AI-music filter.
- TIDAL labels recordings it considers fully AI-generated, lets listeners turn them off, and excludes them from royalty calculations.

## Disclosure is YouTube’s bet

YouTube is not making AI use itself a bar to monetization. It is betting that listeners should be told how a track was made, while other services are also limiting where some AI-generated music appears or whether it earns royalties.

YouTube is also experimenting with AI music creation. Its Dream Track tool lets some creators make soundtracks using AI versions of participating artists’ voices.

I think the labels’ usefulness will depend on how reliably partners and YouTube can distinguish the three categories. The announcement explains what each label means, but not how often YouTube’s detection tools will identify music partners have not labeled. That gap matters: disclosure works only if listeners can trust what is disclosed.
