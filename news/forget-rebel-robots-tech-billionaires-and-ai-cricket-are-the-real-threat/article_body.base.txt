The San Francisco Unicorns cricket club uses AI to select players and suggest tactical decisions during matches. Its Cric Lens tool analyzes statistics and pitch data, while people carry out the recommendations. Sportswriter Barney Ronay argues that this kind of assistance calls the fairness of the game into question and says its regulation should be debated. Using cricket as an example, he warns that if algorithms are given more and more decisions in sport, competitions could lose the human unpredictability that draws people to watch.

## AI takes the field: who will make decisions on it?

[FIGURE:0]

Ronay opens his column with an ironic reflection on Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. Discussions describe him as an indispensable company president, even though Nvidia develops technology that could replace human labor. The author jokingly suggests creating an AI copy of Huang and having Huang look for work that technology cannot destroy.

Behind this paradox, Ronay argues, is a broader fear of AI. In his view, the threat comes not from conscious robots but from people: their greed, despotism and drive for power. Sport is one place where this conflict is already visible.

## AI takes the field

Jay Shah was re-elected unopposed as president of the International Cricket Council (ICC). Ronay calls him a talented administrator and notes that Shah is the son of Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s longtime political ally.

In the author’s assessment, the ICC conference produced no clear results. In particular, participants postponed proposals to tighten regulation of new international T20 cricket leagues until their next meeting. Ronay says the issue deserves closer attention, pointing to Major League Cricket in the United States and the San Francisco Unicorns.

The author learned about the team’s use of AI from an interview with player Ravichandran Ashwin in *The Cricketer* magazine. Ashwin said the Unicorns use the technology both to select players and to make decisions during matches.

The club was founded by Silicon Valley entrepreneurs Anand Rajaraman and Venky Harinarayan. They describe the team as a startup. Like Huang, both graduated from Stanford in the mid-1990s. Ronay argues that they are applying the same approach to technology in cricket that is associated with Silicon Valley: introduce it quickly, before rules define what is allowed.

The club’s main tool is Cric Lens. It collects real-time statistics, information about pitch conditions and player data, then draws on information from every T20 match played to suggest tactical decisions. The recommendations arrive during the game, but people are expected to carry them out. This approach is called “human-in-the-loop execution.”

San Francisco Unicorns player Finn Allen has spoken positively about the system. After an eight-wicket win over Washington Freedom, he told reporters that this level of AI involvement suits the team: decisions remain in human hands, with support from an algorithm.

## Where assistance ends

Ronay says the use of Cric Lens raises at least two questions. The first is entertainment: algorithmic recommendations could make the game less interesting. The second is fairness. If tampering with the ball or wearing tape on the fingers is considered contrary to the spirit of cricket, decisions about a captain’s tactics made with the help of a machine also deserve scrutiny. Captaincy is part of the skill, and sport tests people’s abilities in real time.

That is why, the author writes, systems like these need to be regulated, studied and debated. Limits should be set before the extent of the technology’s impact on the game becomes clear. Otherwise, algorithmic assistance could become a kind of “brain doping.”

The Unicorns’ story, Ronay observes, resembles a pitch for an app: the club attracts well-known players, including Pat Cummins, and talks about scaling its model. One of the club’s founders speculated that ideas like these could not be contained within a single sport: starting with cricket, they could be taken to the NFL, football and other disciplines.

The author rejects that prospect. He fears technology companies will gradually enter sport, profit from it and change how it works, pushing its human element into the background.

There is another reason large technology companies might invest in sport. One theory is that they see it as protection against the effects of AI development: technology could disrupt familiar industries, while live sporting events seem like a resilient investment, much like gold during a currency crisis. But, Ronay argues, AI’s ability to process data could also have a major effect on sport.

Formula 1 has already become a competition in which machines determine a great deal, and it is actively adopting new technological capabilities. The author also mentions a possible future with driverless race cars performing for spectators while broadcasts draw audiences increasingly disengaged from what is happening.

AI is also used to find players in elite football, though the results vary. But football still depends heavily on intuition, personal observation and guesswork—the very elements algorithms can replace. Ronay argues that this uncertainty is what makes the game valuable: it creates stories and emotions, and leaves room for beauty, enjoyment and community.

AI could probably run Manchester United more effectively than its succession of club executives, the author writes. But that does not mean viewers would want to watch such a game. In Ronay’s view, sport run entirely by algorithms would lose its cultural value and its ability to surprise.

Ronay compares concerns about AI with the criticism of industrial production voiced by the Luddites. Mass production increases the number of goods, but does not necessarily make them better or bring people greater satisfaction. Likewise, bringing more technology into sport does not guarantee that it will become more appealing. The author asks whether people will have enough shared resolve to limit the use of new systems.

Instead of the promised future in which technology frees people from hard work and lets culture flourish, Ronay describes the present as a world where a few become extraordinarily rich while everyone else spends time on smartphones. He urges people to resist this scenario and points out that tactical decisions about replacing bowlers are among the first things at risk.

Dataist's take: The debate over Cric Lens is about the boundary between analytical support and handing sporting decisions over to algorithms.
