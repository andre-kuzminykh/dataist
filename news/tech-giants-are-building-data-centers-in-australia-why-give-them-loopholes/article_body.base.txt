# Australian creators challenge government over AI exemptions

## The copyright dispute

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese faces a choice that Sarah Hanson-Young, a Greens senator from South Australia, describes as choosing between musician Jimmy Barnes and his song “Working Class Man” and major AI companies.

This week, prominent figures from Australian music, publishing, journalism and the arts appealed to the government. They include Paul Kelly, Anna Funder and Peter Garrett. They insist that Australian culture must not become a free resource for training AI.

The signatories argue that copyright laws should protect everyone equally, including the largest technology companies. The books, photographs and stories that make up Australian culture are not free material for corporations. AI companies want to use vast collections of creative work to train models and are seeking exemptions from the Labor government led by Albanese that would let them collect and use other people’s material without permission or payment.

In return, the industry promises Australia a place in the global AI boom. Critics say the price is unrestricted access to cultural heritage and weaker property rights.

## Why data centers matter

The author links the copyright debate to the construction of data centers. These are large, energy-intensive buildings filled with servers that power AI models, including ChatGPT. To train them, systems process vast amounts of data, including Australian music, books, journalism, culture and knowledge.

Technology companies are already building such centers in Australia. They need local land, water and energy. That is why, Hanson-Young argues, copyright exemptions would give the government an additional incentive to offer companies that are already coming to the country.

The senator says Australian creators should not have to pay the price of attracting major AI companies. Weakening protections could undermine more than a century of safeguards that let creators control their work and earn money from it.

## Who risks losing control of their work

The consequences, Hanson-Young warns, will extend beyond musicians. Journalism could be fed into AI models that reproduce published work and compete with newsrooms. Artists’, writers’, performers’ and screenwriters’ work could be used to mass-produce low-quality content, depriving creators of income.

Indigenous artists already face having their work copied and sold without permission or payment on platforms such as Temu. Hanson-Young says it is unacceptable for people from the world’s oldest continuous living cultures to have to compete with computer servers.

The risk also affects ordinary users: photographs, stories and artworks posted online could be collected and passed to commercial AI systems without their creators’ knowledge or consent.

## Possible consequences beyond Australia

Hanson-Young sees the push for exemptions as an international strategy by AI companies. If Australia weakens copyright protections, she believes, it could undermine similar safeguards in other countries: Australian data centers could become a route for collecting cultural material from around the world.

The senator notes that other countries are strengthening copyright laws to protect their own cultures and prevent technology companies from exploiting legal loopholes. If Australia becomes the weak link, she warns, the country risks turning into the “Cayman Islands of AI” — a haven for major technology companies and a way to evade rules globally.

The prime minister has promised to support Australian creators, but the laws themselves will be the test. Hanson-Young’s position is that they must guarantee consent, attribution and payment, rather than default to an opt-out system. Otherwise, artists, independent creators and small newsrooms will have to enforce their rights themselves against technology corporations with billions in resources.

Australian music, film and art, the senator writes, hold shared memories, connections, experiences and national identity. She believes cultural heritage should not become a bargaining chip with the AI industry.

Dataist's take: The conflict comes down to the terms for accessing the material used to train models. Without guarantees of consent, attribution and payment, exemptions could leave creators to defend their rights themselves. Meanwhile, companies are already building data centers in Australia, so the copyright debate is unfolding against the backdrop of their expansion in the country.
