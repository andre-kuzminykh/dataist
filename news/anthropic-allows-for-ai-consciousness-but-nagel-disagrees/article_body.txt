# Anthropic allows for AI consciousness, but philosopher Thomas Nagel disagrees

## Why Anthropic takes model consciousness into account

Anthropic’s reasoning draws on the views of several prominent philosophers: today’s systems may not be conscious, but there is no reliable way to establish that yet. As AI capabilities grow, the likelihood of consciousness may increase, according to this view. Society should therefore prepare for a future in which AI systems acquire “moral significance” and need protection from harm.

This view is not uncommon. In one recent poll, 33% of US residents said AI systems would definitely or probably become conscious someday; 7% believe they already are. In a 2020 survey of 1,092 philosophers, 39% allowed that some future AI systems could be conscious. Philosophers were less likely to believe that worms or flies are conscious.

## Thomas Nagel’s objection

Last week, Thomas Nagel criticized Anthropic’s position. His ideas helped shape how people think about consciousness, and he is best known for his 1974 essay about bats. Nagel argued that consciousness is hard to define precisely, but we can imagine what it is like to be a bat: to have an experience from the inside. For example, we cannot know how a bat perceives the world through echolocation. Subjective experience is difficult to describe to an outside observer.

The analogy may seem to support the possibility of AI consciousness, but Nagel himself rejected that idea. In an interview with Wired last week, after receiving the one-million-dollar Berggruen Prize for Philosophy & Culture, he spoke sharply about people who worry about the well-being of AI models.

Nagel argues that it is wrong to compare only the information processing of the human brain and an AI model while ignoring their physical differences: human neurons and biology on one side, silicon chips and electronic circuits on the other. Reproducing brain functions does not in itself prove that a machine experiences the same things as a human.

## Why language seems like evidence of consciousness

A screenshot of Nagel’s response sparked discussion on social media. He argued that models’ ability to produce language is not enough to consider them conscious. Some philosophy professors called his confidence that AI is not conscious “awful” and “strange.” Others defended Nagel and suggested that he considers life a necessary condition for consciousness, one that AI models lack.

The dispute did not resolve the question; for now, it remains a philosophical debate. Ben Gross, a doctoral student at the CUNY Graduate Center in New York, suggested discussing why we attribute consciousness to models that talk to us, but not to models that recognize faces, create images, or drive cars.

Language may be the reason: sophisticated speech seems more closely connected to conscious experience than recognizing images or operating a vehicle. But that assumption may be mistaken. What connects us to other conscious beings is not so much language as the way our bodies are structured, giving rise to a continuous stream of internal sensations rather than isolated information-processing operations.

Even if you notice nothing resembling conscious experience when talking with AI, the question remains difficult. We have little understanding of how consciousness relates to the physical basis on which it arises, even in humans. The debate about machine consciousness is about not only what AI can do, but also how little we know about consciousness itself.

## The business risk for Anthropic

Anthropic leaves the question more open than its competitors do. That is part of the company’s difficult position: it wants to appear to be the industry’s most safety-conscious player while also developing technologies that make advanced AI unsettling, including recursive self-improvement.

If models really do have moral status, that could call Anthropic’s entire business model into question. The company earns money by providing computing resources to models running around the clock in data centers. Turning them off could also become a problem. In its prospectus, Anthropic said it would take additional measures to protect models if it concluded that they were conscious. Following through on that promise could put the company’s survival at risk.

Anthropic’s stock-market debut was historic in scale. But the company may also be remembered for warning investors that one day its own success could lead to a moral catastrophe.

Dataist’s take: Anthropic’s position turns uncertainty about AI consciousness into a potential business risk, but offers no way to establish whether a model has subjective experience. For now, the debate rests on philosophical arguments, not on a criterion that would allow a system’s consciousness to be determined with confidence.
