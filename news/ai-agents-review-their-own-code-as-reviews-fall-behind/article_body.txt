According to the LeadDev AI Code Review Report 2026, half of the engineers surveyed say that after adopting AI tools, the number of pull requests per developer rose by at least 25%. Meanwhile, 45% name code review capacity as the main bottleneck in software delivery. Only 26% are confident they understand AI-generated changes as well as those written by people. And 47% of companies assign AI agents to review code those agents created themselves.

## AI agents review their own code, while reviews struggle to keep up with the volume

The report presents findings from a survey of 377 engineers and engineering team leaders in the United States and Europe; 325 participants completed it in full. The study was sponsored by CodeRabbit, a provider of an AI code review tool. Only 14% of respondents said their companies have clear, consistently enforced rules for reviewing AI-generated code.

## Review has become a bottleneck

According to the report, 67% of those surveyed say pull requests have grown larger. Harjot Gill, CodeRabbit’s co-founder and CEO, told VentureBeat that previously, the flow of work was constrained before any code was written: product managers selected and refined tasks because engineering work was expensive. Now, ideas can become code more quickly, and review has become the last checkpoint where changes pile up.

The workload is affecting timelines: 38% of respondents said review cycles slowed after AI was introduced, while 25% said they sped up. In BairesDev’s 2026 developer survey, 67% of participants also said they spend more time reviewing AI-generated code than they did a year earlier.

Developers may work faster, but that does not necessarily speed up the entire software delivery process, said Paul Nashawaty, principal analyst at Efficiently Connected.

<strong>In brief:</strong> Pull requests are becoming more numerous and larger, while review is increasingly delaying software delivery.

## What engineers are reviewing

Human review remains standard practice: 82% of report participants said developers manually review most or all AI-generated code before it is merged into the main branch. However, 58% usually understand the intent behind the changes, but not every detail. Another 11% mainly check whether the code runs and passes tests.

Only 18% of respondents said reviewers regularly have enough context to assess a change’s architectural impact and the potential scale of its consequences. Meanwhile, 68% said reviewers do not always understand which changes need closer scrutiny.

AI tools also take part in review: 74% of those surveyed said a person always or usually checks what such a tool flags. Nashawaty believes AI can help with review, but advises against asking the agent that wrote the code to give final confirmation that it is correct.

## Metrics, rules and accountability

Some companies do not measure the results of code review: 24% of respondents said their organizations track no metrics for this work. AI token usage is tracked by 30%, while defects discovered after release are tracked by 22%.

Gill says engineering teams should track costs, the number of defects that make it into production, the frequency of failed changes and rollbacks, and the workload on reviewers.

The report asked participants who is responsible for the outcome when an AI agent writes the code, another AI agent reviews it, and an automated system merges it. In this scenario, 48% assigned responsibility to the person who requested the change, and 14% to the person who approved it.

Review rules are also applied unevenly: 42% of those surveyed said recommendations for AI-generated code are followed inconsistently in their organizations. Another 42% leave the decision to individual teams. According to Nashawaty, trust in the outcome depends on how the process is managed, the context available to reviewers, how responsibility is assigned, and engineering standards.

## How to reduce risk

The report highlights three practices for developers, engineering leaders and IT directors.

- <strong>Assign reviewers based on the risk of the change.</strong> Gill suggests considering the possible type of failure and the scale of its consequences, regardless of who wrote the code. Changes to payment logic or accounting ledgers, authorization rules, database schemas and migrations, and shared API contracts should go to an engineer responsible for that area.
- <strong>Match the level of review to accountability.</strong> CodeRabbit uses this approach in its own engineering team: most low-risk changes with limited release impact ship without human review. The company tracks defects introduced by each engineer; if someone submits risky changes without requesting review, those changes are temporarily assigned to a human until their metrics improve.
- <strong>Use multiple layers of control.</strong> Nashawaty recommends combining independent AI review, automated tests, security scanning, policy enforcement, and human review, taking the risk and complexity of the change into account.

Dataist's take: handing review to AI agents does not by itself relieve the burden on engineers. Most companies still review code manually, but do not always have the rules and context needed to assess risk. The report also shows that responsibility for the outcome of an automated chain is often assigned to the person who requested the change, rather than the person who approved it.
