# AI proves the Riemann hypothesis, but mathematicians cannot yet make sense of the proof

## Checking the code is not the same as understanding the proof

The Riemann hypothesis describes the distribution of prime numbers. It is widely believed to be true, but mathematicians have been unable to prove it conclusively for 167 years. The problem has become one of the best-known in number theory.

Proofs written in Lean are designed to be checked by a machine. Mathematical papers, by contrast, need to explain results to people so specialists can discuss and build on them. Alpöge has not yet published a version for readers. He estimates that putting the proof into text would take 250,000 pages. On Monday, he wrote on X that the figure was a joke, and that the Lean text was “obviously full of garbage,” even though it compiles.

More than 200 people began checking the proof soon after it was published. On Saturday, the Lean Focused Research Organization and Mathlib said they trusted its correctness and confirmed that the problem was stated correctly and the code compiles. Mathematicians are now trying to work out what new results can be drawn from the proof and developed further.

Abhishek Saha, a professor of mathematics at Queen Mary University of London, believes that understanding the proof will make it possible to build new areas of mathematics and formulate further hypotheses. In his estimate, collaborative work could reduce the write-up to fewer than 10,000 pages, and perhaps fewer than 1,000.

The proof’s structure makes that difficult: it appears to draw on several advanced areas of number theory and to build a long chain of reasoning that seems highly unintuitive. Saha was surprised to find methods from his own field in the proof and is now trying to understand how they are used. He believes a person would be unlikely to come up with a similar approach: AI can construct unfamiliar lines of reasoning that do not fit easily with human intuition.

Saha uses OpenAI’s Astra model to turn parts of the Lean proof into more accessible material, such as one-page conference-style posters and short documents. But this approach does not remove every obstacle: Saha says it is possible that no specialist has enough knowledge to work through every part of the proof in detail.

## Mathematicians are working through the proof with AI

More than 60 people are using AI agents to translate Lean code into understandable mathematical ideas on Principia Math, a collaborative platform for studying breakthroughs achieved with AI. Participants have already processed more than 3,000 fragments. The platform’s CEO, Anton Shakov, expects mathematicians to need months to develop a solid conceptual understanding of the proof Alpöge created with Claude in about six weeks. Shakov believes that once they have worked through its ideas, mathematicians will see a remarkable beauty in them.

Some results have already emerged: independent mathematicians have understood and presented one small result that followed from Alpöge’s proof.

AI labs’ announcements of mathematical breakthroughs have previously drawn criticism for being rushed: companies claim credit for a result while leaving the academic community to work through the proof and look for errors. The Association for Human-Participatory Mathematics, founded in September and comprising more than 1,000 mathematicians, said on Friday that no person can reasonably claim to have authored or even understood a 17-million-line document. The association also criticized Alpöge’s announcement for relying on more than a century of mathematicians’ work without advancing mathematical understanding in the same way.

Alpöge, who worked as a researcher at Harvard University before joining Anthropic, acknowledged how unusual the situation was: the world had been presented with a complete proof before he had himself mastered the vast field of mathematics connected to it. He wrote that he would try to help people work through the proof, including by preparing a full write-up. The announcement had to wait until the completion of Anthropic’s initial public offering on Thursday; Alpöge explained that he could not wait any longer.

## Other AI results in mathematics

In October, OpenAI proved a weaker version of the Riemann hypothesis, the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. In September, the company also beat Alpöge to a solution to another Millennium Prize Problem, concerning the Navier–Stokes equations, which describe the motion of fluids. After OpenAI announced a partial solution to the problem, Alpöge’s co-author, New York University professor Tristan Buckmaster, accused the company of rushing to publish after learning about the researchers’ work. On Friday, Alpöge and Buckmaster announced that they had completed the remaining unsolved parts of the Navier–Stokes problem.

In Dataist’s view, machine verification confirms that the proof works formally, but does not yet make it accessible to the mathematical community. The gap between correct code and understanding the ideas matters: until it is bridged, the new conclusions that can be drawn from the proof will remain limited.
