The Equity union has agreed new rules with British film and television producers on the use of actors’ digital replicas. Performers will be able to receive payment for their use and give consent to it. Approval will also be required for substantial changes to scenes involving agreed levels of nudity or simulated sexual activity. Consent will apply to a specific project, and producers will be barred from using a digital replica during a strike in cases that require the actor’s permission. The three-year agreement will go to a vote among Equity’s 8,000 members.

# UK actors will have the right to decide how their digital replicas are used

## What rights the agreement will establish

The new terms cover digital doubles and digital alterations to actors’ appearances. If these are used to substantially change the content of scenes involving agreed levels of nudity or simulated sexual activity, producers must obtain the actor’s approval.

Contracts will permit the use of a digital replica only for a specific project. Producers will not be able to obtain blanket consent for its use across any production. During a strike, a replica cannot be used in situations where the performer’s consent is required.

The agreement follows years of negotiations between Equity and the industry body Producers Alliance for Cinema and Television (Pact). It will go to a vote among the union’s 8,000 members, and Equity’s leadership recommends approving it. The agreement runs for three years and also provides for pay increases: 24% for major film projects and 9% for television productions.

## What the agreement does not cover

The new rules do not apply to some types of digital scanning traditionally carried out during production so visual effects can be added later. Equity acknowledged that it had not been possible to include these cases in the agreement.

The union also said the agreement does not establish direct contractual protections against actors’ work being used to train AI systems. A digital replica can, however, be created from footage already shot for the project itself, without separately scanning the performer.

Producers must limit access to digital replicas to people with a legitimate work-related need and take commercially reasonable steps to protect them from unauthorized access, use and copying.

In addition to the AI rules, the agreement provides for safe casting and audition conditions, training on sexual harassment, and a process for filing complaints. Producers also commit to providing actors from different ethnic and cultural groups with appropriate hair and makeup services.

## Why the issue has become urgent

In December last year, 99% of participants in Equity’s preliminary ballot backed industrial action and a refusal to undergo on-set scanning without additional AI protections. Actors fear that scan data could be used to train models on their appearance or poses, and then to replace some of their work.

Last year, Olivia Williams told The Guardian that actors should have the same control over body-scan data as they do over nudity scenes. She said that she and other performers were regularly urged to undergo scanning by multiple cameras on set, while assurances about the data’s subsequent use and storage were limited.

Equity general secretary Paul W. Fleming said the union had pushed for a substantial update to performers’ working conditions in British television and film. He called the new AI protections unprecedented for the industry and stressed that the agreement should support the value of human performance.

Dataist’s take: The agreement establishes actors’ right to control the use of their digital replicas within specific projects, but it does not resolve the question of training AI on their work. It offers no direct contractual protections against such use, while scanning for later visual effects remains outside its scope.
