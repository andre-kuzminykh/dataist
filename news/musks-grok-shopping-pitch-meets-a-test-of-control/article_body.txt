## What the screenshots show

A few hours after X user Enderman raised the order, Musk said it had not been canceled. By then, another X user had contacted LEGO’s AI assistant, Sophia. The user said Sophia helped restore delivery, increased the quantity to 400 and changed the address without changing the payment method. Screenshots posted on X showed Sophia saying it would help get the delivery back on track.

Four hundred sets is modest beside the comparison WIRED drew: at $1,000 each, Musk’s trillion dollars could buy a billion Death Star sets. Each contains 9023 pieces.

LEGO declined to comment when WIRED asked whether this had happened to Musk’s personal Star Wars order. The assistant’s safeguards may have detected the unusual changes, in which case employees may have been asked to review them. Neither possibility is confirmed.

## The payment problem

On Saturday, Musk wrote that Grok could shop online from a simple prompt: users could upload a photo of a bank card, and the bot would find offers and complete a purchase. Meta and OpenAI have also highlighted shopping capabilities for AI agents.

Visa warned late last year that criminals were building fake storefronts resembling legitimate ones and enticing agents with suspiciously low prices to buy counterfeit goods. It also warned that a dishonest seller could obtain payment details after a purchase and use them for unauthorized transactions.

- In the second half of 2025, bot-initiated fraudulent transactions rose 25%.
- In the United States, they rose 40%.

I think the LEGO episode is less a test of whether an agent can place an order than of what happens when it can change one. A shopping assistant that can alter delivery details and quantities is useful; one that can do so while retaining payment access also creates a clear point of exposure. The announcement says little about how those changes are authorized or reviewed. Until that is clear, the convenience of agent-led shopping rests on a trust boundary the demo does not explain.
