OpenAI o1 co-creator Noam Brown believes AI will develop very quickly over the next one to two years: language models are already helping create the next generation of models. At the No Limit poker-bot tournament, he explained why game theory does a poor job of predicting human behavior and why models need to learn to reason before answering. For now, people and AI work better together: systems handle some tasks well but struggle with long-term planning and correcting their own mistakes. Brown advised promising students to leave university and join AI labs.

# Noam Brown advises promising students to work at AI labs

Brown developed Libratus and Pluribus, the first poker AIs to beat professional players. He also worked on Meta’s Diplomacy AI CICERO and has been researching reasoning models at OpenAI since 2023. At the No Limit tournament, hosted by Sequoia Capital, Citadel Securities and OpenAI, Brown discussed lessons from more than a decade of research into games.

## Why game theory fails to describe human behavior

Game theory assumes that players act rationally and know that everyone else is rational too. In practice, Brown said, people do not fit that assumption.

He illustrated the point with a game involving a $100 split: one participant proposes how to divide the money, and the other can accept or reject the offer. According to game theory, the first player only needs to offer one cent. But, Brown noted, it is easy to imagine what would happen if someone actually made that offer.

Diplomacy bots also lost when they made decisions based only on game theory. The team first tried to build in ideas of fairness and retribution, but the rules became too complicated. They then trained the models to imitate people as accurately as possible and trained them against these “human imitations.” This approach enabled the AI to play successfully alongside real participants.

## How Brown arrived at reasoning models

Game theory’s failure in Diplomacy was the point when Brown had to reconsider his earlier views. He had specialized in computational game theory, but came to believe that AI needed to be trained on large amounts of data about human behavior and taught to reason before acting.

In 2022, while interviewing at AI labs, Brown encountered objections. Large language models at the time could not play Go, chess or poker. Brown thought they lacked reasoning; the labs responded that larger models with more pretraining would be enough. Brown estimated that teaching a model to play Go reliably through pretraining alone would require increasing the scale of training by about a million times. He did not consider that realistic.

OpenAI was an exception: it already had a team working to develop general reasoning abilities in language models. Brown did not know this when he applied, but he joined the team and helped create a model that eventually became o1.

A key step was reinforcement learning, which encouraged models to think through their answers. A simple prompt asking them to “think step by step before answering” was not enough. Brown said reinforcement learning on reasoning chains made it possible to link longer deliberation with more reliable improvements in results. It also provided a way to scale inference-time compute across different fields, not just games.

Brown does not consider chains of thought the best possible solution: people think not only in words but also in images. Even so, the approach already works well enough.

## Where models still need people

Brown describes the current period as one of collaboration between people and AI. Models’ capabilities are uneven: they outperform people in some areas and are unexpectedly weak in others. For example, they can write good components of poker solvers but struggle with long-term planning, organization and, especially, correcting their own mistakes.

Brown said models do not always notice when they have chosen the wrong direction, and have trouble stopping to reconsider their approach. One model spent several hours fixing insignificant numerical errors in an algorithm that already handled small inaccuracies.

That makes it important for people to understand what a model is good at and where it needs help, then break up the task accordingly. A skilled user divides the work into increasingly small subtasks that the model can solve one at a time. The parts it handles well can be assigned to it; the rest can be broken into simpler steps or done by a person.

Progress is already visible: OpenAI’s Astra model can earn money playing online poker, Brown said. Development is held back by the availability of compute and by skills models have not yet mastered. Brown did not specify which methods might overcome these limits, and noted that some abilities may reach human level much later than others.

## Rapid growth and advice for students

Brown links the acceleration in progress to language models already taking part in creating the next generation of models. He expects very rapid development over the next one to two years.

That forecast informs his advice to promising students: leave university and join AI labs now. Brown said changes are happening so quickly that staying on the sidelines is not the best choice. OpenAI’s labs and those of other companies continue to grow and compete for specialists, a resource that cannot be scaled up in the same way as computing power.

Dataist's take: Brown’s remarks reflect both strong demand for specialists and the limitations models still face. They can already perform certain complex tasks, but still need human oversight and work broken into smaller parts. His advice to students rests on an expectation of rapid progress, although Brown himself acknowledges that the timeline for reaching human-level performance will vary by capability.
