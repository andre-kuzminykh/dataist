## A promise with no one behind it

Hugh had been curious about how well Sophie was trained. He considered asking about her work history, family or vacations, then decided against it. She sounded convincing enough that he treated her as he would a human employee.

The unease came later. On Saturday, Hugh found the shop’s steel gates shut, despite its website saying it was open until 4 p.m. Sophie checked the car again and confidently moved the pickup date to Wednesday. When Hugh reminded her that she had promised Saturday, she asked if she could help with anything else.

Hugh found a way inside and spoke to Mike, the mechanic. Mike said the system was new and Hugh was not the first customer to have this problem. The car would be ready Tuesday, he said, and advised Hugh to call Monday and ask for him rather than Sophie.

That is what Hugh did. Sophie transferred him immediately. Mike was busy, but he could answer for the repair; Hugh collected the car the next day, as Mike had promised.

## The interface is not the service

Sophie handled the handoff correctly when Hugh asked for Mike. But her pleasant voice and fluent conversation had made the original mistake harder to spot: she could state a completion date without being able to ensure it was true.

I think that is the more revealing failure than the awkwardness of an AI voice sounding human. A customer does not need to decide whether Sophie has a personality. He needs to know whether the answer she gives is connected to the work happening in the shop.

Sophie has already taken one job. If she could repair cars, she might eventually replace Mike too. The story’s tension is that the system can make human interaction feel optional before it can reliably do the work behind that interaction. Until then, a warm voice may make a broken promise easier to accept—and a human mechanic remains the person customers need to trust.
