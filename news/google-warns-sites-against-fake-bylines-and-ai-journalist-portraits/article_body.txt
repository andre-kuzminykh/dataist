## A policy update after the crackdown

Last month, Futurism reported that Brown Brothers Media had bought struggling news sites and turned them into AI content operations drawing tens of millions of page views a month. The company invented journalists and experts, gave them fake credentials and biographies, and published thousands of search-optimized articles under their names.

Some stories copied real reporting; others claimed personal experiences that never happened or were so careless they offered readers little value. According to data reviewed by Futurism, a substantial share of the network’s audience came from Google.

Google did not comment in detail after the investigation, but began limiting Brown Brothers sites on its platforms, including Search and News. The company soon stopped publishing.

The revised guidance retains Google’s recommendation to identify who created content, then adds a warning against deceptive authorship. It names AI-generated photos, invented names and false qualifications as examples of a fake profile designed to make work appear to come from human experts. Google says such deception makes a page untrustworthy to both users and its automated quality systems, and indicates low page quality.

## The byline is part of the product

The update follows other reporting on content operations accused of using fabricated bylines to attract readers. In Canada, one publication’s nonexistent journalists began producing error messages. Another was caught plagiarizing competitors’ work under the name of a fictional Indigenous journalist. Similar schemes surfaced in Florida and Rhode Island.

I think Google’s rule is a useful line to draw, but a narrow one. It targets a visible form of deception; it does not answer whether search systems can reliably distinguish original reporting from a polished imitation, or whether a real byline guarantees useful journalism.

That gap matters because Google is also rolling out AI and replacing links to original reporting with AI-generated summaries that can contain errors. Futurism argues those summaries are rapidly undermining independent journalism. The company is asking publishers to make authorship legible while its own products make the route from search results to original reporting less direct.

The policy can make fake identities a clearer liability. It cannot, by itself, resolve that tension.
